title: South Loop & Prairie Avenue
area: Downtown
blurb: The Auditorium, the city's library and the mayor it's named for, Printers Row and the old Dearborn Station, millionaires' row on Prairie Avenue, and Chess Records on Motor Row.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour starts at the southern edge of the Loop and heads south into a part of the city that has been reinvented more times than almost any other.

In the 1870s and 1880s, Prairie Avenue, about a mile and a half south of the Loop, was the most exclusive address in Chicago. After the Great Fire, the families who ran the city's biggest businesses built mansions here: Marshall Field, the merchant; George Pullman, the sleeping car king; Philip Armour, the meatpacker; and John Glessner, the farm machinery maker. It was close enough to the Loop for a carriage ride to the office, and far enough to feel like a world apart.

It didn't last. The railroads that had made these men rich surrounded the neighborhood with tracks and yards, and smoke and noise drifted in. Just to the west, a red-light district called the Levee grew up, with saloons, gambling halls, and brothels, protected by corrupt politicians. By 1900, the wealthy were moving north to the Gold Coast, and most of the mansions of Prairie Avenue were eventually torn down for factories and warehouses.

Meanwhile, the blocks just south of the Loop filled with railroad stations, printing plants, and showrooms. Six major passenger stations ringed the downtown, and the streets around Dearborn Station became the center of the printing industry, which produced the timetables, catalogs, maps, and magazines that the railroads carried across the country. Along Michigan Avenue, a row of showrooms for the new automobile companies grew up, and in the 1950s, one of those buildings became the most important studio in the history of the blues.

By the 1970s, much of the South Loop was empty. Since then, it has been rebuilt as a neighborhood of lofts, townhouses, and towers, and a handful of the old mansions have been saved as museums.

We'll start at the Auditorium Building on Michigan Avenue, walk west to the library and south through Printers Row, and then east to Prairie Avenue, ending at Chess Records.
=== STOP The Auditorium Building @ 41.8761,-87.6250
You are on Michigan Avenue at Ida B. Wells Drive, which until 2018 was called Congress Parkway, in front of the massive granite and limestone building that fills the block to the west. This is the Auditorium Building, completed in 1889 by Dankmar Adler and Louis Sullivan, and it was the project that made them famous.

It was a remarkable combination: a theater with about four thousand seats, a luxury hotel, and an office building, all wrapped in one heavy, rusticated exterior. The idea came from a businessman named Ferdinand Peck, who wanted a great opera house for Chicago that ordinary people could afford, paid for by the hotel and the offices around it. When it was finished, its tower was the tallest structure in the city, and the building was one of the largest in the country. President Benjamin Harrison came to its dedication.

Adler was the engineer and the acoustical genius of the firm. The theater's acoustics are still considered nearly perfect, and great musicians and singers performed here. Sullivan designed the interior, with gold-leaf arches lined with hundreds of the newfangled electric light bulbs. A young draftsman named Frank Lloyd Wright worked on the drawings.

The building is so heavy that it has sunk more than two feet into Chicago's soft clay over the years. Look at the floor of the lobby, and you may notice it slopes.

The hotel and theater struggled in the Depression, and during the Second World War, the theater was used as a servicemen's center, with a bowling alley on the stage. In 1947, the building was bought by Roosevelt University, a new college founded to be open to students of all races and backgrounds at a time when many universities were not. The theater was restored and reopened in 1967. It's worth seeing inside if you can.

Next door, at 410 South Michigan, is the Fine Arts Building, built in the 1880s as a showroom for the Studebaker carriage company, and later converted into studios for artists, musicians, and writers. It still is, and its old elevators are still run by operators.

The building was so famous that it was used even before it was finished. In June 1888, the Republican Party held its national convention in the half-built theater, and nominated Benjamin Harrison, who came back the next year, as president, to dedicate the building.

Walk around to the south side of the building, along Ida B. Wells Drive. In the 1950s, when the street was widened to carry traffic from the new Eisenhower Expressway into the Loop, there was no room left for a sidewalk. So the city cut the sidewalk right through the building, carving an arcade out of the old hotel's ground floor. You walk under the Auditorium, beneath the rooms where guests once slept.
=== STOP Harold Washington Library Center @ 41.8763,-87.6282
Walk west on Ida B. Wells Drive to State Street. The enormous red brick and granite building on the corner, with huge green metal ornaments on its roof, is the Harold Washington Library Center, Chicago's main public library, which opened in 1991.

Look up at the roof corners. Those giant green figures are owls, the symbol of wisdom, perched among swirling leaves and seed pods. The design, by the architects Hammond, Beeby, and Babka, was chosen in a public competition. It deliberately echoes the heavy stone buildings of the nineteenth century around it, like the Auditorium and the Rookery, and it was controversial at the time for looking backward rather than forward. Inside, on the top floor, there's a glass-roofed winter garden.

The library is named for Harold Washington, who in 1983 became Chicago's first Black mayor. Chicago's politics had long been dominated by a powerful Democratic political machine, which had been led for over two decades by Richard J. Daley. Washington, a congressman from the South Side, ran as a reformer, and he won with a coalition of Black, Latino, and white liberal voters, with an extraordinary turnout in Black neighborhoods.

His first years in office were consumed by what were called the Council Wars, as a majority of the city council, mostly white aldermen, blocked his agenda. Washington won a second term in 1987, but he died of a heart attack at his desk in City Hall, months later. He remains a beloved figure, and the library holds an exhibit about his life.

Washington's 1983 campaign was one of the most dramatic in the city's history. In the Democratic primary, he beat both the sitting mayor, Jane Byrne, and Richard M. Daley, the son of the late mayor, who split the white vote between them. In Chicago, winning the Democratic primary had always meant winning the election. But this time, many white Democrats deserted their party for the Republican candidate, Bernard Epton, in a campaign full of open racial appeals. Washington won by a narrow margin.

His slogan was "Chicago works together," and he set out to dismantle the patronage system that had kept the old machine in power, opening city jobs and contracts to people who had long been shut out of them. When he died, in November 1987, thousands of people lined up for hours, in the cold, to file past his casket at City Hall. Many Chicagoans still speak of him simply as Harold.

When the library opened, it was said to be the largest public library building in the world. Take the escalators to the top floor to see the winter garden, under its glass roof.
=== STOP Printers Row & Dearborn Station @ 41.8724,-87.6290
Walk south on State Street and then west to Dearborn Street, and walk south along Dearborn to Polk Street. At the end of the street stands Dearborn Station, a brick building with a clock tower, built in 1885. It's the oldest surviving passenger train station in Chicago.

For nearly a century, this was the Chicago terminal for the Santa Fe Railway, whose famous trains, like the Super Chief, carried movie stars, businessmen, and tourists between Chicago and Los Angeles. Because Chicago was the meeting point of the eastern and western railroads, long-distance passengers almost always had to change stations here, which is part of the reason Chicago became such a hotel town. The station closed in 1971, and the train shed behind it was torn down. The head house now holds offices and shops.

The blocks along Dearborn and Plymouth Court, leading up to the station, are called Printers Row. From the 1880s to the mid-twentieth century, this was one of the great printing centers of America. Look at the tall, heavy brick loft buildings with big windows, built to let in light for typesetters and press operators, and with floors strong enough to hold heavy printing presses. The railroads needed tickets, timetables, maps, and brochures, and the mail-order companies needed catalogs, and Chicago printed them.

The biggest of the printers was R. R. Donnelley and Sons, whose Lakeside Press building is just to the west, on Plymouth Court. For generations, Donnelley printed telephone directories, catalogs, books, and magazines, including Time and Life.

By the 1970s, the printers had moved out. Printers Row became one of the first places in Chicago where old industrial buildings were converted into loft apartments, and it's now a lively residential neighborhood. Every June, the Printers Row Lit Fest fills the street with booksellers.

As you walk along Dearborn Street, look up at the buildings. At number 431, the Manhattan Building, finished in 1891 and designed by William Le Baron Jenney, was one of the first buildings anywhere to reach sixteen stories, held up by an iron and steel frame. At number 720, the Franklin Building, built in 1912 for a printing company, has a colorful tile mural over its entrance, showing printers at work, pulling the first impression from a press.

R. R. Donnelley's old Lakeside Press building, on Plymouth Court, was designed by Howard Van Doren Shaw. The company later built an even bigger plant about a mile south, on Cermak Road, a Gothic fortress decorated with the marks of famous early printers, which is now one of the largest data centers in the world. Since 1903, the company has given its friends a small book every Christmas, a series of firsthand accounts of American history called the Lakeside Classics. It's one of the longest-running series in American publishing.
=== STOP Clarke House & Glessner House @ 41.8576,-87.6216
From Printers Row, walk about a mile south and east, or take a bus, to 18th Street and Prairie Avenue. You are now in the Prairie Avenue Historic District, all that remains of Chicago's first millionaires' row.

On the west side of the block, along Indiana Avenue, look for a white wooden house with a columned porch. That's the Clarke House, built around 1836, which makes it the oldest house in Chicago. It was built for Henry Clarke, a merchant, when this was open prairie, far from the small town on the river. The house has been moved twice. In 1872, it was moved to the South Side, and in 1977, the city brought it back near its original site, lifting it over the elevated railroad tracks on the way.

Now walk to the corner of 18th Street and Prairie Avenue. The rough granite house on the southwest corner, which looks like a fortress, is the Glessner House, completed in 1887. It was designed by Henry Hobson Richardson, the great Boston architect, for John Glessner, a partner in a company that made farm machinery, which later became part of International Harvester.

Richardson turned the house inward. Its street walls are heavy and nearly windowless, giving privacy from the noise and dirt of the city, while the rooms open onto a sunny private courtyard on the south side. Prairie Avenue's other residents were shocked. George Pullman, who lived across the street, is said to have complained that he didn't know what he'd done to have that thing staring him in the face every time he went out his door. Richardson died before it was finished, at forty-seven.

In 1966, the house was going to be demolished, and a group of architects bought it to save it. The organization they formed became the Chicago Architecture Center, which runs the river boat tours. Glessner House is now a museum, with its original furniture and decorations.

The Clarke House has its own sad story. Henry Clarke died in the cholera epidemic of 1849, and his widow, Caroline, kept the family afloat by selling off pieces of their land, as the city grew out to meet the house.

The Glessners raised two children here, and their daughter, Frances, became one of the most remarkable people ever to grow up on Prairie Avenue. As Frances Glessner Lee, she became fascinated with how deaths were investigated, at a time when police and coroners often missed the evidence in front of them. In the 1940s, she built a series of tiny, astonishingly detailed dollhouse rooms, each showing a real death scene, with every clue in place, down to the calendars on the walls and the food in the kitchens. She called them the Nutshell Studies of Unexplained Death, and they were used to train detectives. They're still used for training today, and she's often called the mother of forensic science.
=== STOP Pullman's Mansion & the Battle of Fort Dearborn @ 41.8578,-87.6193
Walk east on 18th Street toward Calumet Avenue. Somewhere along here, on the east side of Prairie Avenue at number 1729, stood the mansion of George Pullman, the man who built the Pullman Palace Car Company and made the railroad sleeping car a symbol of luxury. Marshall Field lived down the block, at 1905. Both houses are long gone.

Pullman had a special interest in this spot. The tradition in Chicago was that this was near where the Battle of Fort Dearborn took place, on August fifteenth, 1812. When the garrison and settlers evacuated the fort, heading south along the beach toward Fort Wayne, they were attacked by a large force of Potawatomi warriors, allied with the British in the War of 1812. Most of the soldiers, and many women and children, were killed, and others were taken captive. For more than a century, Chicagoans called it the Fort Dearborn Massacre.

In 1893, Pullman commissioned a bronze sculpture of the event and placed it near his house. It showed a Potawatomi chief, Black Partridge, protecting a settler's wife from a tomahawk. For many years, it stood in the lobby of the Chicago Historical Society. Today, it's in storage, after Native American groups objected to its portrayal of the battle.

The small park on the corner of 18th and Calumet is called Battle of Fort Dearborn Park, dedicated in 2009. Historians now describe the event from both sides: the Potawatomi were fighting to defend their land from the spreading American settlement that would soon push them out of the Chicago area entirely, through a treaty signed in 1833.

For many years, an old cottonwood tree stood near this corner, and Chicagoans said it was the very tree where the battle had been fought. The so-called Massacre Tree died, and it was cut down in 1894. Whether the battle really happened here, or somewhat farther north, near what's now Roosevelt Road, is still debated by historians. Like many Chicago landmarks, this one rests partly on legend.
=== STOP Second Presbyterian Church @ 41.8558,-87.6244
Walk west on Cullerton Street to Michigan Avenue and look for the rough stone church on the west side of the avenue, near 20th Street. This is Second Presbyterian Church, the church of many of the Prairie Avenue families.

The church was built in 1874, designed by James Renwick, the New York architect of Saint Patrick's Cathedral. In 1900, a fire gutted the interior, and the congregation rebuilt it in the new Arts and Crafts style. If it's open, step inside. The result is one of the most beautiful church interiors in America: dark wood, painted murals of angels and saints by the artist Frederic Clay Bartlett, and a remarkable collection of stained glass windows, including several by the Tiffany studios.

The Arts and Crafts movement started in England in the nineteenth century, as a protest against mass production. It emphasized handmade craftsmanship, natural materials, and simple, honest design. Look at how the decoration in this church is inspired by plants and nature. It's the same spirit you saw in the Prairie School houses of Frank Lloyd Wright.

As the wealthy families moved north, the church lost most of its members. It survived through the years when the neighborhood around it was abandoned and industrial. Today, with new residents moving in, it's growing again.

Look along this stretch of Michigan Avenue. Many of the old buildings have huge plate glass windows on the ground floor. That's because this was Motor Row. In the early twentieth century, dozens of automobile companies, including Ford, Buick, and Cadillac, had their showrooms and dealerships along here, on what was then the main road south out of the city.
=== STOP Chess Records @ 41.8536,-87.6242
Walk south on Michigan Avenue to number 2120, a narrow building with a blue awning. This was the headquarters and studio of Chess Records from 1957 to 1967, and it's one of the holiest sites in American music.

Chess was founded by two brothers, Leonard and Phil Chess, Jewish immigrants from Poland who arrived in Chicago as children. In the 1940s, they ran nightclubs on the South Side, and they saw the music Black migrants from the Mississippi Delta were bringing to Chicago's clubs. They started a record label and began recording it.

The Chicago blues was electric: the Delta blues of the South, played on amplified guitars, with drums and harmonica, loud enough to cut through a crowded South Side bar. Muddy Waters, who had come up from Mississippi in 1943, was its king, and Chess recorded him. The label's roster became legendary: Muddy Waters, Howlin' Wolf, Little Walter, Bo Diddley, Etta James, and Chuck Berry, whose records for Chess helped invent rock and roll. The great songwriter and bass player Willie Dixon wrote many of their songs and ran the sessions.

The music recorded at Chess went across the Atlantic and inspired a generation of British musicians. In 1964, the Rolling Stones, who had named themselves after a Muddy Waters song, came here to record, and they called one of the songs they recorded 2120 South Michigan Avenue.

The building is now the home of Willie Dixon's Blues Heaven Foundation, founded by Dixon, who fought for years to recover royalties for blues musicians. You can tour the old studio.

Chess had already helped launch rock and roll before it moved here. In 1951, the label released a record called Rocket 88, recorded in Memphis by Sam Phillips and credited to Jackie Brenston and his Delta Cats, a band led by Ike Turner. Many people call it the first rock and roll record. A few years later, Etta James recorded At Last for a Chess label, and it became one of the most beloved songs of the century.

This stretch of the city has a much older and rougher history too. Just a few blocks west, between about 18th and 22nd Streets, lay the Levee, Chicago's most notorious vice district, where hundreds of brothels and saloons operated openly in the early 1900s under the protection of the local aldermen. The most famous was the Everleigh Club, run by two sisters, Ada and Minna Everleigh, which had gold-leafed pianos and rooms decorated in different themes, and was patronized by wealthy men from around the country. When Marshall Field's son was shot in 1905 and died a few days later, the official story was that he had shot himself by accident at his home on Prairie Avenue. But a rumor spread that the shooting had happened at the Everleigh Club. There was never any proof, and it remains a legend. The mayor shut the Everleigh Club down in 1911, and the Levee was closed soon after.

This is the end of the South Loop tour. Nearby, at Cermak Road, is the Cermak-McCormick Place Green Line station, which will take you back downtown.
