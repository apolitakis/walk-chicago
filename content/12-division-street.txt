title: Division Street & Humboldt Park
area: West & Northwest Sides
blurb: Polish Broadway and the old Russian bathhouse, Louis Sullivan's Orthodox cathedral, the thirteen domes of Ukrainian Village, Paseo Boricua and its steel flags, and Jens Jensen's prairie river in Humboldt Park.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour follows Division Street west from Wicker Park, through Ukrainian Village, to Humboldt Park, one of the great parks of the West Side. In about two miles, you'll walk through the history of half a dozen immigrant communities, each of which made this street its own.

In the late nineteenth century, the blocks where Division Street meets Milwaukee Avenue became the center of Polish Chicago, and Division Street was nicknamed Polish Broadway, lined with Polish shops, banks, bakeries, and taverns. Germans and Scandinavians settled farther west, around Humboldt Park, and in the early twentieth century, Jewish families moving out of the crowded Maxwell Street district followed them, building synagogues and shops along Division Street. Around 1900, immigrants from the eastern edge of the Austro-Hungarian and Russian empires, Ukrainians and their neighbors, began building churches south of Division Street, in the neighborhood now called Ukrainian Village.

After the Second World War, a new community arrived. Puerto Ricans, who had been American citizens since 1917, came to Chicago by the thousands, looking for work. Many settled first near the lake, in Lincoln Park and Lakeview, but as those neighborhoods were cleared for urban renewal in the 1960s, families moved west. By the 1970s, Humboldt Park and this stretch of Division Street had become the heart of Puerto Rican Chicago, as they still are.

In 1967, the broadcaster Studs Terkel published his first book of oral history, a collection of interviews with ordinary Chicagoans about their lives and their city. He called it Division Street: America. The title was a metaphor. Most of the people in the book didn't live on Division Street at all, but the name seemed to capture a city divided by race, by class, and by neighborhood.

We'll start at Division Street and Damen Avenue, detour south into Ukrainian Village to see two cathedrals, and then walk west along Paseo Boricua into Humboldt Park, ending at the boathouse on the lagoon.
=== STOP Division Street & the Russian Baths @ 41.9033,-87.6763
You're at the corner of Division Street and Damen Avenue. Wicker Park is just to the north, and the Polish Triangle, where Division Street meets Milwaukee Avenue, is a few blocks to the east. In the early twentieth century, this stretch of Division Street was the main street of a crowded, working-class neighborhood of Poles, Jews, and Ukrainians, full of bakeries, kosher butchers, saloons, and storefront theaters.

A few doors east of Damen, at number 1916, look for the building that housed the Division Street Russian and Turkish Baths, which opened in 1906. For generations, men came here to sit in the steam, plunge into ice-cold pools, and get the platza, a scrubbing with brooms of oak leaves soaked in hot, soapy water. Workingmen, gangsters, politicians, and businessmen all sweated here together. The novelist Saul Bellow, who grew up a mile west of here, wrote about the baths in his novel Humboldt's Gift.

On June 12th, 1966, this corner was the scene of a turning point in the history of Puerto Rican Chicago. That day, the city had celebrated its first Puerto Rican parade, downtown. That evening, near Division and Damen, a police officer shot and wounded a young Puerto Rican man named Arcelis Cruz. Crowds gathered, angered by years of harassment by the police, and for three nights there was rioting along Division Street, with stores burned and many people injured and arrested. It was one of the first major riots by Puerto Ricans in an American city. Afterward, community leaders organized new groups to fight for jobs, housing, schools, and better treatment from the police, and Division Street became the center of Puerto Rican political life in Chicago.
=== STOP Holy Trinity Cathedral @ 41.9020,-87.6818
Walk west on Division Street to Leavitt Street, and south a block to Haddon Avenue. The small, cream-colored stucco church with the octagonal dome and the square bell tower is Holy Trinity Orthodox Cathedral, and it was designed by Louis Sullivan, the great architect of the early Chicago skyscraper.

Holy Trinity was founded in 1892 by immigrants from the Carpathian Mountains, in what was then the eastern edge of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. They were Rusyns, a people closely related to the Ukrainians, and many of them joined the Russian Orthodox Church after arriving in America. They were poor, and they wanted a church that reminded them of home. Sullivan, whose career was then at its height, gave them a church inspired by the small wooden churches of the Russian countryside, with an eight-sided dome over a square base and a bell tower at the front, but simplified and transformed into his own style. It was completed in 1903. Look at the ornament around the door and the windows of the tower, and you'll see Sullivan's delicate swirls and leaves, the same kind of ornament you'll find on his skyscrapers and on the Sullivan Center on State Street.

The little church had powerful friends. Tsar Nicholas the Second of Russia donated four thousand dollars toward its construction. Its priest during the building of the church was John Kochurov, who later returned to Russia and, in 1917, became one of the first priests killed in the Russian Revolution. The bishop who consecrated the church, Tikhon, later became Patriarch of Moscow, and he was persecuted and imprisoned by the Soviet government. Both men are now saints of the Orthodox Church, and the people of this parish speak of them as their own.

If the church is open, step inside. It's a single, warm room under the dome, glowing with icons and painted decoration, and it's one of the few places where you can see Sullivan's genius applied to a building so small and so intimate.
=== STOP Saint Nicholas Cathedral & Ukrainian Village @ 41.8970,-87.6839
Walk south on Leavitt Street about a third of a mile, to Rice Street, and turn right toward Oakley Boulevard. You'll see thirteen copper domes rising above the rooftops. This is Saint Nicholas Ukrainian Catholic Cathedral, completed in 1915, and modeled on the great Cathedral of Saint Sophia in Kyiv. The thirteen domes stand for Christ and the twelve apostles.

Ukrainian Catholics follow the Eastern, Byzantine tradition of worship, like the Orthodox, with icons and chanting, but they are in communion with the Pope in Rome. The first Ukrainian immigrants came to Chicago in the 1890s, mostly from the region of Galicia, then part of Austria-Hungary, to work in the city's factories, rail yards, and stockyards. Many more came after the Second World War, as refugees who couldn't or wouldn't go home to a Ukraine ruled by the Soviet Union, and many of them settled here, making this the center of Ukrainian life in the Midwest. If the cathedral is open, step inside to see the walls and domes covered with mosaics and paintings in the Byzantine style.

A block south, at Oakley Boulevard and Superior Street, is a newer church, Saints Volodymyr and Olha, with a huge mosaic over its entrance showing the baptism of the people of Kyiv into Christianity, in the year 988. It was built in the 1970s by parishioners who left Saint Nicholas after a bitter dispute, partly over whether to switch from the old Julian calendar to the modern Gregorian calendar, which would have changed the date of Christmas. Just down the block, the Ukrainian National Museum holds folk costumes, embroidery, and pysanky, the intricately decorated Easter eggs for which Ukrainians are famous.

Look around the neighborhood. Many of the houses are workers' cottages and two-flats from the 1880s and 1890s, and the streets are dotted with Ukrainian churches, credit unions, and social clubs. Since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, these churches and halls have been centers of fundraising, prayer, and protest, and you'll often see the blue and yellow Ukrainian flag on the porches.
=== STOP Paseo Boricua & the Steel Flags @ 41.9030,-87.6866
Walk north on Oakley Boulevard back to Division Street, and then west to Western Avenue. Look west down Division Street, and you'll see an enormous steel Puerto Rican flag arching over the road, its stripes and star rippling as if blowing in the wind.

This flag and its twin, about half a mile to the west, were installed in 1995. Each is nearly sixty feet tall and weighs about forty-five tons. They mark the two ends of Paseo Boricua, the stretch of Division Street that is the commercial and cultural heart of Puerto Rican Chicago. The word Boricua comes from Borikén, the name the Taíno people gave to the island of Puerto Rico before the Spanish arrived.

Puerto Ricans first came to Chicago in large numbers in the late 1940s. Some were recruited by an employment agency to work in steel mills and as domestic workers, and many more followed, settling near the Loop, in Woodlawn, in Lincoln Park, and in Lakeview. In the 1960s, urban renewal cleared many of those blocks. In Lincoln Park, a street gang called the Young Lords, led by a young man named José Cha Cha Jiménez, transformed itself into a political organization, occupying buildings and demanding housing for the poor, and it inspired Young Lords chapters in New York and other cities. But the displacement went on, and families moved west, to West Town and Humboldt Park.

By the 1990s, the community faced a new threat: the gentrification spreading west from Wicker Park, as rising rents began to push families out once again. Neighborhood leaders and the city created Paseo Boricua, with the flags as its gateways, as a way to plant the community's identity firmly in place. As you walk west, look for the bakeries, restaurants, and social clubs along the street, and for the murals of Puerto Rican heroes on the walls.

On the corner of Western Avenue, just north of Division Street, is Roberto Clemente Community Academy, a high school named for the great Puerto Rican baseball player, who died in 1972 in a plane crash while flying relief supplies to earthquake victims in Nicaragua.
=== STOP La Casita de Don Pedro @ 41.9026,-87.6929
Walk west along Division Street. At number 2625, on the south side of the street, look for a small wooden house painted in bright colors. This is La Casita de Don Pedro, a replica of a traditional Puerto Rican country house, the kind that the jíbaros, the farmers of the island's mountains, lived in. It's used as a community and cultural center, for music and gatherings. It's named for Pedro Albizu Campos, who led the movement for Puerto Rican independence in the 1930s and 1940s and spent many years in American prisons.

The question of Puerto Rico's status, whether it should remain a territory of the United States, become a state, or become independent, has divided Puerto Ricans for more than a century, and you'll see all of those views expressed along this street.

Paseo Boricua is also the place to try Puerto Rican food: pastelillos, which are fried turnovers; alcapurrias, fritters of green banana and root vegetables stuffed with meat; mofongo, mashed fried plantains with garlic; and the jibarito, a sandwich of thin steak, cheese, lettuce, and tomato served not on bread, but between two slices of flattened, fried green plantain. The jibarito isn't from Puerto Rico. It was invented right here in Chicago, in 1996, at a restaurant called Borinquen, less than a mile north of here, and it has since spread around the country.

Every June, the neighborhood holds its Puerto Rican festival and parade, which fill Division Street and Humboldt Park with music, food, and flags. You'll hear salsa, and also the older Puerto Rican music of bomba and plena, with their hand drums and call-and-response singing, which have their roots in the music of enslaved Africans on the island.
=== STOP The Humboldt Park Stables @ 41.9023,-87.7035
Continue west on Division Street, past the second steel flag and across California Avenue, into Humboldt Park. On the south side of Division Street, look for a handsome old building of brick and stone, with steep roofs and a turreted tower, which looks as if it belongs in a German village.

This is the Humboldt Park Receptory and Stables, built in 1896 for the West Park Commission, the agency that ran the West Side parks. It was designed by two German-born architects, Frommann and Jebsen. It held the park's offices, a waiting room for visitors, and stables for the horses and wagons that maintained the parks. Today, it's home to the National Museum of Puerto Rican Arts and Culture.

The park itself was laid out starting in 1869, and named for Alexander von Humboldt, the German explorer and naturalist, who had died ten years earlier. Its first designer was William Le Baron Jenney, the architect of the Home Insurance Building, often called the world's first skyscraper. The neighborhood around the park filled with Germans, Scandinavians, Poles, and Jews, and they decorated the park with statues of their heroes. Look for the bronze of Alexander von Humboldt himself, and a statue of the Viking explorer Leif Erikson, given by the Norwegian community. Until the 1970s, there was also a monument to Thaddeus Kosciuszko, the Polish general who fought in the American Revolution, given by the Polish community.

Saul Bellow grew up near this park in the 1920s, the son of Jewish immigrants who had come from Russia by way of Montreal. He went to Tuley High School, near Division and Western, which later became Roberto Clemente Community Academy, and he set the opening of his novel The Adventures of Augie March in these streets. The book begins, "I am an American, Chicago born." Bellow himself was born in Canada, but he became the great novelist of Chicago, and in 1976, he won the Nobel Prize in Literature.
=== STOP The Boathouse & Jensen's Prairie River @ 41.9056,-87.7007
Walk north through the park, past the formal rose garden with its circular beds, to the lagoon and the long, low boathouse on its shore, with its wide, overhanging roof and its open arches facing the water.

The boathouse was built in 1907, in the Prairie style, as part of the remaking of the park by one of the great landscape designers in American history, Jens Jensen. Jensen was a Danish immigrant who came to Chicago in 1886 and got a job as a laborer in the West Side parks. He worked his way up to superintendent of Humboldt Park, but in 1900, he was fired for refusing to go along with the political corruption that ran the park system. When reformers took over in 1905, they brought him back to run all of the West Side parks.

Jensen loved the native landscape of the Midwest: the prairies, the woods, and the rivers he found when he went exploring outside the city. He believed that people who lived in crowded city neighborhoods needed to experience that landscape, and he brought it into the parks. Here, he created what he called a prairie river, a waterway that winds through the park like a natural stream, its banks lined with flat, layered limestone, like the rock ledges along the rivers of Illinois, and planted with native wildflowers and grasses. He's considered the father of the Prairie style in landscape design, the counterpart of Frank Lloyd Wright's Prairie style in architecture.

A couple of miles south, in Garfield Park, Jensen designed one of the largest greenhouses in the world, the Garfield Park Conservatory, which opened in 1908. He called it landscape art under glass, and it's still one of the hidden treasures of the city.

This is the end of the Division Street tour. Humboldt Boulevard leads north from the park about a mile, to Palmer Square, where the Logan Square tour begins.
