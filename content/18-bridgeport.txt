title: Bridgeport & the Stockyards
area: South Side
blurb: Canal diggers, mayors and meatpackers: the White Sox and the Black Sox, the Daleys' front porch, a Spanish-style movie palace, the gate of the Union Stock Yards, and a Burnham and Root church in Canaryville.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour explores Bridgeport and the old Union Stock Yards, on the Southwest Side, about three miles south of the Loop. If you want to understand how Chicago really worked in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, its industry, its immigrants, and its politics, this is the place.

Bridgeport began with the Illinois and Michigan Canal. Digging started in 1836, at the South Branch of the Chicago River, and the workers were mostly Irish immigrants, who settled in shanties near the canal's starting point. The area was first nicknamed Hardscrabble. The name Bridgeport is said to come from a low bridge over the river that forced boats to unload their cargo here. When the canal opened in 1848, Bridgeport became an industrial district of lumber yards, brickyards, packinghouses, and rolling mills.

In 1865, just south of here, a group of railroads and meatpackers opened the Union Stock Yards, a vast complex of pens, chutes, and slaughterhouses where cattle, hogs, and sheep shipped in by rail from across the West were bought, sold, and killed. Companies like Armour, Swift, and Morris built enormous packing plants beside the yards. They turned the slaughter of animals into a moving assembly line, or more precisely, a disassembly line, where carcasses moved along overhead rails past workers who each made one cut. Henry Ford later said that the packinghouses' overhead trolleys helped inspire his assembly line. Swift and others used refrigerated railroad cars to ship fresh meat across the country. For decades, Chicago was the meatpacking capital of the world. Carl Sandburg, in his 1914 poem Chicago, called the city "Hog Butcher for the World."

The work was brutal and dangerous, the pay was low, and the smell hung over the whole South Side. The neighborhoods around the yards, Bridgeport, Canaryville, and Back of the Yards, filled with Irish, German, Polish, Lithuanian, Slovak, Croatian, and Mexican workers.

Bridgeport also gave Chicago its politics. From 1933 to 1979, and again from 1989 to 2011, almost every mayor of Chicago came from this one neighborhood: Edward Kelly, Martin Kennelly, Richard J. Daley, Michael Bilandic, and Richard M. Daley.

We'll start at the White Sox ballpark, walk west through Bridgeport to Halsted Street, and then south to the gate of the old stockyards, ending at a church in Canaryville.
=== STOP Rate Field & Old Comiskey @ 41.8306,-87.6325
You're near the Sox-35th Street station on the Red Line, in front of the home of the Chicago White Sox, at 35th Street and the Dan Ryan Expressway. The ballpark opened in 1991, and it has had several names since, most recently Rate Field.

Look across 35th Street, to the north, at the parking lot. That was the site of old Comiskey Park, which opened in 1910 and was the Sox's home for eighty years. Look for a marble plaque in the pavement, which marks the location of its home plate.

Old Comiskey saw some of the most famous moments in baseball. In 1919, the White Sox lost the World Series to the Cincinnati Reds, and it came out that eight Sox players had been paid by gamblers to throw it. The players were acquitted in court, but the new commissioner of baseball, Judge Kenesaw Mountain Landis, banned all eight for life, including the great hitter Shoeless Joe Jackson. A famous story has a boy outside the courthouse pleading, "Say it ain't so, Joe." That line is probably a reporter's invention, but the scandal was real, and the team became known as the Black Sox.

In 1933, during the Century of Progress fair, Comiskey Park hosted the first Major League Baseball All-Star Game. That same year, it also hosted the first East-West All-Star Game of the Negro Leagues, which was played here for most of the next three decades and drew huge crowds.

And in 1979, between games of a doubleheader, a radio host blew up a crate of disco records in center field, during a promotion called Disco Demolition Night. The crowd stormed the field, and the White Sox had to forfeit the second game.

The Sox finally won the World Series again in 2005, their first championship since 1917. Bridgeport is White Sox territory. The North Side has the Cubs. That divide is still one of the most reliable ways to start an argument in Chicago.

Why were they called the Black Sox? The obvious answer is the scandal. But there's an older story, that the team's owner, Charles Comiskey, was so stingy that he made his players pay to have their own uniforms washed, and when they refused, the uniforms got dirtier and dirtier. Comiskey's stinginess is often given as a reason why his players were tempted by the gamblers' money. The laundry story is probably part legend, but Chicagoans have been telling it for a century.

The South Side was also the capital of Black baseball. A few blocks south of here, at 39th Street, stood the White Sox's earlier ballpark, which later became the home of the Chicago American Giants, owned and managed by Andrew Rube Foster, a former star pitcher. In 1920, Foster organized the Negro National League, the first successful professional league for Black players, and he's often called the father of Black baseball.
=== STOP The Daleys' Bridgeport @ 41.8296,-87.6427
Walk west on 35th Street, across the expressway and into the residential streets of Bridgeport, to Lowe Avenue, and turn south. At number 3536 South Lowe, there's a modest brick bungalow. A police booth often stood nearby. This was the home of Richard J. Daley, mayor of Chicago from 1955 until his death in 1976.

Daley was born in Bridgeport in 1902, the only child of Irish American parents, and he lived in this neighborhood all his life. He worked in the stockyards as a young man, studied law at night, and rose through the Democratic Party organization of the eleventh ward, the most powerful political base in the city.

As mayor and as chairman of the Cook County Democratic Party, Daley ran what was probably the most powerful urban political machine in America. Patronage jobs were handed out in exchange for votes and loyalty, and the precinct captains knew every voter on their block. Under Daley, the city built expressways, O'Hare Airport, McCormick Place, and much of the modern Loop, and it was called "the city that works." It also built huge public housing projects that concentrated poverty, and it resisted the civil rights movement's campaign against segregation. The police violence during the 1968 Democratic convention happened on his watch. The newspaper columnist Mike Royko called him simply "Boss."

Just south of here, at 37th and Lowe, is the Nativity of Our Lord church, where the Daley family worshipped, and where his funeral was held. In his youth, Daley was president of the Hamburg Athletic Club, one of the neighborhood social clubs that were partly gangs. Members of such clubs were accused of attacking Black Chicagoans during the 1919 race riot. What role, if any, Daley played has never been established, and he never spoke about it.

His son, Richard M. Daley, was mayor from 1989 to 2011, even longer than his father.

Daley was famous for his loyalty, his temper, and his tangled sentences. During the turmoil of 1968, he told reporters, "The policeman isn't there to create disorder. The policeman is there to preserve disorder." Everyone knew what he meant.

His machine was also famous for getting out the vote, sometimes too well. In the presidential election of 1960, John F. Kennedy won Illinois by fewer than nine thousand votes, and Republicans charged that the Daley machine had stolen the election for him in Chicago, with votes cast in the names of the dead. Whether the fraud was enough to change the result has been argued ever since, and it's become one of the great legends of Chicago politics.
=== STOP The Ramova & Halsted Street @ 41.8303,-87.6466
Walk west to Halsted Street and north to number 3518. The small Spanish-style movie theater with the vertical sign is the Ramova Theatre, opened in 1929.

The Ramova was an atmospheric theater. Its auditorium was designed to look like a Spanish courtyard under an open night sky, with twinkling stars and drifting clouds on the ceiling, and it seated about fifteen hundred people. For decades, it was the neighborhood movie house, and the diner next door, the Ramova Grill, was famous for its chili. The theater closed in 1985 and stood empty for decades. It was restored and reopened in 2023 as a concert venue, with a brewery, and the Grill was revived.

Halsted Street was Bridgeport's main shopping street, lined with taverns, bakeries, and shops serving the Irish, Lithuanian, Polish, Italian, and Croatian families who lived on the side streets. Today, the neighborhood is changing again. Many Mexican American families live here now, and Chinatown has spread into Bridgeport's northeastern corner, so you'll see Chinese restaurants and signs alongside the old Irish taverns.

A few blocks west of here, on 35th Street, the huge concrete building known as the Bridgeport Art Center was once a warehouse for Spiegel, one of the big Chicago mail-order companies, which, like Sears and Montgomery Ward, shipped catalogs and goods to homes across the country. Today, it's full of artists' studios.

Look at the houses on the side streets as you walk. Many are small wooden cottages, some sitting below the level of the street, because the city raised the streets for sewers long after they were built.

A couple of blocks south, at 37th and Halsted, is Schaller's Pump, which has been serving drinks since 1881, making it one of the oldest taverns in Chicago. For decades, it stood across the street from the headquarters of the eleventh ward Democratic organization, Daley's political home, and it's said that more city business was settled at its bar than at City Hall. During Prohibition, it was a speakeasy, and legend says it never actually stopped selling beer.
=== STOP The Union Stock Yard Gate @ 41.8187,-87.6484
Walk south on Halsted Street, past the old railroad tracks, to Exchange Avenue, and turn west to Peoria Street. Here stands a triple-arched limestone gate. It's almost all that remains of the Union Stock Yards.

The gate was built in 1879, and its design is attributed to the young firm of Burnham and Root, who also designed buildings inside the yards. Look at the carved head of a steer over the central arch. It's said to represent Sherman, a prize steer named after John B. Sherman, the yards' longtime manager. Through this gate passed buyers, sellers, workers, and visitors. Touring the stockyards was one of the most popular attractions in Chicago, and visitors came from around the world to watch the killing floors.

The yards covered hundreds of acres, with miles of pens, and at their peak, tens of thousands of people worked here and in the packing plants. In 1906, Upton Sinclair published The Jungle, a novel about a Lithuanian immigrant family working in the yards, based on weeks of reporting here. Sinclair wanted to expose how workers were treated, but readers were more horrified by his descriptions of rotten meat, rats, and filth. "I aimed at the public's heart," he said, "and by accident I hit it in the stomach." Within months, Congress passed the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act.

In December 1910, a fire in a meatpacking warehouse here killed twenty-one firefighters, including the city's fire chief, James Horan. Nearby stood the International Amphitheatre, a huge arena built next to the yards in 1934, which hosted political conventions, including the 1968 Democratic National Convention, and concerts by Elvis Presley and the Beatles.

As the meat industry decentralized, moving closer to the ranches, the packers left Chicago. The Union Stock Yards closed in 1971. Today, the land is an industrial park. Just north and west of here, a fork of the river was so polluted with packinghouse waste that it bubbled with gas. It's still called Bubbly Creek.

The packers liked to say that they used every part of the pig except the squeal. Nothing was wasted. Blood, bones, hooves, hides, and fat were turned into fertilizer, glue, soap, gelatin, brushes, buttons, and even strings for musical instruments and tennis rackets. The waste that couldn't be used went into the river, and into Bubbly Creek.

The neighborhoods around the yards, especially Back of the Yards, just to the west, were among the most crowded and polluted in the city. Families lived in wooden houses beside the pens and the smokestacks, the air smelled of animals and rendering vats, and children worked in the plants. The packers fought unions for decades, and the great strikes of 1904 and 1921 were both broken. It wasn't until the late 1930s and 1940s that the packinghouse workers finally won union contracts, with Black, white, and Mexican workers organizing together.
=== STOP Saint Gabriel Church @ 41.8118,-87.6419
Finish by walking southeast to 45th Street and Lowe Avenue, in the neighborhood called Canaryville. The name may have come from the flocks of sparrows that fed on the grain spilled around the stockyards, or from the rowdy young men of the neighborhood, who were called canaries. No one knows for sure.

The big brick church with the tall, square tower is Saint Gabriel Church, built in 1887 and 1888, and designed by John Wellborn Root, of Burnham and Root. Root designed few churches, and this is a fine one, with massive brick walls and Romanesque arches, and a tower that rises in simple stages, with almost no ornament. It's the same spirit you saw in his Monadnock Building downtown.

The church was built for an Irish parish led by Father Maurice Dorney, who was one of the most powerful men in the stockyards district. He was known as the King of the Yards. He helped settle strikes between workers and packers, found jobs for his parishioners, and fought the saloons. It's said that he persuaded the packers to donate money for the church, and that the bricks were laid by men from the yards.

Canaryville and Back of the Yards were tough, working-class neighborhoods, and in 1939, a young organizer named Saul Alinsky worked with churches, unions, and local groups here to create the Back of the Yards Neighborhood Council. It became a model for community organizing across the country, and its methods later influenced, among many others, a young organizer named Barack Obama.

This is the end of the Bridgeport and Stockyards tour.
