title: Uptown & Andersonville
area: North Side
blurb: Jazz-age Uptown and Swedish Andersonville: the Green Mill and its gangsters, the great ballroom and movie palace, Chaplin's Chicago studio, Little Saigon on Argyle, Clark Street's Swedes, and the pink hotel where a ballplayer was shot.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour explores two neighborhoods on the Far North Side, about six miles north of the Loop: Uptown, along Broadway and Lawrence Avenue, and Andersonville, along Clark Street.

A century ago, Uptown was one of the great entertainment districts in America. The elevated line reached here in 1900, and in the 1910s and 1920s, the neighborhood boomed. Developers built apartment hotels, ballrooms, nightclubs, and movie palaces, aimed at the young, single office workers who were pouring into Chicago and wanted to live near the lake and the trains. On a Saturday night in the 1920s, tens of thousands of people came to Uptown to dance at the Aragon, see a movie at the Uptown Theatre, or listen to jazz at the Green Mill. Uptown was also, briefly, part of the movie business, because before Hollywood took over, Chicago was one of the centers of American film, and one of its biggest studios was here.

After the Depression and the Second World War, Uptown declined. Its big apartments were divided into small units, and it became a landing place for newcomers with little money: white migrants from Appalachia in the 1950s, Native Americans relocated from reservations by a federal program, and later refugees from Southeast Asia, Ethiopia, and many other places. For decades, it was one of the most diverse neighborhoods in the country, and also one of the poorest, with a large population in single-room hotels. In recent decades, much of it has gentrified.

Andersonville, just to the west, has a different story. It grew up as a Swedish community in the late nineteenth century. Swedes came to Chicago in large numbers, and by 1900, Chicago had one of the largest Swedish populations of any city in the world. Swedish shops, bakeries, delis, and churches lined Clark Street. Many Swedes have since moved away, but the neighborhood has kept its Swedish flavor, and since the 1980s, it has also become one of the centers of Chicago's lesbian and gay community, known for its independent businesses.

We'll start at the corner of Broadway and Lawrence, in the heart of old Uptown, walk north and west through Argyle Street, then to Andersonville on Clark Street, and end at the lake in Edgewater.
=== STOP The Green Mill & the Uptown Theatre @ 41.9693,-87.6600
You're at the corner of Broadway and Lawrence Avenue. On the northwest corner, look for the green neon sign of the Green Mill Cocktail Lounge, one of the oldest and most famous jazz clubs in the country.

It opened in 1907 as a roadhouse, and around 1910 it was renamed the Green Mill, after the Moulin Rouge, the red mill, in Paris, complete with a green windmill on the roof. In the 1920s, during Prohibition, a part owner was Jack McGurn, known as Machine Gun Jack, one of Al Capone's top gunmen. There's a well-known story about the comedian and singer Joe E. Lewis, who performed here. In 1927, he decided to leave for a rival club. Shortly afterwards, he was attacked, and his throat was cut. He survived, learned to speak and sing again, and went on to a long career. The story was later made into a film starring Frank Sinatra.

Inside, there's a booth said to have been Capone's favorite, with a view of both doors, and there are stories of trap doors and tunnels under the bar. Those are legends, but the club's dark wood, velvet booths, and statues haven't changed much since the 1930s.

The Green Mill is also where the poetry slam was born. In 1986, a construction worker and poet named Marc Smith started a Sunday night competition here, where poets performed their work and the audience judged them. Poetry slams have since spread all over the world.

Now look across Broadway at the huge, ornate building with the tall vertical sign. This is the Uptown Theatre, opened in 1925 by Balaban and Katz, the same company that built the Chicago Theatre downtown, and designed by the same architects, the Rapp brothers. With more than four thousand seats, it was one of the largest movie palaces in the country, with a lobby that rises several stories, like a Spanish cathedral. It closed in 1981, and efforts to restore it have dragged on for years.

Jack McGurn, the Green Mill's gangster part owner, was the chief suspect in planning the Saint Valentine's Day Massacre in 1929, though he was never convicted. Seven years later, almost to the day, on February 15th, 1936, he was shot dead in a bowling alley on Milwaukee Avenue. Today, the Green Mill is still a working jazz club, open late every night, and it's one of the best places in Chicago to hear live music.

Before you leave the corner, walk a block south on Broadway to Leland Avenue, and look at the Uptown Broadway Building, built in 1926. Its facade is covered with colorful glazed terra cotta in an extravagant Spanish style, with twisting columns, shields, and carved figures. It's one of the most exuberant terra cotta buildings in a city full of them, and a reminder of how much money flowed into Uptown in the 1920s.
=== STOP The Aragon Ballroom @ 41.9693,-87.6580
Walk east on Lawrence Avenue to number 1106, the Aragon Ballroom, with its tall, Spanish-style facade.

The Aragon opened in 1926, built by two Greek immigrant brothers, Andrew and William Karzas, who also owned the Trianon Ballroom on the South Side. The interior was designed to look like the courtyard of a Spanish palace, with balconies, arches, tiled roofs, and a ceiling painted like a night sky, with twinkling stars and drifting clouds. It was meant to feel like dancing outdoors.

In the age of the big bands, from the 1920s to the 1950s, the Aragon was one of the most famous ballrooms in America. The great dance orchestras played here, and their performances were broadcast live on the radio across the country. The Karzas brothers enforced strict rules of conduct to make the ballroom respectable: no improper dancing, and an atmosphere that made it safe for young women to come.

As big band dancing faded, the Aragon was used for many things, including roller skating, boxing, and wrestling. Since the 1960s, it has been a concert venue, especially for rock, and it was known for a while by the nickname the Aragon Brawlroom, for its rowdy crowds. Many of the biggest names in rock have played here, along with generations of Chicago's own bands.

As you walk along Broadway and Lawrence, look for other remnants of the old entertainment district, including the Riviera Theatre, at Broadway and Racine, which opened in 1917, and the big apartment hotels, built for single people, with small apartments and shared lobbies and dining rooms.
=== STOP Argyle Street @ 41.9731,-87.6560
Walk north on Broadway to Argyle Street, and turn east. This short stretch of Argyle, between Broadway and Sheridan Road, is the heart of what's sometimes called Little Saigon, or Asia on Argyle.

After the end of the Vietnam War in 1975, hundreds of thousands of refugees left Vietnam, Cambodia, and Laos, many of them by boat. Many of them came to the United States, and some were resettled in Chicago, often in Uptown, where rents were cheap. Ethnic Chinese from Vietnam, who had faced persecution there, were a large part of this group. They opened restaurants, groceries, bakeries, jewelry stores, and travel agencies along Argyle Street, turning it into one of the most important Southeast Asian commercial streets in the Midwest.

There had been plans, in the 1970s, by Chinese American business leaders, to develop this area as a second Chinatown. The arrival of the refugees gave the street its character.

Today, Argyle Street is famous for its Vietnamese restaurants, especially its bowls of pho, the beef noodle soup, and banh mi sandwiches. In the late 2010s, the street was redesigned as a shared street, without curbs, so that pedestrians, cyclists, and cars mix, and it hosts night markets in summer.

The Uptown neighborhood around it has also been home to refugees and immigrants from Ethiopia, Eritrea, Somalia, Bosnia, and many other places, as well as a large Native American community, and it's one of the most diverse parts of the city.

In the 1950s and 1960s, Uptown was home to so many poor white migrants from the mountains of Kentucky, Tennessee, and West Virginia, who came north to work in the factories, that people called it Hillbilly Heaven. In 1969, a group of young Southern whites from Uptown, called the Young Patriots, joined an alliance with Fred Hampton's Black Panther Party and the Puerto Rican Young Lords, to fight together against poverty, police brutality, and urban renewal. Hampton called it the Rainbow Coalition. It didn't last long, but the name did. Jesse Jackson later borrowed it for his own political movement.
=== STOP Essanay Studios @ 41.9724,-87.6633
Walk west on Argyle Street, under the elevated tracks, to number 1345. Look at the entrance of the long, low brick building, and you'll see a terra cotta portrait of a Native American chief in a feathered headdress. That was the logo of Essanay Studios, one of the most important movie studios in America in the 1910s.

Essanay was founded in 1907 by George Spoor, a Chicago businessman, and Gilbert Anderson, an actor. The name comes from their initials, S and A. Anderson became famous as Broncho Billy, one of the first cowboy stars of the movies. The studio made hundreds of films here, and for a few years, Chicago was one of the capitals of the film industry.

In late 1914, Essanay signed the biggest star in the world, Charlie Chaplin, at a huge salary. Chaplin came to this studio in the winter and made a film here, called His New Job, released in 1915. But he hated the cold, gray Chicago winter, and he didn't get along with Spoor. He soon moved to the company's studio in California. A young woman named Gloria Swanson also got her start at Essanay as an extra, and went on to become one of the greatest stars of the silent era.

Hollywood's weather, light, and space soon drew the film industry west, and Essanay closed by the end of the 1910s. Today, the building is part of Saint Augustine College, which has kept the studio's history alive, and you can still see the terra cotta logos over the doors.
=== STOP Andersonville & the Swedish American Museum @ 41.9767,-87.6681
Walk west on Argyle and then north on Clark Street to Foster Avenue, and continue to the 5200 block of Clark. This is the heart of Andersonville.

In the late nineteenth century, Swedish immigrants settled here. The usual explanation is that after the Great Fire of 1871, the city banned wooden buildings within its limits, and this area was then outside the city, so working families could build cheaper wooden houses here. Andersonville became a village of Swedish shops, churches, and social clubs. Clark Street had Swedish bakeries, delis, and restaurants, and Swedish was spoken in the stores.

At number 5211, look for the Swedish American Museum, founded in 1976. It tells the story of Swedish immigration to America, and it has a children's museum about the journey of immigrants. Look for the Swedish flags along the street, and for the blue and yellow on many of the shops.

Andersonville celebrates its heritage each June with Midsommarfest, a street festival marking the summer solstice, the most important holiday of the Swedish year. In December, there's a Swedish Christmas festival, with the procession of Saint Lucia, a girl in a white gown with a crown of candles.

Since the 1980s, Andersonville has also been one of the centers of Chicago's gay and lesbian community. Women and Children First, one of the largest feminist bookstores in the country, has been here since the 1990s. The neighborhood is known for its independent shops and restaurants, and its business association has campaigned to keep chain stores to a minimum.

As you walk along Clark Street, look for the survivors of Swedish Andersonville. At number 5210, Simon's Tavern, opened by a Swedish immigrant in the 1930s, still has its old neon sign of a fish holding a martini glass. You'll also find Swedish pancakes with lingonberries on the menus nearby. A couple of miles west, along Foster Avenue, the Swedish Covenant church built a hospital and a college, North Park University, which are still there.

Chicago's Swedes were carpenters, builders, machinists, and domestic servants, and some became very successful. Charles Walgreen, the son of Swedish immigrants, opened his first drugstore on Chicago's South Side in 1901, and built it into one of the largest drugstore chains in the country.
=== STOP The Edgewater Beach Apartments @ 41.9837,-87.6547
Walk north on Clark Street and then east on Bryn Mawr Avenue all the way to Sheridan Road, near the lake. Here, at number 5555 North Sheridan Road, stands a tall, pink apartment building with a pointed roof. This is the Edgewater Beach Apartments, completed in 1928.

It's the survivor of a famous resort. Next to it, on the lake, stood the Edgewater Beach Hotel, a sprawling hotel painted a cheerful yellow, which opened in 1916. For decades, it was one of the most glamorous resorts in the Midwest. It had its own private beach, a boardwalk along the lake called the Beach Walk, where big bands played outdoors on summer nights, and guests including presidents and movie stars. Its broadcasts of dance music went out across the country on the radio.

In June 1949, the hotel was the scene of a famous crime. Eddie Waitkus, the first baseman for the Philadelphia Phillies, who were in town to play the Cubs, received a note asking him to come to a room in the hotel. There, a young woman named Ruth Ann Steinhagen, who had become obsessed with him, shot him with a rifle. He survived. The incident inspired Bernard Malamud's novel The Natural, about a baseball player who is shot by a mysterious woman.

In the 1950s, Lake Shore Drive was extended north past the hotel, on landfill, cutting it off from the lake, and it went into decline. It closed in 1967 and was demolished a few years later. Only the pink apartment building remains, a reminder of the days when Edgewater was a summer resort at the edge of the city.

This is the end of the Uptown and Andersonville tour.
