=== TOUR
id: gold-coast
title: The Gold Coast
area: North Side
blurb: Mansions, gangsters and soapbox orators: Holy Name Cathedral, Bughouse Square, the Pump Room, the first Playboy Mansion, Sullivan and Wright on Astor Street, and the Palmers' vanished castle.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour explores the Gold Coast, the few blocks between Michigan Avenue and Lincoln Park that have been Chicago's richest neighborhood for more than a century.

It did not start that way. After the Great Fire of 1871, the fashionable address in Chicago was Prairie Avenue, on the South Side, where the families of George Pullman, Marshall Field, and Philip Armour lived. The land along the lake north of the river was swampy, sandy, and mostly empty.

The man who changed that was Potter Palmer. He had made a fortune in dry goods, in a store that later became Marshall Field's, and then in real estate, transforming State Street into the city's main shopping street. In the 1880s, he bought up the marshy land along the lake north of Oak Street, filled it, and built himself and his wife Bertha a turreted stone castle on the new Lake Shore Drive. Where the Palmers went, society followed. The value of the land around them soared, and Palmer made a second fortune selling it.

The Gold Coast that grew up here is a mix of styles. You will see Romanesque mansions with heavy stone arches, French chateaux, Georgian Revival townhouses, and, sitting quietly among them, a house designed by Louis Sullivan and a young Frank Lloyd Wright that pointed toward a completely different future. From the 1920s on, many mansions were torn down for elegant apartment towers, so the neighborhood is a layer cake of the Gilded Age and the twentieth century.

The Gold Coast has had its colorful side too: gangsters shooting it out in the street in the 1920s, anarchists and hoboes giving speeches in the park, celebrities in the Pump Room, and Hugh Hefner's first Playboy Mansion.

We will start at Holy Name Cathedral, south of the neighborhood proper, and walk north, ending at the edge of Lincoln Park.
=== STOP Holy Name Cathedral @ 41.8960,-87.6276
You are at the corner of State Street and Superior Street, in front of Holy Name Cathedral, the seat of the Roman Catholic archbishop of Chicago. The Gothic Revival church was designed by Patrick Keely and dedicated in 1875, after an earlier church was lost in the fire.

Chicago has one of the largest Catholic populations in the country, built from waves of Irish, German, Polish, Italian, and Mexican immigrants. For much of the twentieth century, the city's politics and its parishes were tightly woven together, and Chicagoans still often answer the question of where they grew up by naming their parish.

In October 1926, this corner was the site of one of the most notorious killings of the Prohibition era. Hymie Weiss, a leader of the North Side Gang and an enemy of Al Capone, was gunned down as he crossed State Street toward his headquarters above a flower shop, which stood across the street. The gunmen fired from a window of a rooming house nearby. Some of the bullets struck the front of the cathedral, and for years afterward, people pointed to marks on the cornerstone. The church has since been renovated, but the story is part of the city's lore.

Look up at the cathedral's ceiling if you go inside. Hanging high above the altar are several red hats, called galeros, which belonged to cardinals of Chicago. By tradition, a cardinal's hat is hung from the ceiling of his cathedral after his death, and it stays there until it decays and falls, which is said to symbolize the passing of earthly glory.

Weiss wasn't the first gang leader to die on this corner. His boss, Dean O'Banion, ran the North Side Gang from a flower shop across the street, Schofield's, where he arranged the elaborate floral tributes for gangland funerals. In November 1924, three men came into the shop, one shook his hand and held on, and the others shot him. The killing, widely blamed on Capone's organization, started the war in which Weiss and so many others died. Gangsters went to mass here, too. Holy Name has seen a great deal of Chicago's history, from the funerals of mayors to a visit from Pope John Paul the Second in 1979.
=== STOP Newberry Library & Bughouse Square @ 41.8997,-87.6305
Walk north on State Street and west on Walton Street to Washington Square Park. The small square, laid out in the 1840s, is one of the oldest parks in the city.

On its north side stands the Newberry Library, a dark granite building in Spanish Romanesque style, completed in 1893 and designed by Henry Ives Cobb. It was founded with money left by Walter Loomis Newberry, an early Chicago banker and land speculator, and it is a free, independent research library, with famous collections of maps, Native American history, and genealogy.

The park in front of it had a very different reputation. For much of the twentieth century, it was known as Bughouse Square, bughouse being slang for an insane asylum. From roughly the 1910s to the 1960s, it was the city's great outdoor free speech forum, where orators stood on soapboxes and argued with the crowd on any subject. Socialists, anarchists, members of the radical union called the Industrial Workers of the World, religious preachers, poets, and cranks of all kinds spoke here. The writer and activist Lucy Parsons, widow of one of the men executed after the Haymarket affair, spoke in this park. Crowds came to heckle as much as to listen, and the heckling was part of the show.

The Newberry Library brings the tradition back each summer with the Bughouse Square Debates, where speakers once again climb onto soapboxes in the park.

The Newberry is a library for researchers, but anyone over high school age can get a reader's card and use it. Its collections include one of the great libraries of maps in the country, the business archives of the Pullman Company and the Illinois Central Railroad, a huge collection on Native American history gathered by the collector Edward Ayer, and rare books and manuscripts going back to the Middle Ages. It also has one of the largest genealogy collections in the country, and many visitors come to trace their families.

Just off the square, in a narrow alley called Tooker Place, was the Dill Pickle Club, a bohemian nightclub and forum that flourished from the late 1910s into the early 1930s. Its door was painted with a sign telling visitors to step high, stoop low, and leave their dignity outside. Writers like Carl Sandburg and Sherwood Anderson, the lawyer Clarence Darrow, radicals, poets, and curious society people all crowded in for lectures, debates, plays, and jazz. One of the regulars was Ben Reitman, a doctor who treated the poor and was known as the King of the Hobos, and who was the manager and lover of the anarchist Emma Goldman.
=== STOP The Pump Room & the First Playboy Mansion @ 41.9066,-87.6287
Walk north on Dearborn or State Parkway to Goethe Street. At the corner of State Parkway and Goethe stands a hotel that was long known as the Ambassador East. It was the home of the Pump Room, a restaurant named for the famous Pump Room in the English spa town of Bath, which opened in 1938 and became the place where celebrities stopped on their journeys between New York and Los Angeles. Trains between the coasts changed in Chicago, and stars often spent a night here between trains. The most coveted seat was called Booth One, where Humphrey Bogart, Lauren Bacall, Judy Garland, and many other stars posed for photographs.

Now walk a few steps north to number 1340 North State Parkway, the large red brick and limestone mansion on the east side of the street. This was the original Playboy Mansion. Hugh Hefner, who was born in Chicago and started Playboy magazine in 1953, bought it in 1959. He lived here and threw famous parties, with a pool, a bowling alley, and a brass plate on the door with a Latin motto that roughly translated as: if you don't swing, don't ring.

Playboy was a Chicago company, and for years it was one of the most visible in the city, with its giant sign on the Palmolive Building and its Playboy Clubs. Hefner moved to Los Angeles in the 1970s, and the mansion was later donated to the School of the Art Institute of Chicago, which used it as a dormitory before it was sold and turned into condominiums.

Look along this stretch of State Parkway, and you'll see some of the grandest townhouses in the city.

A few blocks east of here, along Rush Street and Division Street, is the Gold Coast's nightlife district, a strip of bars, steakhouses, and clubs that has been busy since the 1950s. In those years Rush Street was a famous entertainment strip, with jazz clubs and supper clubs where rising performers played. One of them, Mister Kelly's, gave early stages to comedians and singers who went on to become stars, including a young Barbra Streisand. Today the area around Rush and Oak Streets is full of steakhouses and hotel bars, and its triangle of outdoor patios is a people-watching spot on summer evenings.
=== STOP Astor Street & the Charnley-Persky House @ 41.9077,-87.6274
Walk east to Astor Street, a quiet, tree-lined street named after the fur baron John Jacob Astor, as if to borrow some New York money for Chicago. It is one of the best preserved streets of mansions in the city.

Walk to number 1365 North Astor. The house on the corner of Schiller Street looks deceptively simple compared to its neighbors: a cube of thin Roman brick on a stone base, with a balcony in the middle of the front. This is the Charnley-Persky House, built in the early 1890s for James Charnley, a lumber businessman, by the firm of Adler and Sullivan.

The young draftsman who worked on it was Frank Lloyd Wright, then in his twenties, who would later claim a large share of the credit for it. Whoever deserves it, historians often point to this house as an early sign of what would become modern American architecture: clean, horizontal lines, unbroken surfaces, ornament limited to a few rich places like the balcony. Compare it to the heavy, picturesque Romanesque and Queen Anne mansions around it, built at the same time.

The house is now the headquarters of the Society of Architectural Historians, which offers tours.

As you walk along Astor Street, look for a few other gems. At 1444 North Astor is an Art Deco townhouse, called the Russell House, designed by Holabird and Root in 1929. And look for the Patterson-McCormick Mansion at 20 East Burton Place, a huge palazzo of brick and terra cotta by Stanford White of New York, built for a family that controlled the Chicago Tribune.
=== STOP Potter & Bertha Palmer's Castle @ 41.9071,-87.6257
Walk east to Lake Shore Drive, and look at the apartment towers between Banks Street and Schiller Street. This is the site of the house that created the Gold Coast: the Palmer castle, at 1350 North Lake Shore Drive.

Potter Palmer built it in the early 1880s. It was a fantasy of a castle in brown stone, with turrets and an eighty-foot tower, and supposedly no outside doorknobs, so that no one could enter unless a servant opened the door from inside. The Palmers held court here for decades.

Bertha Honoré Palmer was as formidable as her husband. In 1893 she was president of the Board of Lady Managers of the World's Columbian Exposition, responsible for the Woman's Building. She was also an early and passionate collector of French Impressionist paintings, buying works by Monet, Renoir, and Degas when they were still controversial. Many of the paintings she collected are now at the Art Institute of Chicago, and they're one of the reasons its Impressionist collection is among the best in the world.

Potter Palmer is also remembered for the Palmer House hotel downtown. The first one opened shortly before the Great Fire and burned less than two weeks later. He rebuilt immediately, promising a fireproof hotel, and the Palmer House still stands on State Street. The hotel's kitchen is often credited with inventing the chocolate brownie for the 1893 fair, which Bertha is said to have requested as a portable dessert for fairgoers. That's a story the hotel tells, and it's a good one.

The castle itself was torn down in 1950 and replaced with these apartment buildings, which is a pattern repeated up and down the drive.
=== STOP The Museum of Surgical Science @ 41.9102,-87.6269
Walk north along Lake Shore Drive to number 1524, a limestone mansion with a columned facade facing the lake. It's one of the very few mansions left on this stretch of the drive, which was once lined with them. It was built in 1917 for Eleanor Robinson Countiss, the daughter of a wealthy Chicago family, and designed by Howard Van Doren Shaw, one of the favorite architects of Chicago's rich. Shaw modeled it on the Petit Trianon, the small palace built for Marie Antoinette at Versailles.

Since the 1950s, the house has held one of the strangest museums in Chicago: the International Museum of Surgical Science, founded by the International College of Surgeons. Inside the elegant rooms are four floors of the history of medicine: ancient skulls with holes drilled in them by prehistoric surgeons, sets of amputation saws, early X-ray machines, an iron lung, and paintings and sculptures of famous moments in medicine. Some of it is grisly. All of it is fascinating, and you can see the mansion's original paneling, staircases, and fireplaces as you go.

Look up and down Lake Shore Drive at the high-rise apartments that replaced the mansions. By the 1920s, the rich were already moving into luxury apartment buildings, which were easier to run than a house full of servants, and the drive filled with cooperatives. Many of the most famous, like the ones at 1500 and 999 Lake Shore Drive, have apartments of a dozen rooms or more on a single floor, with views straight out over the lake.
=== STOP The Archbishop's Residence @ 41.9107,-87.6286
Walk north and west to the corner of North Avenue and State Parkway. The large red brick mansion surrounded by a lawn, with a remarkable roof crowded with chimneys, is the Archbishop's Residence, built around 1880.

Count the chimneys if you can. There are said to be nineteen of them. It was built when this area was at the very northern edge of the city, next to a cemetery that was later moved to create Lincoln Park. The Queen Anne style residence was the home of Chicago's Catholic archbishops for more than a century, and visiting dignitaries stayed here, including Pope John Paul the Second, in 1979.

The Gold Coast you have just walked was built by the wealth of a single generation of Chicagoans, the merchants, meatpackers, railroad men, and real estate speculators who rebuilt the city after the fire. Their mansions show what they wanted to be seen as: European aristocrats. The houses designed by Sullivan, Wright, and Mies just a few blocks away show the other side of the city: a place that wanted to invent something new.

Look north, across North Avenue. That's Lincoln Park, the city's largest park, and the Lincoln Park tour starts just across the street, at the Chicago History Museum. This is the end of the Gold Coast tour.
