=== TOUR
id: pilsen
title: Pilsen
area: West & Northwest Sides
blurb: A Czech neighborhood that became the heart of Mexican Chicago: the railroad strike of 1877, Thalia Hall, the mural movement, a high school won by protest, and the National Museum of Mexican Art.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour explores Pilsen, on the Lower West Side, about two miles southwest of the Loop. It's one of the most vibrant neighborhoods in Chicago, famous for its murals, its Mexican bakeries and restaurants, and its long history as a home for immigrants.

The neighborhood was settled in the second half of the nineteenth century by Czech immigrants, who called themselves Bohemians, along with Germans, Irish, Poles, Slovaks, and others. They came to work in the lumber yards and factories along the South Branch of the Chicago River and in the rail yards around it. In the 1870s and 1880s, Chicago was the largest lumber market in the world, and huge stacks of white pine from Michigan and Wisconsin lined the river just south of here.

The name Pilsen comes from the Czech city of Plzeň. The story goes that one of the early restaurants in the neighborhood was named At the City of Pilsen, and the name spread to the whole area.

Pilsen was a working-class neighborhood with a strong tradition of labor activism. Workers here were involved in the great railroad strike of 1877 and in the eight-hour movement of the 1880s.

In the 1950s and 1960s, the Czech and Polish families began moving to the suburbs, and Mexican families arrived, many of them displaced from the Near West Side by the construction of expressways and the University of Illinois campus. By 1970, Pilsen was majority Mexican, and it became the heart of Mexican Chicago. Activists, artists, and community groups transformed it, painting murals on walls all over the neighborhood and building institutions, from a high school to a national museum.

Today, Pilsen is facing the pressures of gentrification, and you may see signs and murals protesting it. We'll start at the eastern end of 18th Street, near Halsted Street, and walk west, ending at the National Museum of Mexican Art.
=== STOP The Viaduct & the Arts District @ 41.8588,-87.6468
You're on Halsted Street, near 16th Street, at the northeastern edge of Pilsen. Look north, where Halsted rises over the railroad tracks. In July 1877, this was the site of what's known as the Battle of the Viaduct.

That summer, a nationwide railroad strike broke out after the railroads cut wages. In Chicago, workers from many industries joined the strike. Here on Halsted, crowds of workers clashed with police and militia over several days. Around thirty people were killed in Chicago during the strike, many of them young workers, and many of them from this neighborhood. The strike was crushed, but it shocked the country, and it helped launch decades of labor struggle in Chicago.

Walk south to 18th Street. The blocks around Halsted and 18th Street are known as the Chicago Arts District, with galleries and studios in old industrial buildings, many of them opened since the 1990s.

As you walk west on 18th Street, look up at the facades. You'll see brick buildings with stores on the ground floor and apartments above, many built in the late nineteenth century by Czech immigrants. Some still have names and dates carved over their doorways, and some have the projecting bay windows of Czech and German design. Today, the storefronts are mostly Mexican bakeries, restaurants, and shops.

Pilsen was also part of the story of Haymarket. Many of the workers who joined the eight-hour strikes of May 1886 lived here, and the McCormick Reaper Works, where police killed strikers on May third, the day before the Haymarket bombing, stood just southwest of the neighborhood, at Blue Island and Western Avenues. Cyrus McCormick had invented a mechanical reaper that let one farmer harvest as much grain as several men with scythes, and his Chicago factory made the city the center of the farm machinery industry. The company later became International Harvester.
=== STOP Thalia Hall & Saint Procopius @ 41.8579,-87.6575
Walk west on 18th Street to Allport Street. On the corner, the big brick and stone building with a tower is Thalia Hall. It was built in 1892 by a Czech immigrant named John Dusek, and it was said to be modeled on an opera house in Prague. It held a theater, a hall, shops, and apartments, and for decades it was the center of Czech cultural life in the neighborhood: plays, concerts, political meetings, and social clubs. It sat largely unused for many years, and it was restored and reopened in 2013 as a music venue.

A block north, on Allport Street, is Saint Procopius Church, built in the 1880s for the Czech Catholic community, one of the first Czech parishes in Chicago. Benedictine monks from the parish founded a college here, which later moved to the suburbs and became Benedictine University. Today, Saint Procopius serves a largely Mexican congregation, and its masses are in Spanish.

Just to the east, at 1831 South Racine Avenue, is a building that was once Casa Aztlán, a community center that was the heart of the Chicano movement in Pilsen. In the 1970s, the artist Ray Patlán and others covered its walls with murals of Mexican history, workers, and revolutionaries. In 2017, after the building was sold and converted to apartments, the murals were painted over, to the anger of many in the neighborhood, and it became a symbol of the fight over gentrification.

Czech Pilsen was famous for its societies. There were gymnastic clubs called Sokols, which promoted physical fitness and Czech national pride, savings and loan associations that helped immigrants buy houses, freethinkers' clubs, and theater groups. Many Czechs here were skeptical of the church, and the neighborhood had both devout Catholic parishes and halls for those who'd rather debate politics on a Sunday. One of the most famous Czech Chicagoans, Anton Cermak, began his career in this part of the city, as a teenager working in the rail yards and then driving a wagon hauling kindling. He became mayor in 1931, built a coalition of immigrant groups that became the foundation of the Democratic machine, and was killed in 1933 in Miami by a bullet that may have been meant for President-elect Franklin Roosevelt. Twenty-second Street was renamed Cermak Road in his honor.

Chicago's Czechs played a part in the creation of their homeland. During the First World War, Czech Americans raised money and recruited volunteers for the cause of Czech independence from Austria-Hungary. In 1918, Tomáš Masaryk, the leader of the independence movement, came to Chicago, and huge crowds turned out to greet him. Later that year, Czechoslovakia declared its independence, and Masaryk became its first president.
=== STOP The Fisk Power Plant & Samuel Insull @ 41.8500,-87.6534
Walk south on Racine Avenue to Cermak Road, and look south, toward the river, at the massive brick power plant with its tall smokestack. This is the Fisk Generating Station, which opened in 1903, and it's one of the most important buildings in the history of electricity.

In 1892, a young Englishman named Samuel Insull, who had been Thomas Edison's private secretary, came to Chicago to run a small electric company. Insull believed that electricity could be cheap enough for everyone, if it were made in huge central plants and sold to as many customers as possible, around the clock. Here, at Fisk Street, he installed what was then the largest steam turbine generator in the world, a new kind of machine that was smaller, cheaper, and more powerful than the old piston steam engines. It worked, and it set the pattern for how electricity has been generated ever since. Insull's company, Commonwealth Edison, came to supply the whole city, and he built an empire of utilities across the Midwest, along with elevated railways and electric interurban lines that ran on his power.

For a time, Insull was one of the most powerful men in America. He built the Civic Opera House on Wacker Drive, a huge office tower with an opera house inside, which opened in 1929. Because from the river it looks like an enormous armchair, people called it Insull's Throne. His wife had been an actress, and he spent lavishly to return her to the stage, and Orson Welles later acknowledged that Insull was one of the models for Citizen Kane, who builds an opera house for his wife, a singer. Then came the Depression. In 1932, Insull's holding companies collapsed, and hundreds of thousands of small investors lost their savings. Insull fled to Europe. He was brought back, tried for fraud, and acquitted, but he died nearly penniless in Paris in 1938.

The Fisk plant burned coal for more than a century. By the 2000s, it was one of the oldest coal plants in the country, and Pilsen residents, together with their neighbors in Little Village, where another old coal plant stood, organized a long campaign against the pollution, which they blamed for asthma and other illnesses. In 2012, both plants shut down. It was one of the great victories of the environmental justice movement in Chicago.
=== STOP Benito Juárez High School @ 41.8533,-87.6633
Walk west along Cermak Road toward Ashland Avenue. The large school building here is Benito Juárez Community Academy, a public high school.

It exists because of a fight. In the early 1970s, Pilsen had no high school of its own, and students had to go to an overcrowded school far away, where many of them dropped out. In 1973, students and parents organized protests, staged walkouts, and demanded a high school for the neighborhood. After years of pressure, the city agreed. Benito Juárez High School opened in 1977, named for the nineteenth-century Mexican president who was a hero of reform.

Look at the building, with its open plaza and the murals around the campus. The school became a symbol of what Pilsen's community organizing could achieve, and many of the activists who fought for it went on to careers in politics and public service. The neighborhood's activists also fought successfully for a health clinic, a library, and community housing.

Mexican Chicago is far older than many people realize. Mexican workers arrived in the 1910s and 1920s, recruited by the railroads, the steel mills of South Chicago, and the meatpackers, and settled near the yards and the mills. During the Depression, many were pressured or forced to leave, in deportation and so-called repatriation drives that also swept up American citizens. After the Second World War, Mexican immigrants came back in growing numbers, and today Chicago has one of the largest Mexican American communities in the United States, centered here in Pilsen and in Little Village, just to the southwest, whose main street, 26th Street, is one of the busiest shopping streets in the city.

Benito Juárez, for whom the school is named, was a Zapotec Indian from the mountains of Oaxaca who became president of Mexico in 1858 and led the country's resistance to a French invasion in the 1860s. He's remembered for a line that's inscribed on monuments across Mexico: "Among individuals, as among nations, respect for the rights of others is peace."
=== STOP The 18th Street Station Murals @ 41.8579,-87.6692
Walk north on Paulina Street back to 18th Street, to the elevated Pink Line station at 18th Street. Walk up into the station if you can.

The station's walls are covered with brightly colored murals, painted by local artists, beginning in the 1990s. You'll see images of Mexican history, Aztec gods, the Virgin of Guadalupe, and portraits of Pilsen's people.

Pilsen is one of the great outdoor mural galleries of the United States. The mural movement here began in the 1970s, inspired by the great Mexican muralists of the early twentieth century, Diego Rivera, José Clemente Orozco, and David Alfaro Siqueiros, who painted murals as public art meant for working people. Chicago artists like Ray Patlán, Marcos Raya, and many others followed their example, painting walls that told the stories of the community. Look for murals on the sides of buildings along 18th Street, 16th Street, and the railroad embankment along 16th Street, which is covered for blocks with murals.

As you walk, look for newer murals too. Some of them address gentrification and displacement, a sign that Pilsen's artists still use walls to speak out.

Look north along Paulina Street toward 17th Street, and you'll see the two tall towers of Saint Adalbert Church, built in 1914 for a Polish congregation, in the grand Renaissance style of the Polish cathedral churches built across Chicago in those years. For a century, it was one of the landmarks of the Lower West Side, with towers about a hundred and eighty-five feet tall. The archdiocese closed it in 2019, despite years of protests by parishioners, and its future has been uncertain. Like the Czechs, the Poles of Pilsen have mostly moved away, and their churches tell the story of the neighborhood's changes.

For the artists of the 1970s, the murals were a way to turn the neighborhood's walls into its history books, telling the stories of people who rarely saw themselves in the official ones. Walk the side streets, and you'll see that tradition alive: murals of Aztec warriors, farmworkers, immigrants crossing the border, famous Mexican artists like Frida Kahlo, and neighborhood heroes whose names you won't find in any textbook.
=== STOP National Museum of Mexican Art @ 41.8561,-87.6729
Walk west to Wood Street and south to 19th Street, to Harrison Park. Here, in a building that was once a park boathouse, is the National Museum of Mexican Art.

The museum was founded in 1982 by a group of teachers, led by Carlos Tortolero, who wanted a place to show Mexican art and culture in the neighborhood. It opened in 1987. Its collection now numbers in the thousands of objects, covering ancient Mexican art, colonial art, the great twentieth-century painters and printmakers, folk art, and contemporary Mexican and Mexican-American art. It's one of the largest Latino arts institutions in the country, and admission is free.

The museum is especially famous for its Day of the Dead exhibition each fall, which fills the galleries with altars, or ofrendas, built by artists and community members to honor the dead.

The museum began in a time when Mexican art was rarely shown in American museums, and its founders were determined that it would be in the neighborhood, free, and open to everyone. Its galleries tell the story of what its founders call Mexican culture without borders, the idea that the culture of Mexico doesn't stop at the border, but continues in Chicago, in Texas, in California, and wherever Mexican people live. It's a fitting idea for a neighborhood that has been home to so many people from somewhere else.

This is the end of the Pilsen tour. If you're hungry, 18th Street is lined with taquerias, Mexican bakeries selling sweet breads called pan dulce, and other restaurants.
