id: chinatown
title: Chinatown
area: South Side
blurb: A Chinatown that moved south a century ago: the gate, the On Leong building, the Chinese American Museum, the Nine Dragon Wall, and a riverside park built by a neighborhood that refused to be squeezed out.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour explores Chicago's Chinatown, centered on Wentworth Avenue and Cermak Road, about two miles south of the Loop.

Chinese immigrants began arriving in Chicago in the 1870s, many of them moving east from California, where anti-Chinese violence and discrimination had grown fierce. In 1882, Congress passed the Chinese Exclusion Act, which banned almost all immigration from China and barred Chinese immigrants from becoming citizens. It was the first major American law to bar immigrants by nationality, and it stayed in force until 1943. The Chinese community in America remained small, largely male, and isolated, and it relied on its own institutions for support.

Chicago's first Chinatown grew up downtown, around Clark Street and Van Buren Street, with laundries, groceries, and restaurants. In the early 1900s, rising rents and pressure from landlords pushed the community to look elsewhere. Around 1912, the On Leong Merchants Association, one of the most powerful organizations in Chinese Chicago, began leasing buildings here, around Wentworth and 22nd Street, which is now Cermak Road. Much of the community followed, and this became Chicago's Chinatown.

For decades, Chinatown was hemmed in by railroads, the river, expressways, and other neighborhoods, and it could barely grow. After immigration laws were reformed in 1965, new waves of immigrants arrived, from Hong Kong, Taiwan, mainland China, and Southeast Asia. Chinatown expanded north onto old railroad land, with a shopping plaza and a riverside park, and its residents spread into neighboring Bridgeport and beyond.

Today, Chinatown is one of the few Chinatowns in America whose population is still growing. We'll start at the Chinatown Gate on Wentworth Avenue, walk south along Wentworth, and then north to the river and Ping Tom Memorial Park.
=== STOP The Chinatown Gate & the On Leong Building @ 41.8524,-87.6320
You're at the Chinatown Gate, which spans Wentworth Avenue just south of Cermak Road. It was built in 1975 and designed by a local architect, Peter Fung. Look at the green tiles of its roof, its red columns, and the golden characters in the center.

Those four characters read Tian Xia Wei Gong, a phrase from classical Chinese philosophy, made famous by Sun Yat-sen, the founder of the Republic of China. It's often translated as "the world belongs to all," or "what is under heaven is for all." Sun Yat-sen visited Chicago while organizing support for the revolution that overthrew China's last emperor in 1911, and Chinese communities in America were an important source of money for his movement.

Look south down Wentworth Avenue. This is the old main street of Chinatown, lined with restaurants, bakeries, tea shops, herbal medicine stores, and gift shops. Look for the street signs, which are in both English and Chinese.

As you walk, notice the many family associations and benevolent associations, often in second-floor offices with their names in Chinese characters over the doors. For generations, these associations were the backbone of Chinese immigrant life, helping new arrivals find work and housing, settling disputes, and caring for the elderly and the dead.

Walk a few steps south on Wentworth to number 2216, the largest and most striking building on the street. It's covered in ornate terra cotta, with green and red tiles and two pagoda-like towers on the corners.

This is the On Leong Merchants Association Building, completed in 1928. It was designed by two Norwegian-American architects, Christian Michaelsen and Sigurd Rognstad, who drew on Chinese motifs to give it its distinctive look. Look for the dragons, lions, and floral patterns in the terra cotta.

For decades, it was nicknamed Chinatown City Hall. It housed the On Leong association's offices, a school, a meeting hall, and a temple. The association acted as a kind of local government, resolving disputes, collecting dues, and representing the community. Like some other Chinese associations of the era, the On Leong was also involved in gambling, and in the late 1980s, after a federal investigation, the association lost the building.

Today, the building is called the Pui Tak Center, and it's owned by a Chinese Christian church. It offers English classes, programs for immigrants, and community events. It's one of the most important buildings in Chinatown, a city landmark, and a reminder of how the community took care of itself in the years of exclusion.

Chinatown's biggest celebration is the Lunar New Year, in late January or February, when a parade with lion and dragon dancers, drums, and firecrackers winds down Wentworth Avenue. The lion dancers stop at shops and restaurants, where owners hang lettuce and red envelopes of money above their doors for the lion to eat, for good luck in the new year. In summer, Chinatown holds a street fair along Wentworth, and in the fall there's the Moon Festival.
=== STOP Chinese American Museum of Chicago @ 41.8512,-87.6335
Walk south on Wentworth to 23rd Street, and then west to number 238 West 23rd Street. This old brick warehouse is the Chinese American Museum of Chicago.

The museum opened in 2005, in a building that was once a warehouse for a Chinese grocery business. In 2008, a fire badly damaged the building and destroyed part of the collection. The community raised money to restore it, and it reopened in 2010.

The museum tells the story of Chinese immigrants in the Midwest. You'll learn about the laundries and restaurants that were among the few businesses open to Chinese immigrants during the exclusion era, about the paper sons, immigrants who entered the country using false identities as the sons of Chinese American citizens, and about the community's long fight against discrimination.

The museum also has exhibits about the Chinese immigrants who worked on the transcontinental railroad, and about the people of Chinatown, past and present. It's small, but it's well worth a visit.

When the Chinese community moved here a century ago, it moved into an Italian neighborhood. The streets around Wentworth were home to Italian immigrants, and some of the churches were theirs. Just south of here, on Alexander Street, Saint Therese Chinese Catholic Church occupies a building that was once an Italian parish. Over the decades, as Italian families moved away, the parish was taken over by Chinese Catholics, and today it serves Chinatown, with masses in Cantonese, Mandarin, and English. Chinatown's population has changed too. For most of its history it was overwhelmingly Cantonese, from the area around Guangzhou and Hong Kong. Since the 1980s, more immigrants have come from mainland China, and you'll hear Mandarin and other dialects on the street as often as Cantonese.

Chinatown's history was shaped by the law. Under the Chinese Exclusion Act, most Chinese men in America couldn't bring their wives and children from China, and the community remained overwhelmingly male for decades. Many men spent their whole working lives in laundries and restaurants, sending money home to families they rarely saw. Only after the act was repealed in 1943, and especially after immigration reform in 1965, did Chinatown become a neighborhood of families, which is what you see today.
=== STOP The Nine Dragon Wall, the Library & Chinatown Square @ 41.8538,-87.6328
Walk back north to Cermak Road. Near the Cermak-Chinatown station on the Red Line, look for a long wall covered in colorful glazed tiles, with dragons twisting across it. This is the Nine Dragon Wall, built in 2003.

It's modeled on a famous wall in Beihai Park in Beijing, built in the eighteenth century, during the Qing dynasty. Nine dragon walls are a traditional feature of imperial Chinese architecture. In Chinese tradition, the dragon is a symbol of power, strength, and good fortune, and the number nine is associated with the emperor. Screen walls like this one were traditionally placed in front of gates, and folk belief held that evil spirits can only travel in straight lines, so a wall in the way keeps them out. Look closely at the nine large dragons, each in a different color, and at the many smaller dragons hidden throughout the wall.

Now cross Cermak Road to the north. On the corner of Wentworth and Archer Avenue stands the Chinatown branch of the Chicago Public Library, which opened in 2015. It was designed by the architecture firm Skidmore, Owings and Merrill as a rounded triangle of glass, wrapped in vertical fins, so that it has no front or back. Inside, a two-story atrium lets in daylight from above. The old library was in a cramped storefront, and the new one became one of the busiest branches in the city, full of students, retirees reading Chinese newspapers, and families. It's a good place to see everyday Chinatown.

Just west of the library is Chinatown Square, a two-level outdoor shopping plaza that opened in 1993, on land that was once a railroad yard. Look for the twelve bronze statues of the animals of the Chinese zodiac: the rat, ox, tiger, rabbit, dragon, snake, horse, goat, monkey, rooster, dog, and pig. See if you can find the animal for your birth year. The restaurants and bakeries here, and along Archer Avenue, are where many Chicagoans come for dim sum.

As you walk, look up at the elevated tracks of the Red Line and the expressways at the edges of the neighborhood. In the 1950s and 1960s, the Dan Ryan and Stevenson expressways were built along Chinatown's edges, taking homes and businesses and walling the neighborhood in even more tightly. That's part of why the purchase of the railroad land to the north, in the 1980s, mattered so much: it was the first room the neighborhood had found to grow in decades.
=== STOP Ping Tom Memorial Park @ 41.8585,-87.6328
Walk north along Wentworth Avenue or through the residential streets, past the townhouses built on more former railroad land, to Ping Tom Memorial Park, along the South Branch of the Chicago River. This park opened in 1999, on another old railroad yard. It's named for Ping Tom, a Chinatown businessman and civic leader, who led the effort to buy the railroad land that allowed Chinatown to expand north, to build Chinatown Square, and to create this park. He died in 1995, before it opened.

The park has a Chinese-style pavilion, a boathouse where you can rent kayaks, and a dock for the water taxi, which runs downtown in the warmer months. Look north, and you'll see a dramatic view of the skyline, framed by the old railroad lift bridge that crosses the river here. The whole span of that bridge rises straight up between its two towers to let boats pass. It's one of the best views of the city.

Every June, the river in front of the park fills with long, narrow dragon boats, paddled by teams of twenty and steered to the beat of a drummer in the bow, for the Chicago Dragon Boat Race. Dragon boat racing comes from a two-thousand-year-old Chinese festival, and the race here draws teams from all over the region.

Look at the park's boathouse, a low building whose roof rises and falls in a series of peaks, like waves. It was designed by the Chicago architect Jeanne Gang, whose firm also designed the rippling Aqua tower downtown, and it opened in 2013.

Chinatown has also found its political voice. For decades, the neighborhood was split among several city council wards, and its residents had little say at City Hall. When the ward map was redrawn in 2022, Chinatown and its neighbors were united in a single ward, the eleventh, the old political home of the Daleys in Bridgeport, which became the first ward in the city's history with an Asian American majority. In 2023, Nicole Lee became the first Chinese American ever to serve on the Chicago City Council.

For Chinatown, the park is a symbol of how a neighborhood that was once squeezed in on all sides found room to grow. This is the end of the Chinatown tour. If you want, you can take the water taxi back downtown.
