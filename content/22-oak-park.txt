=== TOUR
id: oak-park
title: Oak Park: Wright & Hemingway
area: West suburbs
blurb: The suburb where Frank Lloyd Wright invented the Prairie house and Ernest Hemingway grew up: Unity Temple, the Hemingway houses, Wright's own home and studio, Forest Avenue, and the house behind a scandal and a tragedy.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour leaves the city limits for Oak Park, the suburb just west of Chicago, at the end of the Green Line. It's worth the trip. In a few blocks, you'll see more buildings by Frank Lloyd Wright than anywhere else in the world, and the streets where Ernest Hemingway grew up.

Oak Park was settled in the 1830s and grew after the Civil War into a commuter suburb, linked to downtown Chicago by railroad. After the Great Fire of 1871, many Chicagoans moved out here. It was a village of churches, schools, and wide lawns, and it took its respectability seriously. Oak Park banned saloons, and it stayed officially dry until 1973. People sometimes called it Saints' Rest.

In 1889, a young architect named Frank Lloyd Wright, just twenty-one years old and working as a draftsman for Adler and Sullivan downtown, married a young woman named Catherine Tobin and built a small house for his family here, on Chicago Avenue. Over the next twenty years, working from a studio attached to that house, Wright and the talented group of architects around him developed a new kind of American house. It was long and low, hugging the ground, with broad overhanging roofs, bands of windows, and open rooms flowing around a central fireplace. It came to be called the Prairie style, after the flat landscape of the Midwest. Wright designed dozens of buildings in Oak Park and the neighboring village of River Forest, and many of them still stand.

A few blocks away, in 1899, Ernest Hemingway was born. He grew up here, went to Oak Park and River Forest High School, and left at eighteen to work as a reporter in Kansas City, and then as a volunteer ambulance driver in Italy in the First World War. He rarely wrote about Oak Park, but it shaped him.

The two men overlapped here for about ten years, Wright at the height of his early career and Hemingway as a boy. As far as anyone knows, they never had anything to do with each other.

Oak Park has other famous children. Ray Kroc, who built McDonald's into a worldwide empire, was born here in 1902. Edgar Rice Burroughs, the creator of Tarzan, lived here in the 1910s. And in the late 1960s and 1970s, as many white suburbs resisted Black families moving in, Oak Park did something unusual: it passed a fair housing law and worked deliberately to become a racially integrated community, and it largely succeeded.

We'll start at Unity Temple, near the Oak Park stop on the Green Line, walk north past the Hemingway houses to Wright's home and studio, then down Forest Avenue, and end at the house of Edwin and Mamah Cheney.
=== STOP Unity Temple @ 41.8884,-87.7966
You're on Lake Street at Kenilworth Avenue, in front of a building that looks like a fortress of gray concrete blocks. This is Unity Temple, a Unitarian Universalist church designed by Frank Lloyd Wright and completed in 1908.

In 1905, the congregation's old wooden church was struck by lightning and burned. Wright, whose family attended the church, got the commission. The congregation had very little money, so Wright chose a material that was cheap but unusual for a church: poured concrete. He designed the building so that the same wooden forms could be used over and over, with the concrete poured into them on site, and he left the concrete exposed, with a pebbly surface. It was one of the first major public buildings in America made this way.

Look at how the building is divided into two parts, connected by a low entrance hall: the temple for worship on one side, and Unity House, for social gatherings, on the other. And look for the entrance. It's hidden. You don't walk straight in from the street, as you would at most churches. You go around to the side, through a low, dim passage, and then up into the worship space.

If you can go inside, the effect is extraordinary. The square sanctuary is full of light, which comes from clerestory windows high on the walls and from a ceiling of amber-colored art glass skylights. The pews are arranged on three levels around the pulpit, so that no one in the congregation sits far from the minister. Wright called Unity Temple his contribution to modern architecture.

The building was carefully restored, and in 2019, it was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site, along with seven other Wright buildings, including the Robie House in Hyde Park and Fallingwater in Pennsylvania.

Before you go in, look above the entrance for the inscription, which sums up Wright's idea of what a liberal church should be: "For the worship of God and the service of man." Wright later said that the real substance of a building was not its walls and roof, but the space inside them, and Unity Temple was the first building where he really proved it.
=== STOP Hemingway's Birthplace @ 41.8928,-87.7950
Walk east to Oak Park Avenue and north to number 339, a large Queen Anne house with a wraparound porch and a round corner tower. Ernest Hemingway was born here on July 21st, 1899, in an upstairs bedroom.

The house belonged to his mother's father, Ernest Hall, a prosperous English-born businessman, after whom Hemingway was named. Hemingway's father, Clarence, was a doctor, and his mother, Grace, was a singer and music teacher who had trained for an opera career. Ernest spent his first six years in this house. Then his grandfather died, and the family built a new house a few blocks away, at 600 North Kenilworth Avenue, designed partly by his mother, with a music room for her lessons.

Hemingway went to Oak Park and River Forest High School, where he wrote for the school newspaper and literary magazine, played football, and boxed. Summers were spent at the family's cottage in northern Michigan, where his father taught him to hunt and fish, and those woods and lakes appear in many of his early stories.

He left Oak Park after high school. His relationship with his parents was difficult, especially with his mother, and after his father took his own life in 1928, it grew worse.

You'll often hear that Hemingway called Oak Park a place of wide lawns and narrow minds. It's one of the most quoted lines about any American suburb. But no one has ever found it in his writing or letters, and scholars now think it was made up long after his death. It's a good example of how a quotation can become a legend.

The house is now a museum, restored to how it looked when he was a boy.

In high school, Hemingway wrote humorous columns for the school paper, the Trapeze, in imitation of Ring Lardner, the Chicago sportswriter. In 1918, as a volunteer ambulance driver for the Red Cross in Italy, he was badly wounded by an Austrian mortar shell while handing out chocolate and cigarettes to soldiers at the front. He was eighteen. He came home to Oak Park in January 1919, with a limp and a medal, and spoke about the war at an assembly at his old high school. The experience became the basis of his novel A Farewell to Arms.
=== STOP Frank Lloyd Wright Home and Studio @ 41.8941,-87.7994
Walk north on Oak Park Avenue to Chicago Avenue, and then west to the corner of Forest Avenue. The shingled house with the big triangular gable facing Forest Avenue is the home Frank Lloyd Wright built for his family in 1889. The studio, added in 1898, faces Chicago Avenue.

Wright paid for the house with a loan from his employer, Louis Sullivan, to be paid back out of his salary. The family grew to six children, and Wright kept remodeling the house, adding a barrel-vaulted playroom upstairs for the children, one of the most delightful rooms he ever designed.

To earn more money, Wright took on private commissions of his own, designing houses on the side, despite his contract with Sullivan. He later called them his bootleg houses. When Sullivan found out, in 1893, the two parted bitterly, and Wright set up on his own.

The studio became one of the most creative architectural offices in history. Look at the entrance on Chicago Avenue, with its columns topped with sculptures of storks, symbols of wisdom and fertility, and the octagonal drafting room. Here, Wright and a remarkable group of colleagues worked out the Prairie style. Among them was Marion Mahony, one of the first women licensed as an architect in the United States, whose beautiful drawings of Wright's buildings made them famous in Europe. Others, like Walter Burley Griffin, whom she later married, went on to careers of their own. Together, they would design the city plan of Canberra, the capital of Australia.

In 1909, Wright left his wife and children and his practice and went to Europe with Mamah Borthwick Cheney, the wife of a client. The scandal was enormous.

The house and studio are now a museum, run by the Frank Lloyd Wright Trust, with tours inside.

If you take the tour, look for the children's playroom, with its high barrel-vaulted ceiling, a skylight of art glass, and a mural over the fireplace showing a scene from the Arabian Nights, the story of the fisherman and the genie. And look for the passageway between the house and the studio, which Wright built around a willow tree, letting it grow up through the roof.

Before you head down Forest Avenue, walk a few doors west along Chicago Avenue, and you'll pass three of the bootleg houses that cost Wright his job with Sullivan: the Parker House, the Thomas Gale House, and the Walter Gale House, all designed in 1892 and 1893. They're fairly conventional houses, with their towers and steep roofs, but if you look closely, you'll see hints of the architect he was about to become.
=== STOP Forest Avenue: the Heurtley and Moore Houses @ 41.8928,-87.7996
Walk south on Forest Avenue. You're now on one of the most remarkable residential streets in America, lined with houses by Wright and his contemporaries.

On the east side, at number 318, is the Arthur Heurtley House, completed in 1902. Heurtley was a banker. Look at how the house hugs the ground under its wide, low roof. The brick walls are laid in alternating bands that project and recede, giving the whole house horizontal stripes of light and shadow. The main living rooms are on the upper floor, raised above the street for privacy and views, with a continuous band of windows under the eaves. Look at the great round arch of the entrance, half hidden in the brick wall. It's a Prairie house in its first full form.

Across the street, at number 333, is something very different: the Moore-Dugal House, a big, half-timbered house that looks like a Tudor manor in an English village. It's also by Wright. His client, Nathan Moore, a lawyer and neighbor, wanted something traditional, and the young Wright, who needed the work, gave him what he wanted in 1895. Wright is said to have avoided walking past it for years afterward, though he took care with every detail. After a fire in 1922, Wright redesigned the upper floors, and the house shows a mix of his early and late styles.

Standing between these two houses, you can see how fast Wright's thinking changed in just seven years. Many of the houses along this street, by other architects, also show the influence of the Prairie style. Wright wasn't the only one working in it, and Oak Park's neighbors often copied his ideas.
=== STOP The Frank Thomas House @ 41.8907,-87.7998
Continue south on Forest Avenue to number 210, a long, low house of cream stucco, with a dark wood trim and an arched entrance hidden at the side. This is the Frank Thomas House, completed in 1901, often called the first Prairie-style house built in Oak Park.

Look at how Wright has raised the main floor above the ground, on a high base, and how the low, hipped roofs stretch out in every direction. The entrance is tucked away, reached by walking along the side of the house and through an arch, which is a typical Wright touch: he liked to make you search for the way in, and to compress you in a low space before releasing you into a larger one.

Look also at the art glass windows, in geometric patterns. Wright designed these windows as screens, not pictures. They let in light and let you look out, while keeping people outside from seeing in.

Many of Wright's Oak Park clients were businessmen, not artists. They were often self-made, practical people who liked the idea of a modern house for a modern family, without the clutter of the Victorian age. Wright offered them open plans, built-in furniture, and simple, natural materials. In return, he demanded control of almost everything, from the plan of the garden down to the furniture and the light fixtures.

As you walk back north along Forest Avenue, you'll pass other Wright houses, including the Hills-DeCaro House, at number 313, which Wright remodeled for Nathan Moore's daughter and her husband, and several houses by his contemporaries. Many of them are private homes, so please enjoy them from the sidewalk.
=== STOP The Cheney House @ 41.8949,-87.7898
Finally, walk east along Chicago Avenue to East Avenue, and north to number 520. Behind a low brick wall and a hedge, you'll see a one-story brick house, with a broad, hipped roof and a band of windows. This is the house Wright designed in 1903 for Edwin Cheney, an electrical engineer, and his wife, Mamah Borthwick Cheney.

Mamah Cheney was a highly educated woman, a translator and a feminist, and she and Wright fell in love. In 1909, they left their spouses and children and went to Europe together, to the horror of Oak Park and the delight of the newspapers. When they returned, Wright built a house and studio for them in Wisconsin, in the valley where his mother's family had settled, and called it Taliesin.

In August 1914, while Wright was in Chicago, a man who worked at Taliesin set fire to the house and attacked the people inside with an axe. Mamah, her two children, who were visiting, and four other people were killed. It was one of the most horrifying crimes of the era, and Wright was devastated. He rebuilt Taliesin, and he lived and worked for many more decades, designing Fallingwater and the Guggenheim Museum in New York, among hundreds of other buildings. He died in 1959, at ninety-one.

The Cheney House is a quiet, inward-looking building, designed so that the main rooms are hidden from the street, behind the wall. It seems fitting for a house whose story became so public.

Mamah Cheney is often remembered only as Wright's lover, but she was a serious intellectual in her own right. While she lived with Wright in Europe and in Wisconsin, she translated the work of the Swedish feminist writer Ellen Key, whose ideas about love, marriage, and women's freedom shaped how she and Wright explained their choices to a hostile public.

This is the end of the Oak Park tour. The Oak Park station on the Green Line, at Oak Park Avenue and South Boulevard, will take you back to downtown Chicago.
