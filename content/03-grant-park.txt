title: Grant Park & the Museum Campus
area: Downtown
blurb: Chicago's front yard: the Tiffany dome of the old library, the Bean and Millennium Park, the Art Institute's lions, Buckingham Fountain, the hotel where the whole world watched in 1968, election night 2008, Soldier Field, and the Field, Shedd and Adler on the lake.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour explores Grant Park, the great open space between the Loop and Lake Michigan, and the Museum Campus at its southern end. Chicagoans call Grant Park the city's front yard.

When Chicago was laid out in the 1830s, the land east of Michigan Avenue was marked on the map as public ground, "forever to remain vacant of buildings." In the nineteenth century, it was a narrow strip along the shore. The Illinois Central Railroad built its tracks on a trestle in the lake just offshore, and after the Great Fire of 1871, rubble from the burned city was dumped into the water between the shore and the tracks. Landfill extended the park farther and farther into the lake.

For decades, the city wanted to put buildings on this land: an armory, a post office, a police station, exhibition halls, and the Field Museum. One man stood in the way. Aaron Montgomery Ward, whose mail-order company had made him rich, looked out at the lakefront from his office on Michigan Avenue, and he wanted it kept open. Beginning in the 1890s, he sued the city again and again, insisting on the original promise. He won. The courts ruled that the lakefront must remain open, and that principle has protected Chicago's lakefront ever since. The Art Institute, already built, was allowed to stay, but almost everything else went elsewhere.

Daniel Burnham's Plan of Chicago of 1909 imagined the lakefront as a great formal park, lined with museums at its edges. The park was renamed for President Ulysses S. Grant in 1901, and over the following decades, it was laid out with formal gardens, fountains, and broad lawns. Burnham is often quoted as saying, "Make no little plans. They have no magic to stir men's blood." He may never have said those exact words, but the phrase has become Chicago's motto.

Grant Park has been the stage for some of the most dramatic moments in the city's history, from the riots during the 1968 Democratic National Convention to Barack Obama's victory celebration in 2008. We'll start at the Chicago Cultural Center, across Michigan Avenue from Millennium Park, walk south past the Art Institute and Buckingham Fountain to the hotel where the whole world was watching in 1968, cross the lawn where Chicago celebrated in 2008, and end at the Museum Campus on the lake.
=== STOP Chicago Cultural Center @ 41.8839,-87.6248
You're on Michigan Avenue at Washington Street, in front of a long, gray stone building with classical columns and arched windows. This is the Chicago Cultural Center. It was built in 1897 as Chicago's first central public library, and it's one of the most beautiful buildings in the city. It's free to go inside, and you should.

The library had an unusual beginning. After the Great Fire of 1871, a British author named Thomas Hughes, who had written the popular novel Tom Brown's School Days, organized a gift of books from Britain to help Chicago start over. Thousands of volumes arrived, donated by authors, scholars, and publishers, and by Queen Victoria herself. Chicago had never had a free public library, and these books became the start of its collection. The library opened on New Year's Day of 1873, inside an old iron water tank that had survived the fire, at LaSalle and Adams Streets, on the spot where the Rookery stands today.

A quarter century later, the city built this palace for it. The architects were Shepley, Rutan and Coolidge, of Boston, who also designed the Art Institute, just down the avenue. The library shared the building with a memorial hall for the Grand Army of the Republic, the organization of Union veterans of the Civil War, whose members met in the rooms on the Randolph Street side.

Inside, climb the grand staircase of white Carrara marble, inlaid with mosaics of colored glass, mother of pearl, and polished stone, and lettered with quotations about books and learning. At the top, on the third floor, is Preston Bradley Hall, the old circulation room, under a stained glass dome thirty-eight feet across, made by the studios of Louis Comfort Tiffany. It's often called the largest Tiffany glass dome in the world. A restoration in 2008 let natural light shine through it again, for the first time in decades. On the other side of the building, the old memorial hall has a second glass dome, in a Renaissance pattern, made by a Chicago firm, Healy and Millet.

In the 1970s, the library moved out, and the building became a free cultural center, with art exhibitions, lectures, and concerts. On Wednesdays at lunchtime, there's a free classical concert, named for the British pianist Myra Hess, who played concerts in London's National Gallery during the bombing of the Second World War. And on the ground floor, you'll find the city's visitor center.
=== STOP Millennium Park & Cloud Gate @ 41.8827,-87.6234
You're in Millennium Park, at the northwest corner of Grant Park, near Michigan Avenue and Washington Street. Until the late 1990s, this was an ugly corner of the park, covered with railroad tracks and surface parking lots. The city decked over the tracks and built a park on top of a parking garage. It opened in 2004, years late and far over budget, but it became one of the most popular attractions in the Midwest.

The star is Cloud Gate, a sculpture by the British artist Anish Kapoor, which everyone calls the Bean. It's made of more than a hundred and sixty stainless steel plates, welded together and polished so smooth that the seams are invisible. Walk around it and under its arch, called the omphalos, or navel, and look at the reflections of the skyline and the people, warped and stretched.

To the north is the Jay Pritzker Pavilion, an outdoor concert stage designed by Frank Gehry, with its explosion of curling stainless steel. Over the lawn stretches a trellis of steel pipes carrying the sound system, so the music sounds clear everywhere on the lawn. Summer concerts here are free.

To the south is the Crown Fountain, by the Spanish artist Jaume Plensa: two glass block towers, fifty feet tall, facing each other across a shallow pool. They show video portraits of about a thousand Chicagoans, who purse their lips, and then water spouts from their mouths, like the gargoyles of old fountains. In summer, kids play in the water.

Millennium Park ended up costing several times its original budget, and private donors paid for much of it, which is why nearly every part of it carries a donor's name. Look for the Lurie Garden, south of the Pritzker Pavilion, a garden of prairie plants and perennials, enclosed on two sides by a tall hedge meant to evoke Carl Sandburg's description of Chicago as the City of the Big Shoulders. And east of the pavilion, look for the BP Bridge, a winding walkway designed by Frank Gehry and clad in stainless steel, which snakes over Columbus Drive toward Maggie Daley Park, a park full of playgrounds and climbing walls named for the wife of Mayor Richard M. Daley.

Under your feet, beneath the gardens and the garage, the Metra Electric trains still run into Millennium Station, along the same route the Illinois Central took along the lakefront in the 1850s.

When Cloud Gate was first unveiled in 2004, it wasn't finished. The seams between its plates were still visible, and it was covered up again while workers spent months grinding and polishing them away. Kapoor said he was inspired by liquid mercury. He reportedly wasn't fond of the nickname "the Bean," but the name stuck before the sculpture was even complete.
=== STOP The Art Institute of Chicago @ 41.8796,-87.6237
Walk south along Michigan Avenue to the entrance of the Art Institute, guarded by two bronze lions. The lions were sculpted by Edward Kemeys and installed in 1894. The one on the south is said to be standing in an attitude of defiance, and the one on the north to be on the prowl. When the city's teams win championships, the lions sometimes wear giant helmets or caps, and in December they wear wreaths.

The building was built for the World's Columbian Exposition of 1893, as a hall for a series of conferences held during the fair, including the World's Parliament of Religions, one of the first great meetings of the world's religious traditions. After the fair, it became the Art Institute's home.

The Art Institute is one of the great art museums of the world. Thanks partly to Bertha Palmer and other Chicago collectors, it has one of the finest collections of Impressionist and Post-Impressionist paintings outside Paris, including Georges Seurat's huge painting, A Sunday on La Grande Jatte. It also has two of the most famous American paintings: Grant Wood's American Gothic, which won a prize here in 1930, and Edward Hopper's Nighthawks, bought by the museum in 1942, the year it was painted.

Now look at the street sign at the corner of Adams Street and Michigan Avenue. It says "Begin Historic Route 66." This was the eastern starting point of the famous highway, which ran more than two thousand miles from Chicago to Santa Monica, California, and became a symbol of the open road.

If you go inside, look for two things that tell Chicago's own story. One is the Thorne Miniature Rooms, sixty-eight tiny rooms built at a scale of one inch to one foot, showing interiors from European and American history in astonishing detail. They were commissioned in the 1930s by Narcissa Niblack Thorne, whose husband was an heir to the Montgomery Ward fortune. The other is the trading room of the old Chicago Stock Exchange, designed by Adler and Sullivan in 1894, with its stenciled walls and art glass. When the Stock Exchange building on LaSalle Street was demolished in 1972, the photographer and preservationist Richard Nickel was killed when part of the building collapsed on him while he was trying to salvage its ornament. The trading room was rebuilt inside the museum, and the building's great arched entrance now stands outside, on the museum's east side, facing Columbus Drive.

The museum has grown in stages, eastward over the railroad tracks behind it. Its newest addition, the Modern Wing, designed by Renzo Piano, opened in 2009, with a footbridge across Monroe Street to Millennium Park.

And if the gallery with the big Seurat looks familiar, you may have seen it in the 1986 movie Ferris Bueller's Day Off, in which Ferris and his friends skip school and spend part of the day here. The director, John Hughes, said the Art Institute had been a refuge for him when he was young.
=== STOP Buckingham Fountain @ 41.8758,-87.6189
Walk south and east through the gardens to Buckingham Fountain, the centerpiece of Grant Park.

It was dedicated in 1927, a gift from Kate Buckingham, a wealthy art collector, in memory of her brother Clarence, who had been a director of the Art Institute. The design was inspired by the Latona Fountain at the Palace of Versailles, but at roughly twice its size. It's one of the largest fountains in the world.

The fountain is built of pink Georgia marble, in three tiers, like a wedding cake. Look at the four pairs of bronze sea horses around the base. They represent the four states that border Lake Michigan: Illinois, Wisconsin, Michigan, and Indiana. The basin itself represents the lake.

From spring to fall, the fountain puts on a water show every hour, with the central jet shooting up to about a hundred and fifty feet. At night, there's a light and music show.

Look back toward the city from here. The view of the skyline from Buckingham Fountain is one of the most famous in Chicago, and you may recognize it from the opening credits of the television show Married with Children.

The fountain was designed by Edward Bennett, who had worked with Daniel Burnham on the Plan of Chicago, and its sea horses were made by a French sculptor, Marcel Loyau. Kate Buckingham didn't just pay for the fountain. She also set up a fund to pay for its upkeep, worried that the city might not take care of it.

Notice how the fountain fits into the plan of the park. It sits on the line of the broad street that runs west from here into the Loop, and it's the centerpiece of a formal composition of gardens, terraces, and promenades in the French style, the kind of grand, ordered landscape that Burnham imagined for the whole lakefront.
=== STOP The Hilton & August 1968 @ 41.8725,-87.6251
Walk west from the fountain toward Michigan Avenue, through Congress Plaza, where two giant bronze horsemen guard the entrance to the park. They're the Bowman and the Spearman, by the Croatian sculptor Ivan Meštrović, installed in 1928. Look closely: the archer has no bow, and the spearman has no spear. The sculptor left the weapons to your imagination.

Then walk south on Michigan Avenue to Balbo Drive. The enormous hotel on the corner is the Hilton Chicago. It opened in 1927 as the Stevens Hotel, and with about three thousand rooms, it was the largest hotel in the world. It had its own hospital, a bowling alley, a theater, and a miniature golf course on the roof. It was built by James Stevens and his son Ernest, whose family also ran an insurance company. The Depression ruined them. The hotel went bankrupt, and Ernest Stevens was charged with embezzlement, though his conviction was overturned. His youngest son, John Paul Stevens, grew up to become a justice of the Supreme Court. During the Second World War, the Army bought the hotel and used it as barracks for thousands of airmen in training, who did their drills in Grant Park. After the war, it was bought by the hotelier Conrad Hilton.

In August 1968, this was the headquarters hotel of the Democratic National Convention. The country was torn apart by the Vietnam War, and Martin Luther King Junior and Robert Kennedy had both been assassinated that spring. Thousands of antiwar protesters came to Chicago, and Mayor Richard J. Daley was determined to keep order, with nearly twelve thousand police officers on duty and thousands of National Guard troops and federal soldiers standing by. All week, the police and the protesters clashed in Lincoln Park and here in Grant Park.

On the night of August twenty-eighth, while the delegates were nominating Vice President Hubert Humphrey at the convention hall near the stockyards, marchers tried to leave Grant Park. At this intersection, right in front of the hotel, the police charged into the crowd, clubbing protesters, reporters, and bystanders. People were shoved through the plate glass windows of the hotel's bar. Television cameras filmed it all under the bright lights at the hotel's entrance, and the footage was broadcast across the country that night, while the convention was still going on. The protesters chanted, "The whole world is watching." Inside the convention hall, Senator Abraham Ribicoff of Connecticut, speaking from the podium, condemned what he called Gestapo tactics on the streets of Chicago, while Daley shouted back at him from the floor. An official investigation later described what happened here as a police riot. Eight protest leaders were charged with conspiracy, and they became known as the Chicago Seven after one defendant's case was separated from the others.

Before you move on, look at the street sign. Balbo Drive is named for Italo Balbo, an Italian aviator, who led a fleet of twenty-four seaplanes from Italy to Chicago in 1933, landing on the lake during the Century of Progress world's fair. The city gave him a hero's welcome and renamed Seventh Street in his honor. But Balbo was also one of the leaders of Mussolini's Fascist party, and there have long been calls to change the name. In 2018, the city renamed the broad street that runs west from Buckingham Fountain, which had been Congress Parkway, for the journalist and anti-lynching crusader Ida B. Wells. It was the first street in downtown Chicago named for a Black woman.
=== STOP Hutchinson Field & Election Night 2008 @ 41.8715,-87.6192
Walk back east along Balbo Drive, across Columbus Drive, to the big lawn south of Buckingham Fountain. This is Hutchinson Field, the largest open lawn in Grant Park.

On the night of November fourth, 2008, a crowd of hundreds of thousands gathered here to hear Barack Obama speak, after he was elected the first Black president of the United States. Obama had come to Chicago in 1985 as a young community organizer on the South Side, and it was here that he'd built his career, as a state senator and then as a United States senator from Illinois. People who were in the crowd remember it as a night of joy, with strangers hugging and weeping. Television cameras caught the civil rights leader Jesse Jackson in the crowd, tears running down his face. "If there is anyone out there who still doubts that America is a place where all things are possible," Obama began, "tonight is your answer."

The contrast with August 1968, a few hundred yards away and forty years earlier, was lost on no one.

Grant Park has gathered huge crowds many times. In October 1979, Pope John Paul II celebrated Mass here, before one of the largest crowds in the city's history. And on November fourth, 2016, eight years to the day after Obama's victory speech, this field held the rally for the Chicago Cubs, who had just won the World Series for the first time in a hundred and eight years. The city estimated that millions of people lined the parade route and filled the park.

Every summer, the field fills up again for Lollapalooza, a rock festival that began in 1991 as a traveling tour, founded by the singer Perry Farrell, and that has settled in Grant Park every summer since 2005, drawing more than a hundred thousand people a day. And the city has offered free outdoor concerts in the park for generations. The Grant Park Music Festival, which puts on free classical concerts all summer, now at the Pritzker Pavilion, began in 1935. It was started by James Petrillo, the powerful head of Chicago's musicians' union, as a way to put musicians to work during the Depression.
=== STOP The Field Museum & Soldier Field @ 41.8663,-87.6170
Walk south through the park, and through the tunnels or along the paths, to the Museum Campus. This area was created in the late 1990s, when the city moved Lake Shore Drive to the west, so that the three museums here could be joined in one landscape.

The white marble Greek temple in front of you is the Field Museum of Natural History. It was founded after the 1893 fair, to keep its natural history collections, and it was named for Marshall Field, Chicago's greatest merchant, who gave a million dollars to start it. The museum moved into this building in 1921.

Field built the department store that bore his name on State Street into one of the great stores of the world, with the motto "Give the lady what she wants." He was, at his death in 1906, one of the richest men in America.

The museum's most famous resident is Sue, the most complete Tyrannosaurus rex skeleton ever found, discovered in South Dakota in 1990 by a fossil hunter named Sue Hendrickson. After a legal battle over who owned it, Sue was sold at auction in 1997 for more than eight million dollars, and the Field Museum bought her with the help of McDonald's and Disney. The museum's collections also include Egyptian mummies, gems, and the famous man-eating lions of Tsavo.

The museum's first home was the Palace of Fine Arts, left over from the 1893 fair in Jackson Park, the building that is now the Museum of Science and Industry. This building was designed by Daniel Burnham's firm and completed after his death. Look at the porches at either end, where the roof is held up by sculpted women, in the manner of the Erechtheion, a temple on the Acropolis in Athens. Marshall Field's nephew Stanley Field ran the museum for fifty-six years, and the great hall inside is named for him. Today, the hall is dominated by the cast skeleton of a titanosaur, one of the largest dinosaurs ever found, and Sue has her own gallery upstairs.

The lions of Tsavo are worth a visit too. In 1898, two lions without manes attacked the workers building a railway bridge over the Tsavo River in Kenya, killing many of them. The engineer in charge, John Henry Patterson, finally shot them, and years later he sold their skins and skulls to the museum. Patterson claimed the lions had killed a hundred and thirty-five people. Scientists who have studied the lions' remains estimate a much smaller number, about thirty-five, but the legend of the man-eaters has never faded.

Now look south. The stadium with the classical colonnades is Soldier Field, which opened in 1924 and was dedicated to the soldiers who had died in the First World War. In 1927, more than a hundred thousand fans watched Gene Tunney beat Jack Dempsey here, in the famous boxing match of the long count. In July 1966, Martin Luther King Junior led a rally here for the Chicago Freedom Movement, his campaign against segregated housing in the city, and then marched with the crowd to City Hall, where he taped a list of demands to the door. Since 1971, Soldier Field has been the home of the Chicago Bears. In 2003, a new stadium was built inside the old colonnades. Many critics hated it, and said it looked as if a spaceship had landed on top of the old stadium. A few years later, Soldier Field lost its status as a National Historic Landmark.
=== STOP Shedd Aquarium, Adler Planetarium & Northerly Island @ 41.8664,-87.6070
Walk east along the lakefront past the Shedd Aquarium, an octagonal marble building that opened in 1930. It was founded with a gift from John G. Shedd, who had worked his way up from stock boy to president of Marshall Field and Company. In its early years, the aquarium brought in seawater by railroad tank car from the ocean.

At the end of the peninsula is the Adler Planetarium, a twelve-sided building with a copper dome, which opened in 1930. It was the first planetarium in the Western Hemisphere, founded by Max Adler, an executive at Sears, Roebuck. From here, you have one of the finest views of the Chicago skyline.

Look south at the long, green island stretching into the lake. This is Northerly Island, the only one built of the chain of islands imagined in Burnham's plan. In 1933 and 1934, it was part of the site of Chicago's second world's fair, the Century of Progress. Later, it became a small airport, Meigs Field. On the night of March thirtieth, 2003, Mayor Richard M. Daley, who wanted a park here, sent bulldozers to carve huge X's into the runway without warning. Planes were stranded at the airport. The city was fined, but the airport never reopened. Today, Northerly Island is a nature park with an outdoor concert venue.

The Century of Progress fair, in 1933 and 1934, celebrated the hundredth anniversary of Chicago's founding as a town, and it was held right here, along the lakefront and on Northerly Island, in the depths of the Depression. Its motto was "Science Finds, Industry Applies, Man Conforms." The fair opened with a stunt that delighted the public. Astronomers aimed telescopes at the star Arcturus, and its light, turned into electric current by photoelectric cells, switched on the fair's lights. That starlight was believed to have left Arcturus about forty years earlier, around the time of Chicago's last great fair, in 1893.

The fair was a hit, and unlike most world's fairs, it paid back its investors. Visitors came to see streamlined trains, model homes made of steel and glass, and a dancer named Sally Rand, who performed with nothing but two large ostrich feather fans and was arrested for it more than once. Several of the fair's Homes of Tomorrow were later moved across the lake to Beverly Shores, Indiana, where you can still see them.

Max Adler, who founded the planetarium, had been a concert violinist before he became an executive at Sears. He was the brother-in-law of Julius Rosenwald, who built Sears into a giant and founded the Museum of Science and Industry. Families like theirs, rich from mail order, meatpacking, and retail, built almost every great cultural institution you've seen on this tour.

This is the end of the Grant Park tour.
