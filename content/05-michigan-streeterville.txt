=== TOUR
id: michigan-ave
title: Michigan Avenue & Streeterville
area: Downtown
blurb: The Magnificent Mile from the river to the lake: the Water Tower, the Hancock, Palmolive and Playboy, Mies on the lakefront, a squatter's empire, and Navy Pier.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour walks north up Michigan Avenue, the stretch Chicagoans call the Magnificent Mile, and then east to the lake through Streeterville and down to Navy Pier.

A hundred years ago, there was no grand avenue here. North of the river, Michigan Avenue was a quiet residential street called Pine Street, lined with houses and churches, and it ended at the river. You crossed by ferry or a narrow swing bridge. The 1909 Plan of Chicago, by Daniel Burnham and Edward Bennett, imagined a broad boulevard leaping the river and connecting the Loop to the north side. The city followed the plan. The Michigan Avenue bridge opened in 1920, the street was widened, and within a decade it was lined with new towers: the Wrigley Building, the Tribune Tower, hotels, athletic clubs, and offices.

The name Magnificent Mile came later, in 1947, from a real estate developer named Arthur Rubloff, who was trying to sell the street's future. It worked. Over the next half century, the avenue filled with department stores, hotels, and vertical shopping malls, and it became one of the great retail streets in the country.

East of the avenue lies Streeterville, which has one of the strangest origin stories of any neighborhood in America. Much of it did not exist in 1880. It was lake. It was built out of landfill, some of it dumped deliberately, some of it rubble, and a man named Cap Streeter claimed that new land as his own country. You'll hear his story near the lake.

Look for three things on this walk. First, the survivors: two stone buildings that made it through the Great Fire of 1871. Second, the Chicago engineering tradition, in the tapered black Hancock tower and in Mies van der Rohe's glass apartments by the lake, which changed how the world built tall residences. And third, the names of companies that made fortunes here: Palmolive soap, Playboy magazine, Wrigley gum, and the department stores of State Street that followed their customers north.

We will start near the corner of Michigan Avenue and Grand Avenue, walk north to Oak Street, cut east to Lake Shore Drive, and then head south through Streeterville and along the lake to Navy Pier.
=== STOP The Magnificent Mile & the Medinah Athletic Club @ 41.8913,-87.6238
You are on Michigan Avenue a few blocks north of the river. Look south and you can see the Wrigley Building and Tribune Tower framing the bridge, and north, the long canyon of shops and hotels that is the Magnificent Mile.

Look for the building at 505 North Michigan, on the east side of the street, with a gold-and-blue onion dome perched on its roof. It is now a hotel, but it was built in 1929 as the Medinah Athletic Club, a private club for the Shriners, the fraternal order known for their red fez hats and their charity hospitals. The Shriners took their imagery from an imagined Middle East, which explains the dome, and the carved reliefs on the facade, and the club's lavish interiors, which included a running track, a bowling alley, and a junior Olympic swimming pool on an upper floor, decorated with tiles in a Spanish style. The champion swimmer and Tarzan actor Johnny Weissmuller is said to have trained in that pool, a story the hotel likes to tell.

The club opened just months before the stock market crash. By the mid-1930s, it had failed. The building has been a hotel ever since.

The dome had a practical idea behind it, too: the club's designers reportedly hoped it could serve as a mooring mast for dirigibles, which in the late 1920s many people believed would become a common way to travel. The same dream was attached to the spire of the Empire State Building in New York. No airship ever tied up at either one.

As you walk north, notice how the avenue is lined with planters and flowerbeds in the median and along the sidewalks. They are replanted every season, with tulips in spring and kale and grasses in fall, and they are part of a deliberate effort, since the 1990s, to make the avenue feel like a garden.
=== STOP Water Tower & Pumping Station @ 41.8972,-87.6242
Walk north to Chicago Avenue. In the middle of the modern shopping district, here is something that looks like a toy castle: the Chicago Water Tower, with its pale yellow limestone, its battlements, and its little turrets.

It was finished in 1869, designed by William W. Boyington. Inside the tower was a tall iron standpipe running nearly the full height of the tower. The pumps across the street pushed water up into it, which helped to steady the water pressure and smooth out the surges from the steam pumps. The castle was just an elaborate cover for a pipe.

The pumping station across Michigan Avenue, in the matching stone, was the other half of the system. It pulled clean water from a crib two miles out in Lake Michigan through a tunnel under the lake bed, an engineering project led by Ellis Chesbrough that was completed in 1867. Out there, the water was supposed to be beyond the reach of the city's sewage, which was pouring into the lake from the river. That was not the end of the problem, as the river tour tells, but it was a start.

Then, in October 1871, the Great Fire swept north across the river and through this neighborhood. It burned almost everything. The water tower and pumping station survived, scorched but standing, although the fire knocked out the pumps, which made firefighting even more hopeless. After the fire, the tower became a symbol of the city's survival, and it has been treasured ever since.

The tower has had its critics. Oscar Wilde, who visited Chicago in 1882, is often quoted as calling it a castellated monstrosity with pepper boxes stuck all over it. But Chicagoans have repeatedly saved it from the wrecking ball, and today it holds a small gallery. The pumping station across the street still pumps water for part of the city, and it also houses a theater.

Just north of the Water Tower, on the east side of the avenue, rises Water Tower Place, a gray marble tower that opened in 1975. Its lower floors hold a shopping mall stacked eight stories high, around a glass-walled atrium with escalators, and above it are a hotel and condominiums. It was one of the first vertical shopping malls in the country, and when it was finished it was the tallest reinforced concrete building in the world. Its success helped pull the city's shopping center of gravity from State Street, in the Loop, north to Michigan Avenue, where it has stayed.
=== STOP Fourth Presbyterian Church & the Hancock @ 41.8989,-87.6240
Continue north along Michigan Avenue. On the west side, you will pass a gray stone Gothic church with a quiet, grassy courtyard. This is Fourth Presbyterian Church, completed in 1914, designed by Ralph Adams Cram, the great American champion of Gothic church architecture. Step into the courtyard if the gate is open. It is one of the most peaceful places on the avenue, and the cloister walk around it is designed to feel like a college quad at Oxford.

Now turn around and look up at the giant across the street: the black, tapering tower crossed with huge diagonal braces. This is the building long known as the John Hancock Center, finished in 1969. Its official name is now simply its address, 875 North Michigan Avenue, but most people still call it the Hancock.

It was designed at Skidmore, Owings and Merrill by the architect Bruce Graham and the engineer Fazlur Khan, a Bangladeshi-born structural engineer who reshaped tall buildings. Khan's insight was that a tall building could act like a hollow tube, with its outer walls carrying the wind loads. The giant X braces on the outside tie the walls together, so the tube is stiff, and the tower used far less steel than a conventional design would have for a building of its height. Khan went on to engineer the Sears Tower with the same team.

The Hancock was designed as a city in a tower, which was a new idea at the time. The lower floors were stores and parking, then offices, and above that, apartments, with a pool and a grocery store for residents. The taper is part of the logic: offices want big, deep floors, and apartments want smaller ones with windows, so the building narrows as it rises.

The observation deck near the top has one of the best views in the city, and on a clear day you can see across the lake to Indiana and Michigan.

The Hancock nearly didn't get built as planned. Its original developer, Jerry Wolman, who also owned the Philadelphia Eagles football team, ran into financial trouble during construction, and he lost both the building and the team. The John Hancock insurance company, which had lent the money, took over the project and finished it. The building quickly became a symbol of Chicago, and its two tall antennas carry television and radio signals for much of the city.
=== STOP Palmolive Building & the Drake Hotel @ 41.9001,-87.6236
Keep walking north. At Walton Street, look at the building on the east side of the avenue, the elegant limestone tower with setbacks that step back as it rises. This is the Palmolive Building, finished in 1929 by Holabird and Root, the same firm that designed the Board of Trade.

It was headquarters for the Colgate-Palmolive-Peet company, maker of the green Palmolive soap, made with palm and olive oils. At the top, the company installed a powerful rotating searchlight called the Lindbergh Beacon, named for the aviator Charles Lindbergh, to guide planes into Chicago. It swept across the city for decades, until new high-rise neighbors complained about the light in their windows.

In 1965, the building changed tenants in a way that made it famous all over again. Playboy Enterprises, founded in Chicago by Hugh Hefner, who had launched Playboy magazine here in 1953 on a kitchen table, bought a long lease and moved in. For years, giant letters on top of the tower spelled out PLAYBOY, lit up at night above the Gold Coast. Playboy moved its headquarters in 1989, and the building is now condominiums. Hefner's famous mansion, the original Playboy Mansion, was a few blocks away on North State Parkway.

Across Walton Street, at the very top of the Magnificent Mile, stands the Drake Hotel, which opened in 1920. Its owners, the Drake brothers, had built it out on what was then the edge of the lake, and it became one of the city's grand hotels, where presidents and royalty stayed. There is a popular story that Joe DiMaggio and Marilyn Monroe carved their initials into the bar of the hotel's Cape Cod Room. That is a legend that has never been confirmed.

Just past the hotel is Oak Street Beach, where Lake Shore Drive curves around a wide beach right in the shadow of the towers.
=== STOP 860–880 Lake Shore Drive & Cap Streeter @ 41.8987,-87.6186
Walk east on Walton or Chestnut Street to Lake Shore Drive, then turn south a block. The two identical black steel and glass towers set at right angles to each other are 860 and 880 Lake Shore Drive, designed by Ludwig Mies van der Rohe and completed in 1951.

They look ordinary today, because so many buildings around the world copy them. In 1951, they were revolutionary: glass boxes of apartments, floor-to-ceiling windows, the steel frame expressed on the outside. Look closely at the facades. The vertical steel I-beams you see were added purely for appearance. The real structural steel had to be covered in concrete for fire protection, so Mies added these exposed beams to show the frame. He was willing to be practical in order to be beautiful.

Now for the ground you are standing on. In 1886, a man named George Wellington Streeter, who called himself Captain, ran his small boat aground on a sandbar a few hundred feet offshore, roughly near here. The boat stayed, and Streeter encouraged contractors to dump fill around it. Sand and debris piled up, and soon there were acres of new land. Streeter declared that this land was not part of Illinois at all, since it had not existed when the state's boundaries were drawn. He called it the District of Lake Michigan, and he sold lots to buyers.

For more than thirty years, Streeter fought the city, the police, and wealthy property owners who said the new land belonged to them, sometimes with lawsuits and sometimes with shotguns. He was finally evicted for good in 1918, and he died in 1921. He lost every case, but he won the name. The neighborhood built on his landfill is still called Streeterville.

Look at the road in front of the towers, Lake Shore Drive. It began in the 1880s as a pleasure drive along the lake, pushed by Potter Palmer, and over the next century it was extended north and south into an expressway along almost the entire lakefront, much of it on landfill, with parks and beaches on the lake side. It's one of the great urban drives in the world, and in 1971 a Chicago folk-rock trio, Aliotta Haynes Jeremiah, wrote a song about driving it at night that became a local anthem. The drive used to have a dangerous S-curve where it crossed the Chicago River, a notorious spot for crashes. The curve was straightened in the 1980s.
=== STOP Museum of Contemporary Art & Northwestern's Campus @ 41.8972,-87.6211
Walk south and west to Chicago Avenue, between Lake Shore Drive and Michigan Avenue. The building with the broad flight of stairs, clad in cast aluminum panels, is the Museum of Contemporary Art, which opened here in 1996. It was designed by the German architect Josef Paul Kleihues, on the site of an old National Guard armory, and it faces a sculpture garden that runs back toward the lake.

The museum was founded in 1967 by a group of collectors and critics who felt the Art Institute wasn't paying enough attention to the art of their own time, and its first home was a small former bakery on Ontario Street. In 1969, the artist Christo wrapped that building entirely in tarpaulins and rope, one of the first of his famous wrappings. The museum has a reputation for provocative exhibitions, and Chicago artists have long had a strong place in it.

Look around you at the hospitals and medical buildings that fill the blocks to the south. This is the Chicago campus of Northwestern University, whose main campus is in the suburb of Evanston. Here are its law and medical schools and a cluster of hospitals, including Northwestern Memorial and Lurie Children's. It's one of the largest medical centers in the Midwest.

Not all of it has survived. A few blocks south, on Superior Street, stood Prentice Women's Hospital, designed by Bertrand Goldberg, the architect of Marina City, and finished in 1975, with a four-lobed concrete tower that looked like a cloverleaf cantilevered over a glass base. Architects around the world campaigned to save it, but the university won the fight and demolished it in 2014 to build a research tower. It's become a famous case in the debate over when modern buildings deserve protection.
=== STOP Ohio Street Beach & the Water Cribs @ 41.8935,-87.6126
Walk east to the lakefront and south to Ohio Street Beach, a small, curving sandy beach tucked between the high-rises and Navy Pier. It's a favorite place for open-water swimmers and triathletes, who train along the buoys in the summer.

Look out at the lake. On a clear day, you can see a few small round structures standing in the water, a couple of miles offshore. These are the water cribs: intake structures where Chicago draws its drinking water from deep in Lake Michigan, through tunnels under the lake bed. The first one, built in the 1860s, supplied the Water Tower and pumping station on Michigan Avenue. The cribs were once staffed by keepers who lived on them, sometimes for weeks at a time, and kept them free of ice.

Just to the north of the beach, on a peninsula of landfill, is the Jardine Water Purification Plant, which opened in the 1960s. It's one of the largest water treatment plants in the world, and it filters drinking water for millions of people in Chicago and its suburbs. The land on top of the plant is a park called Milton Lee Olive Park, named for an eighteen-year-old soldier from Chicago, Private First Class Milton Olive, who in 1965, in Vietnam, threw himself on a grenade to save the men around him. He was awarded the Medal of Honor, the first given to a Black soldier in the Vietnam War.

The lake is Chicago's great natural resource. It provides the city's water, moderates its weather, and gives it its lakefront, twenty-six miles of mostly public parks and beaches, protected since the days of Montgomery Ward.
=== STOP Navy Pier @ 41.8917,-87.6080
Walk south along Lake Shore Drive to Grand Avenue, then east to the long pier reaching out into the lake. This is Navy Pier.

It opened in 1916 as Municipal Pier, part of Burnham's plan, and it stretches more than half a mile out into Lake Michigan. It was built for lake shipping and passenger steamers, with warehouses on the pier and a recreation area at the end. Look at the two towers at the far end and the domed auditorium, the Grand Ballroom, which are original.

The pier was renamed in 1927 to honor the Navy veterans of the First World War. During the Second World War, it really did become a Navy base, a training center for thousands of sailors and aviation mechanics. Out on the lake, the Navy converted two old paddle-wheel passenger steamers into aircraft carriers, the USS Wolverine and the USS Sable, and young pilots practiced landing on them, safely far from enemy submarines. One of those pilots was a young George H. W. Bush. Many planes went into the lake during training, and some have since been recovered and restored.

After the war, the University of Illinois opened a branch campus on the pier for returning veterans, nicknamed Harvard on the Rocks, until 1965.

The pier was redeveloped and reopened in 1995 as a public attraction, with a giant Ferris wheel. That's fitting, because the very first Ferris wheel was built in Chicago, by the engineer George Washington Gale Ferris Junior, for the World's Columbian Exposition of 1893, as Chicago's answer to the Eiffel Tower. Today's wheel was put up in 2016. Ride it for one of the best views back toward the skyline you've just walked through.

Today, Navy Pier is one of the most visited attractions in the Midwest. Along its length are a children's museum, the Chicago Shakespeare Theater, restaurants, and boat docks where you can catch an architecture tour or a cruise. On summer nights there are fireworks over the lake. Walk out to the very end, past the old Grand Ballroom, where the pier opens up to the lake, for a sense of how far out into the water it reaches.

This is the end of the Michigan Avenue and Streeterville tour. In the warmer months, water taxis run from the pier to the river and to the Museum Campus.
