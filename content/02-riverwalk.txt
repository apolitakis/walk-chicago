area: Downtown
blurb: The river that made the city, then got turned around: DuSable, Fort Dearborn, the Wrigley and Tribune towers, Marina City and the Merchandise Mart.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour follows the main stem of the Chicago River west from Michigan Avenue to the point where its branches meet. It is a short river, only about a mile and a half from the lake to Wolf Point, but without it there would be no Chicago.

For thousands of years, this was a place of portage. The river ran slowly out of low marshland into Lake Michigan, and a few miles to the southwest, across a low and often flooded divide, lay the Des Plaines River, which flows to the Illinois and then the Mississippi. Native peoples, including the Potawatomi, carried canoes across that short gap. The French explorers Louis Jolliet and Jacques Marquette were shown the route in 1673, and Jolliet saw at once that a canal here could join the Great Lakes to the Gulf of Mexico. The name Chicago is usually traced to a Native word for a wild onion or garlic that grew in the marshes.

The first permanent settler who was not Native was Jean Baptiste Point DuSable, a trader of African descent, who built a farm and trading post on the north bank of the river near its mouth in the 1780s. In 1803, the United States Army built Fort Dearborn on the south bank. The Illinois and Michigan Canal finally opened Jolliet's route in 1848, the same year the first railroad reached the city, and Chicago grew faster than almost any city in history.

The river paid the price. Chicago dumped its sewage into it, and the river carried that sewage into the lake, where the city got its drinking water. Outbreaks of waterborne disease followed. The solution was audacious: in 1900, the city opened the Sanitary and Ship Canal and made the river flow backwards, away from the lake and toward the Mississippi. Downstream cities were not pleased, and the case went to the Supreme Court. Today, the river still flows backwards, controlled by locks at the lakefront.

For most of the twentieth century, the river was an industrial back alley. Buildings turned their backs to it. Over the last few decades, that changed completely. Cleaner water, new parks, and finally the Riverwalk, completed in stages up to 2016, turned the river into one of the city's favorite public spaces.

Look at the Chicago flag if you see one flying. The two blue stripes stand for the branches of the river, and the white bands between them for the parts of the city they divide. We will start at the lakefront, where the river meets Lake Michigan, and walk west along the river to the point where its branches meet.
=== STOP The River Mouth & the Chicago Lock @ 41.8888,-87.6128
You are at the eastern end of the Chicago River, near Lake Shore Drive, where the river meets Lake Michigan. Look east toward the lake, and you'll see the Chicago Harbor Lock, a pair of huge steel gates with a chamber between them.

This is where the river's reversal is controlled. Since 1900, the river has been made to flow away from the lake, but that only works if the lake is kept slightly higher than the river. The lock, completed in 1938, lets boats pass between the two while keeping the lake from pouring in. On a busy summer day, tour boats, sailboats, and kayaks wait their turn here. The water level changes only a foot or two inside the chamber, but it's enough to make the system work. In very heavy storms, the gates are sometimes opened to let floodwater out into the lake, which is a reminder that the city's sewage problem has never completely gone away.

The reversal itself was one of the great engineering projects of its time. The Sanitary and Ship Canal, dug between 1892 and 1900, was about twenty-eight miles long, and the methods and machines used to dig it were later used on the Panama Canal. The city opened the canal in January 1900, quickly and quietly, before Missouri could get a court order to stop it. St. Louis, downstream on the Mississippi, sued, saying Chicago was sending its sewage their way. Chicago won. A few years later, the Supreme Court found that St. Louis could not prove its typhoid deaths came from Chicago.

Look west, up the river toward the city. In front of you, the river runs straight as a ruler between walls of concrete and steel. It wasn't always like that. Before the city was built, the mouth of the river was blocked by a sandbar, and the river curved south along the beach before it reached the lake. In the 1830s, the army cut a channel through the sandbar and built piers out into the lake to keep it open, creating the harbor that made Chicago a port.

Now walk west along the river toward Michigan Avenue. As you go, notice the condominium towers on the north bank and the parks on the south. Much of this land was rail yards and warehouses until a few decades ago.

Above you, Lake Shore Drive crosses the river on a bascule bridge that opened in 1937. At its dedication, President Franklin Roosevelt gave one of the most important speeches of his presidency here, the Quarantine Speech, warning that the spreading lawlessness of aggressive nations, which most listeners understood to mean Japan, Germany, and Italy, should be quarantined like an epidemic. Many Americans wanted nothing to do with another war, and the speech was attacked as warmongering, but it marked the start of Roosevelt's turn toward confronting the dictators.
=== STOP DuSable Bridge & Fort Dearborn @ 41.8887,-87.6244
You are at the Michigan Avenue bridge, officially the DuSable Bridge since 2010, named for Jean Baptiste Point DuSable, the city's first permanent non-Native settler.

The bridge opened in 1920. It is a double-leaf bascule bridge, the kind Chicago is famous for. Each half pivots up on a trunnion, a heavy axle, balanced by an enormous counterweight hidden in a pit below the street. The city has dozens of these, and they still open in spring and fall to let sailboats travel between their winter storage yards on the river and Lake Michigan. If you are here on one of those mornings, you can watch the bridges go up one after another, in sequence, down the river.

Look at the four bridge houses at the corners. The sculpted reliefs on them, by James Earle Fraser and Henry Hering, show scenes from Chicago's history: the explorers, the trader, the defense of Fort Dearborn, and the rebuilding after the Great Fire. One of the bridge houses holds a small museum, where you can see the gears that lift the bridge.

Look at the sidewalk at the southeast corner, near Wacker Drive. You may find markers outlining the site of Fort Dearborn. The first fort was built in 1803. In August 1812, during the War of 1812, the garrison was ordered to evacuate, and as soldiers and settlers left along the lakeshore, they were attacked by Potawatomi warriors. Dozens were killed. That event is now usually called the Battle of Fort Dearborn. The fort was later rebuilt and eventually torn down.

DuSable's farm stood across the river, on the north bank, near the plaza in front of the Tribune Tower, where we are headed next.

DuSable married a Potawatomi woman named Kitihawa, and their farm on the north bank had a house, a bakehouse, a dairy, a smokehouse, barns, and orchards. Around 1800, he sold it all and left for Missouri, where he died. The property passed to a trader named John Kinzie, who is sometimes described as Chicago's founding father, though DuSable was here first. For a long time, Chicago's histories barely mentioned DuSable. That has changed. A bust of him stands near the bridge, and in 2021 the city renamed its great lakefront highway in his honor: Jean Baptiste Point DuSable Lake Shore Drive.
=== STOP Wrigley Building & Tribune Tower @ 41.8901,-87.6239
Cross the bridge to the north side. You are now at the foot of what Chicagoans call the Magnificent Mile, and flanking you are two of the city's most famous towers.

On the west side is the Wrigley Building, gleaming white, built in the early 1920s as headquarters for the Wrigley chewing gum company. It is clad in white glazed terra cotta, in several subtly different shades that get lighter toward the top, which helps it glow in the sun. Its clock tower was inspired by the Giralda, the bell tower of the cathedral in Seville. At night it is floodlit, and it was one of the first buildings in the city lit that way on purpose.

On the east side is the Tribune Tower, built for the Chicago Tribune after a famous international design competition in 1922. The winners, John Mead Howells and Raymond Hood, gave the newspaper a Gothic cathedral of an office building, crowned with flying buttresses. The second-place entry, a sleek, stepped design by the Finnish architect Eliel Saarinen, was in some ways more influential. Architects copied it for years.

Walk up to the base of the Tribune Tower and look closely at the walls. Embedded in the stone are fragments from famous places around the world, gathered by Tribune correspondents and others, including pieces said to come from the Great Wall of China, the Taj Mahal, and Westminster Abbey, and a piece of steel from the World Trade Center. Each one is labeled.

The plaza in front is Pioneer Court, near the site of DuSable's homestead. The Tribune has since moved out, and the tower has been converted into condominiums.

William Wrigley Junior came to Chicago in 1891 selling soap. He offered baking powder as a free gift to stores that stocked it, and when the baking powder proved more popular, he switched to selling that, with free chewing gum as the premium. The gum was more popular still. In 1893 he introduced Juicy Fruit and Wrigley's Spearmint, and he built the company on relentless advertising. He later bought the Chicago Cubs, and Santa Catalina Island off the coast of California, where the Cubs held spring training for years. The Tribune, meanwhile, was ruled for decades by Colonel Robert McCormick, a grandson of its famous editor Joseph Medill. McCormick called it the World's Greatest Newspaper, and when the paper bought a radio station in 1924, it took the call letters WGN, from that boast.
=== STOP Riverwalk at State Street @ 41.8874,-87.6266
Take the stairs down to the Riverwalk, and walk west under the bridges until you are near State Street.

The Riverwalk is built right at the water's edge, on the lower level, so the city's double-decked Wacker Drive runs above you. Each block between bridges was designed as a separate room with its own name and purpose. You will pass areas called the Marina, the Cove, where you can rent kayaks, the River Theater, with its broad steps, and the Water Plaza, where children play in fountains. It is worth coming back on a summer evening, when the cafés and wine bars along here are crowded and the architecture boats slide past.

Look up at the bridges as you go under them. Many have the same Art Deco bridge houses, and from down here you can see the steel and the counterweight pits.

Look at the water too. A few decades ago, it was considered too polluted for anything. Now you may see kayakers, and fish, and in recent years even beavers have been spotted. Every year on the Saturday before Saint Patrick's Day, the city dyes the river a startling bright green, a tradition that started in 1962. The dye fades within a day or two.

Across the river to the north, you should be able to see two round concrete towers with scalloped balconies. That is Marina City, our next stop.

Look up at the road above you, Wacker Drive. It's a double-decker, built in 1926 to replace a congested market street along the river called South Water Street, where produce wholesalers jammed the road with wagons. Wacker Drive was one of the first big projects of the Burnham plan. The upper level carries ordinary traffic, and the lower level, a dim world of columns and loading docks, carries trucks and deliveries. Lower Wacker has played Gotham City's streets in the movies, including the chase scenes in The Dark Knight, filmed here in 2007.

Once a year, the river turns a color you won't believe. On the Saturday before Saint Patrick's Day, boats crisscross the main stem near here, scattering an orange powder that turns the water a brilliant emerald green, while crowds pack the bridges and the Riverwalk. The tradition began in 1962. The plumbers' union had been using a green dye to trace illegal discharges of sewage into the river, and its business manager, who was also in charge of the city's Saint Patrick's Day parade, suggested dyeing the whole river for the holiday. Mayor Richard J. Daley loved the idea. The original dye was eventually replaced by a vegetable-based formula, which the plumbers keep secret.
=== STOP Marina City & the IBM Building @ 41.8877,-87.6297
You are now across from Marina City, the pair of round towers on the north bank between State and Dearborn. Chicagoans call them the corncobs.

They were designed by Bertrand Goldberg and completed in the 1960s, as a city within a city. The idea was to lure middle-class families back downtown when everyone else was moving to the suburbs. The complex had apartments, offices, a theater, a bowling alley, a skating rink, shops, a marina at the river's edge, and parking. The bottom floors of each tower are an open spiral parking garage. Look closely and you can see the cars parked right at the edge, with only a low barrier between them and the drop. The apartments above are wedge-shaped, like slices of a pie, each with a curved balcony.

Goldberg had studied at the Bauhaus and worked briefly for Mies van der Rohe, but he rejected the straight line. He thought people did not live in right angles. When Marina City was built, they were the tallest residential buildings and the tallest reinforced concrete structures in the world. The towers are also known from the cover of the album Yankee Hotel Foxtrot by the Chicago band Wilco.

Next door to the east is a sharp contrast: a dark, flat-sided slab of steel and glass. That is the former IBM Building, completed in 1973, the last American office building designed by Mies van der Rohe, who died in 1969. It is now called AMA Plaza and includes a hotel. Standing between these two, you can see the two big arguments of mid-century architecture face off across a single street.

Marina City has a famous moment in film, too. In the 1980 Steve McQueen movie The Hunter, a car chase ends with a car flying off the edge of one of the parking ramps and plunging into the river. It was a real stunt, done with a real car. The towers were built with concrete cores first, poured continuously as they rose, and the floors were cantilevered out from them, which is why the balconies seem to float. The low building between the towers, which was once the complex's theater, has been a music club.
=== STOP The Eastland Disaster @ 41.8869,-87.6318
Continue west along the Riverwalk to the stretch between Clark Street and LaSalle Street. Look for a historical marker near the water's edge. This is the site of the deadliest disaster in Chicago's history, deadlier even than the Great Fire.

On the morning of July twenty-fourth, 1915, thousands of employees of the Western Electric Company, along with their families, gathered here to board excursion boats for the company's annual summer picnic, across the lake in Michigan City, Indiana. Western Electric was the manufacturing arm of the Bell Telephone system, and its giant Hawthorne Works, in the suburb of Cicero, made most of the telephones in America. Many of its workers were young immigrants, especially Czechs, and for many of them the picnic was the event of the year.

The first boat to load was the Eastland, a passenger steamer known to be top-heavy and unstable. It was tied to the dock right here, on the south bank. More than two thousand five hundred people crowded on board, many of them onto the upper decks. As the ship prepared to leave, it began to list, first one way, then the other. Then, still tied to the dock, it rolled over onto its side in about twenty feet of water.

Hundreds of passengers below decks were trapped. Others were thrown into the river and pulled under by the crowd. Despite heroic rescue efforts by people on shore and on nearby boats, eight hundred and forty-four people died, most of them passengers, among them whole families. Bodies were taken to a temporary morgue in the Second Regiment Armory on the Near West Side. That building later became Harpo Studios, where Oprah Winfrey taped her show, and you'll pass its site on the West Loop tour.

There were investigations and trials, but no one was ever held responsible. The Eastland itself was raised, sold to the Navy, and used for years as a training ship called the USS Wilmette.

Look across the river at the red brick building with the clock tower on the north bank. That's the Reid Murdoch Building, built in 1914 for a wholesale grocery company. Its clock looked down on the disaster.
=== STOP Merchandise Mart @ 41.8885,-87.6354
Continue west along the river, past the bend where the river meets Wells Street and the elevated tracks cross overhead, until you reach the enormous building on the north bank. That is the Merchandise Mart.

When it opened in 1930, it was the largest building in the world by floor area, with about four million square feet. It was built by Marshall Field and Company, the department store, as a wholesale market, where retailers from across the country would come to buy goods under one roof. It has its own zip code. In 1945, it was bought by Joseph P. Kennedy, father of the future president, and the Kennedy family owned it for decades.

The building's design is Art Deco, with setbacks and a tower in the center of the long river facade. Look along the top for the rows of decorative panels. Along the river side, you may also see the busts of famous American merchants on tall columns, sometimes called the Merchant's Hall of Fame, including Marshall Field and Frank Woolworth.

At night, look at the river facade. Since 2018, it has been used for Art on the Mart, one of the largest permanent digital art projections in the world, which covers the whole face of the building with moving images on many evenings from spring through fall.

Today, much of the Mart is design showrooms and offices, and it is home to a number of technology companies.

The Mart was built over railroad tracks, on the site of an old passenger station, which was a common trick in Chicago: building on air rights above railroad lines. When Joseph Kennedy bought it in 1945, he paid a fraction of what it had cost to build, and it became the foundation of the Kennedy family fortune. In the early days of television, NBC had studios in the Mart, and several of the first national children's shows were broadcast from here, including Kukla, Fran and Ollie, a puppet show that was hugely popular in the late 1940s and 1950s.
=== STOP Wolf Point @ 41.8868,-87.6374
Walk west to where the river splits, beside Wolf Point. This is the confluence: the North Branch flows in from your right, the South Branch from your left, and together they become the main stem that you have been following back toward the lake. Of course, since the reversal of 1900, the water actually flows the other way, from the lake toward here and then down the South Branch.

Wolf Point, the land on the north side of the meeting point, was one of the first places in Chicago where settlers clustered. In the 1820s and 1830s, there was a tavern here, the Wolf Tavern, and other early buildings, including a store and a church. When Chicago was incorporated as a town in 1833, there were only a few hundred people living here. Four years later, it was a city. By 1890, it had more than a million people. That's how fast this place grew.

For much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the riverbanks here were lined with warehouses, lumber yards, and grain elevators. The river was choked with ships. Now Wolf Point has glass apartment and office towers, and the banks are being opened up for walkers.

Look south down the South Branch. On the left bank, at Madison Street, is a huge building shaped like an enormous armchair facing the river. It's the Civic Opera House, built by the utilities tycoon Samuel Insull in 1929, and home of the Lyric Opera of Chicago. People called it Insull's Throne, and the Pilsen tour tells his story. The tall black tower with the stepped top in the distance is the Willis Tower, formerly the Sears Tower, which was the tallest building in the world when it was completed in 1973.

Look north up the North Branch. About a mile upriver is Goose Island, an island created when a canal was cut across a bend of the river in the 1850s. Irish immigrants who settled there were said to have kept so many geese that the name stuck. For a century, Goose Island was all tanneries, factories, and rail spurs. In 1988, a brewpub named after the island opened nearby, and Goose Island beer became one of the best known craft beers in the country. The neighborhood is now being rebuilt with technology offices and labs.

Here our river tour ends. If you want to keep going, you can follow the branches, or head back east along the Riverwalk, where the architecture boat tours leave from near the Michigan Avenue bridge.
