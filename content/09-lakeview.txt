blurb: A celery-farming town turned ballpark neighborhood: Northalsted, Wrigley Field and the long wait for 2016, a London terrace in Chicago, and Graceland, the cemetery of the men who built the city.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour explores Lakeview, a big North Side neighborhood that includes Wrigleyville, around the ballpark, and Northalsted, the heart of Chicago's gay community.

For most of the nineteenth century, this was not Chicago at all. It was a separate township, Lake View, a mix of farms, lakeside resorts, and summer homes. The land was low and sandy, and it became famous for celery, which German and Luxembourger farmers grew here and shipped downtown. In 1854 a hotel called the Lake View House opened near the lake, giving the area its name. Lake View was incorporated as a city in 1887 and then annexed by Chicago in 1889, part of a huge expansion that year which more than doubled the city's size.

Once the elevated train arrived, at the turn of the twentieth century, the farms disappeared under blocks of two-flats, three-flats, and courtyard apartment buildings. The neighborhood filled with German, Swedish, and Irish families, and later with young people of every kind.

In 1914, a businessman named Charles Weeghman built a ballpark on the site of a former seminary at Clark and Addison Streets. Two years later, the Chicago Cubs moved in. The ballpark, later named Wrigley Field, gave its name to the surrounding blocks, now called Wrigleyville.

Starting in the 1970s, gay men and lesbians moved into the neighborhood around Halsted Street, and it became one of the most visible gay neighborhoods in the country.

We will start on Halsted Street, walk north to Wrigley Field, pass a tiny street that looks like London and an old movie palace, and end in Graceland Cemetery, where many of the people you've heard about on other tours are buried.
=== STOP Northalsted & the Legacy Walk @ 41.9405,-87.6491
You are on Halsted Street near Belmont Avenue. Look at the tall decorative pylons lining the sidewalk, ringed in rainbow colors. They were installed by the city in the late 1990s, making this one of the first officially recognized gay neighborhoods in the United States. The district is called Northalsted, and some people still call it by its older nickname, Boystown.

Look at the pylons more closely. Many carry bronze plaques honoring lesbian, gay, bisexual, and transgender figures from history. This is the Legacy Walk, an outdoor museum dedicated in 2012. As you walk north on Halsted, you'll find plaques for people like Jane Addams, the Chicago social reformer who founded Hull House, the playwright Lorraine Hansberry, the mathematician Alan Turing, and Bayard Rustin, the organizer of the 1963 March on Washington.

Chicago has a long LGBTQ history. The first gay rights organization in the United States that we know of was the Society for Human Rights, founded in Chicago in 1924 by Henry Gerber, a German immigrant and Army veteran. It was shut down by police within months. Gerber's house, on Crilly Court in Old Town, is now a city landmark.

The Chicago Pride Parade, one of the largest in the country, is held each June and runs through these streets.

Chicago's first Pride march was held in June 1970, a year after the Stonewall uprising in New York, when a small group of marchers walked from Washington Square Park, Bughouse Square, to the Civic Center downtown. The parade moved to this neighborhood in the 1970s as the community grew here. A few blocks north, at Halsted and Waveland, is the Center on Halsted, a large LGBTQ community center that opened in 2007, with a gym, a theater, and services for young people and seniors.
=== STOP Wrigley Field @ 41.9476,-87.6555
Walk north on Halsted and then west on Addison Street to Wrigley Field, at Clark and Addison. Look up at the red marquee at the main entrance. It's one of the most famous signs in American sports.

The ballpark opened in 1914 as Weeghman Park, built for the Chicago Whales of the Federal League, a short-lived rival major league. When that league folded, Weeghman bought the Cubs and moved them here in 1916. The chewing gum magnate William Wrigley Junior, whose building you saw on the river, took control of the team, and the ballpark was renamed Wrigley Field in 1926.

It's the second-oldest ballpark in the major leagues, after Boston's Fenway Park. The ivy on the outfield walls was planted in 1937 by a young Bill Veeck, who later became a famous team owner himself. The center field scoreboard, built the same year, is still operated by hand: people inside slide the numbers into place. And for decades, Wrigley Field had no lights. The Cubs played only day games at home until 1988, when lights were finally installed after years of neighborhood opposition.

The Cubs won back-to-back World Series championships in 1907 and 1908. Then they didn't win again for a hundred and eight years. Fans blamed many things, including a curse. In 1945, the story goes, a tavern owner named William Sianis was ejected from a World Series game with his pet goat, and he declared that the Cubs would never win again. That's the Curse of the Billy Goat, a legend that Chicagoans half believed for decades. In November 2016, the Cubs finally won the World Series, in Cleveland, in extra innings of the seventh game. Back home, the victory parade and rally drew what the city estimated as millions of people.

Look across Waveland and Sheffield Avenues behind the outfield walls. The bleachers on the roofs of those apartment buildings are the famous rooftop seats, where fans have been watching games for free, and later for a price, for generations.

And one more legend: in the 1932 World Series here against the Yankees, Babe Ruth is said to have pointed to the center field bleachers before hitting a home run to that very spot. Whether he really called his shot is still argued about.

For half a century, Wrigley Field was also home to Chicago's football team. The Bears played here from 1921 to 1970, and they got their name because their founder, George Halas, figured that football players were bigger than baseball players, so if the baseball team were the Cubs, his team should be the Bears.

After a game, look up at the flagpole above the center field scoreboard. If the Cubs won, a white flag with a blue W flies over the ballpark, and the crowd sings Go, Cubs, Go, written by the folk singer Steve Goodman. If they lost, a blue flag with a white L goes up instead.
=== STOP Alta Vista Terrace @ 41.9515,-87.6563
Walk north on Seminary Avenue, just west of the ballpark, to Grace Street, and then turn east onto a short street called Alta Vista Terrace, running between Byron and Grace.

It feels like you've walked into another city. This one block contains about forty narrow rowhouses, built in the early 1900s by the real estate developer Samuel Eberly Gross. Gross was one of Chicago's most successful developers, famous for selling small, affordable houses to working people on installment plans. Here he tried something different, modeled on the terraces of London.

Look closely at the facades. Each house is different, with its own style of windows, cornice, and doorway. But each one has a twin: the house diagonally across the street has the same design, mirrored. Walk the length of the block and see if you can match the pairs.

In 1971, Alta Vista Terrace became the first historic district designated by the city of Chicago. It's one of the most charming surprises in the city, just steps from the noise of Wrigleyville.

Samuel Gross had another, stranger claim to fame. He had written a play, and when the French playwright Edmond Rostand's Cyrano de Bergerac became an international hit, Gross went to court in Chicago, claiming that Rostand had stolen his plot. In 1902, an American judge agreed with him. It's one of the odder footnotes in the history of the theater, and Rostand's play, of course, is still performed around the world.
=== STOP The Music Box Theatre @ 41.9497,-87.6636
Walk west on Grace Street to Southport Avenue and turn south to number 3733. The small theater with the glowing vertical sign is the Music Box, opened in 1929, just as talking pictures were replacing silent films.

Like the Ramova in Bridgeport and many neighborhood movie houses of its day, the Music Box was built as an atmospheric theater. Step inside and look up: the auditorium is designed like an Italian courtyard at night, with a dark blue ceiling where small lights twinkle like stars and projected clouds drift slowly overhead. There are only about seven hundred and fifty seats, which made it a small, intimate neighborhood theater rather than a palace.

By the 1970s, most neighborhood theaters in Chicago had closed or been chopped up into multiplexes. The Music Box survived as a revival house, and since 1983 it has been one of the leading independent cinemas in the country, showing classic films, foreign films, documentaries, and cult favorites. Some screenings are accompanied by an organist playing the theater's organ, and its sing-along screening of The Sound of Music and its holiday double features have become neighborhood traditions.

Southport Avenue, around the theater, is a typical Chicago neighborhood shopping street, lined with brick two- and three-story buildings with shops below and apartments above, all within walking distance of the Brown Line. When Lakeview was booming in the 1910s and 1920s, streets like this were where people did all their shopping, and they've come back to life in the last thirty years.
=== STOP Graceland Cemetery: the Gate & Eternal Silence @ 41.9549,-87.6618
Walk north to Irving Park Road and east to Clark Street, to the gates of Graceland Cemetery. Founded in 1860, Graceland is often called the cemetery of architects, and it's the resting place of many of the people who built Chicago. It was designed as a peaceful landscape, by landscape architects including Ossian Cole Simonds, who believed a cemetery should feel like a natural park, with native trees and shrubs, open lawns, and winding drives. It's a beautiful place to walk. The cemetery office near the gate often has maps showing where the famous graves are.

Just inside, look for Eternal Silence, a hooded, robed figure by Lorado Taft, made in 1909 for the grave of Dexter Graves, an early hotel owner. The bronze has turned green with age, except for the face, which is dark in the shadow of the hood. There's a legend that if you look into its eyes, you'll see a vision of your own death, and another that it can't be photographed. Visitors photograph it every day.

And look for the grave of Jack Johnson, the first Black heavyweight boxing champion of the world, who won the title in 1908. Johnson was a flamboyant figure who refused to play the humble role white America expected of him. He opened a nightclub in Chicago, the Café de Champion, and married white women, which outraged many white Americans. In 1913, he was convicted under the Mann Act, a law against transporting women across state lines for immoral purposes, in a case widely seen as racially motivated. He fled the country and later served time in prison. In 2018, he was granted a posthumous presidential pardon.

Somewhere in these grounds is also George Pullman, the railroad sleeping-car magnate whose company town and the bitter strike of 1894 made him one of the most hated men in America. When he died in 1897, his family feared his body might be dug up and desecrated. He was buried at night in a pit lined with concrete and steel rails, under tons of cement. The Pullman tour tells his story.

Baseball is here too. Near the main drive, look for the grave of William Hulbert, who founded baseball's National League in 1876 and ran the Chicago team that became the Cubs. His headstone is a giant stone baseball, carved with the names of the league's original cities. And Ernie Banks, Mr. Cub, the Hall of Fame shortstop who played nineteen seasons at Wrigley Field and was famous for saying, "Let's play two," was buried here in 2015.

Look also for the little girl in the glass box: a lifelike marble statue of a child, sitting on a chair, sealed in a glass case. She's known as Inez Clarke, and the legend says she died young in 1880, and that on stormy nights she vanishes from her box. Historians who have dug into the records have found that even her name may be a mistake, but the statue is one of the most visited graves in the cemetery.
=== STOP Graceland: the Architects & the Tycoons @ 41.9607,-87.6604
Walk north along the cemetery's drives toward Lake Willowmere, the small lake in the northern part of the grounds.

On a small island in the lake, under a plain boulder, is Daniel Burnham, the architect of the World's Fair and the Plan of Chicago. John Wellborn Root, his partner, who died so young, is buried here too, not far away. Look also for the Getty Tomb, designed in 1890 by Louis Sullivan for the family of a lumber merchant, Henry Getty. Its intricate bronze gates and the lacy stone ornament on its upper half, contrasted with the plain stone below, make it one of Sullivan's masterpieces. Architects come from around the world to see it. Sullivan himself is buried in Graceland, under a rough stone put up by his admirers, with a bronze portrait medallion. He died poor in 1924, and the monument was paid for by friends and colleagues.

Ludwig Mies van der Rohe is here too, under a simple, polished black granite slab, as minimal as one of his buildings.

So are the merchants and industrialists whose names you've heard on other tours. Potter and Bertha Palmer lie in twin sarcophagi under a Greek temple of columns. Marshall Field's grave has a seated, veiled figure called Memory, by Daniel Chester French, who sculpted the statue in the Lincoln Memorial. Philip Armour, the meatpacker, is here, and Allan Pinkerton, the founder of the famous detective agency, who guarded Abraham Lincoln on his way to his first inauguration.

Wandering Graceland, you can trace the whole story of Chicago's rise in its gravestones.

In the Pinkerton family plot lies Kate Warne, whom Pinkerton hired in 1856 as the first female detective in the United States. She helped him protect Lincoln on his secret journey through Baltimore to Washington in 1861, when the agency uncovered a plot to kill him on the way.

This is the end of the Lakeview tour. The Sheridan station on the Red Line, a few blocks east of the cemetery's main gate on Irving Park Road, will take you back downtown.
