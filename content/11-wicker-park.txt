=== OVERVIEW
This tour explores Wicker Park and Bucktown, two neighborhoods on the Northwest Side, along the diagonal line of Milwaukee Avenue.

Milwaukee Avenue began as a Native American trail running northwest from the river, and it became one of the main roads out of the city toward Wisconsin. In the second half of the nineteenth century, it was the spine of Polish Chicago. Poles came to Chicago in huge numbers, many to work in the factories, rail yards, and stockyards, and by the early twentieth century, Chicago had one of the largest Polish populations of any city in the world. The intersection of Milwaukee, Division, and Ashland was called Polish Downtown, and the neighborhood around it was dense with churches, newspapers, banks, and halls.

Wicker Park itself is named for two brothers, Charles and Joel Wicker, real estate developers who donated a small triangle of land for a park in 1870. The Great Fire never reached this far, and in the years after it, as families who had lost everything looked for somewhere to rebuild, the Northwest Side filled quickly. Wealthy German and Scandinavian brewers, merchants, and manufacturers built grand houses around the park, while workers' cottages and two-flats spread out in all directions.

By the mid-twentieth century, the neighborhood had become poor. Puerto Rican and Mexican families arrived after World War II, and the area faced disinvestment, arson, and neglect. Then, in the 1980s and 1990s, artists and musicians moved in, drawn by cheap rent. Wicker Park became the center of Chicago's alternative rock and art scene, and then, as so often happens, one of its most expensive neighborhoods.

We will start at Saint Stanislaus Kostka Church, in the old Polish Downtown, cross the Polish Triangle, walk up Milwaukee Avenue to the mansions of Wicker Park, then to the Six Corners, and finish on the elevated trail called the 606 and in Bucktown.
=== STOP Saint Stanislaus Kostka & Polish Downtown @ 41.9061,-87.6602
You are at Saint Stanislaus Kostka Church, on Noble Street just north of Division Street. The huge brick church with two tall towers was the mother church of Polish Chicago. The parish was founded in 1867, the first Polish parish in the city, and the present church was completed in 1881, designed by Patrick Keely, the architect of Holy Name Cathedral.

By the end of the nineteenth century, Saint Stanislaus was said to be the largest Catholic parish in the United States, with tens of thousands of parishioners. Masses were said over and over every Sunday to accommodate the crowds, and the parish ran schools, societies, and a bank. The priest who built it up, Father Vincent Barzynski, also helped found Polish newspapers and other parishes across the city.

Now look east toward the Kennedy Expressway, which runs just beyond the church. When the expressway was planned in the 1950s, the route came very close to the church, and there was fear it would be demolished. Local lore says that the parish's political connections led planners to bend the highway around it. The church survived, though a lot of the neighborhood didn't.

The writer Nelson Algren set his novel Never Come Morning in this neighborhood, a grim story of a young Polish-American boxer. It angered many in the Polish community when it was published in 1942. You'll hear more about Algren at the next two stops.

A short walk south, at Milwaukee and Augusta, is the Polish Museum of America, founded in 1935 and one of the oldest ethnic museums in the country. The Polish community has since moved further northwest and to the suburbs, but Polish Downtown is where it began.

Saint Stanislaus had a rival, a couple of blocks south on Noble Street: Holy Trinity, a parish founded by Polish nationalists who wanted their church run by a committee of its own members, and not by the religious order that ran Saint Stanislaus. For about twenty years, the two parishes fought what became known as the Polish church war, with lawsuits, closed doors, and appeals to Rome, until the Pope's representative in America settled it in 1893. Holy Trinity's grand church, finished in the early 1900s, still stands on Noble Street.

Chicagoans used to say that their city had more Poles than any city in the world except Warsaw. That was probably never quite true, but by the early twentieth century, several hundred thousand Polish immigrants and their children lived in Chicago, and the city is still the capital of Polish America.
=== STOP The Polish Triangle @ 41.9036,-87.6672
Walk west on Division Street to the small triangular plaza where Division Street, Milwaukee Avenue, and Ashland Avenue cross. This is the Polish Triangle, the heart of what was once called Polish Downtown, and it's where the Division Street station of the Blue Line comes up to the street.

For the first half of the twentieth century, this intersection was the commercial and political center of Polish Chicago. Around it stood the offices of Polish newspapers, banks, fraternal societies, and the headquarters of the Polish National Alliance, a large fraternal organization that was a power in Polish American life. One of the Polish-language daily newspapers, Dziennik Związkowy, founded in 1908, is still published. On Saturdays, the sidewalks were crowded with shoppers speaking Polish, Yiddish, German, and Ukrainian.

In the middle of the plaza is a fountain dedicated to Nelson Algren, with a line from his writing carved into it: "for the masses who do the city's work also keep the city's heart." Algren knew this intersection well. His novels were full of the bars, pool halls, and boxing gyms of the Polish Northwest Side, and his most famous book, The Man with the Golden Arm, takes place in the streets around here.

Look west along Division Street. That stretch was once known as Polish Broadway, lined with taverns and dance halls. Just west of here is the Chopin Theatre, a 1918 movie house that's now a theater and performance space with a famously eclectic basement café. In the 1960s and 1970s, the Polish community moved farther northwest, and the neighborhood became largely Puerto Rican and Mexican. Since the 1990s, it's been gentrifying, and today Division Street is lined with restaurants, bars, and sidewalk cafés.
=== STOP Beer Baron Row & Algren's Wicker Park @ 41.9092,-87.6804
Walk northwest up Milwaukee Avenue and then west to Hoyne Avenue, north of Division. Walk along Hoyne and the side streets around the park, such as Pierce Avenue and Bell Avenue.

These blocks are lined with some of the most elaborate Victorian mansions in Chicago, built in the 1880s and 1890s for brewers, merchants, and manufacturers, many of them German and Scandinavian. Hoyne Avenue is sometimes called Beer Baron Row. Look for the towers, turrets, and wraparound porches, the carved stone and pressed metal, and the stained glass. Look especially for the Hermann Weinhardt House at 2135 West Pierce Avenue, built in 1888, with an almost overwhelming facade of decoration in wood and stone.

Not far from here, on Evergreen Avenue, lived the novelist Nelson Algren, who wrote The Man with the Golden Arm, about a heroin-addicted card dealer, which won the first National Book Award for fiction in 1950. Algren was the poet of Chicago's down-and-out: drunks, hustlers, boxers, and immigrants. His book-length prose poem, Chicago: City on the Make, contains a line often quoted about the city: "Like loving a woman with a broken nose, you may well find lovelier lovelies. But never a lovely so real."

Algren had a long, famous love affair with the French writer Simone de Beauvoir, who visited him in his earlier Chicago apartment in the late 1940s. Algren lived in this neighborhood for many years. The block of Evergreen where he lived carries an honorary street sign with his name.

Across the street from the Weinhardt House, at 2138 West Pierce, stands the John Runge House, built in 1884, which is often called the Paderewski House. According to neighborhood legend, the great Polish pianist Ignacy Paderewski, who later became prime minister of Poland, once played a concert from its balcony for a crowd gathered in the street, because the hall where he was supposed to perform was too small. No one has ever found a record of the concert, but the name has stuck for a century.
=== STOP Wicker Park & the Six Corners @ 41.9105,-87.6774
Walk to the small triangular park, the Wicker Park itself, and then to its northeast corner, at the six-way intersection of North Avenue, Damen Avenue, and Milwaukee Avenue. Look north at the tall Art Deco tower overlooking the intersection. Built in 1929 as the Northwest Tower, it's nicknamed the Coyote Building, and for years it was a symbol of the neighborhood's art scene. It's now a hotel.

In the 1990s, this intersection was the center of Chicago's indie rock and art world. Clubs like the Double Door, just south on Milwaukee, hosted bands on their way up, and in 1997 the Rolling Stones played a surprise show there. Liz Phair's 1993 album Exile in Guyville was made out of this scene. The neighborhood got so much attention that it became a backdrop for movies, including High Fidelity, with John Cusack, set in a record store near here.

Along the way, Wicker Park was gentrified so thoroughly that it became a symbol of the process. In 2001, when MTV's The Real World filmed a season in a loft building on North Avenue, residents and activists protested outside, angry about what they saw as the neighborhood being turned into a brand.

Look for the Flat Iron Building, a wedge-shaped building on the corner of North and Milwaukee, which has housed artist studios for decades. The neighborhood still has a big art festival each year, and walking down Milwaukee and Division, you'll see plenty of independent shops, bars, and restaurants, along with chain stores that would have been unthinkable thirty years ago.

Long before it was hip, this intersection was a busy shopping district, with stores, banks, and movie theaters, where Polish, German, and Scandinavian families came to shop on Saturdays. The elevated train, now the Blue Line, stopped right at the intersection, as it still does.

In the early 1990s, the music press took notice. Bands like Urge Overkill and Veruca Salt, along with Liz Phair, came out of the Chicago scene and became national names, and magazines began asking whether Chicago would be the next Seattle. It never quite was, but for a few years, this intersection was one of the most exciting places in American music.
=== STOP The 606 @ 41.9138,-87.6775
Walk north on Damen Avenue to the elevated trail crossing overhead, just south of Cortland Street. Take the ramp up. This is the Bloomingdale Trail, more often called the 606, after the first three digits of Chicago's zip codes.

It's an old elevated freight railroad line, the Bloomingdale Line, built at street level in the 1870s along Bloomingdale Avenue. Around 1915, after pressure from the city to make streets safer, the railroad raised the tracks onto an embankment, with bridges over each cross street. Freight trains ran along it for decades, serving factories and warehouses. When the freight traffic stopped, the embankment became an overgrown, unofficial walking path.

The city converted it into a park, which opened in June 2015. It runs for about two and three quarter miles, connecting several neighborhoods from Bucktown and Wicker Park in the east to Logan Square and Humboldt Park in the west. Along the way are small parks at street level, public art, and an observatory at its western end.

Like the High Line in New York, the 606 has been a huge success, and like the High Line, it has driven up property values sharply around it. In the neighborhoods to the west, which were largely Latino and working class, it has raised fears of displacement, and the city has experimented with ways to limit it.

Walk west along the trail, and you'll notice how the neighborhoods change.

As you walk west along the trail from Damen, look back to the east, toward the Kennedy Expressway. The great dome you see on the skyline belongs to Saint Mary of the Angels, a church built by Polish immigrants and completed in 1920, modeled on Saint Peter's Basilica in Rome. By the late 1980s, it was crumbling and about to be torn down. It was saved, restored, and reopened in the 1990s, and its dome is once again one of the landmarks of the Northwest Side.
=== STOP Bucktown & Margie's Candies @ 41.9176,-87.6872
Leave the trail at Western Avenue and walk north to Armitage Avenue. You're now in Bucktown, the neighborhood north of Wicker Park.

The name, the story goes, comes from the goats that Polish immigrants kept in their yards in the nineteenth century. The area was called Kozie Prery, or goat prairie, in Polish, and a male goat is a buck. Bucktown was a working-class neighborhood of Polish and German families, with factories along the river and the railroads, until it too was gentrified in the 1990s.

On the corner of Armitage and Western is Margie's Candies, an old-fashioned ice cream parlor and candy shop that has been open since the 1920s. It's famous for its huge sundaes served in clamshell-shaped dishes, and its walls are covered with memorabilia. Margie's likes to tell visitors that the Beatles stopped in for sundaes during their tour in the 1960s, and there's even a story that Al Capone was a customer. Those are stories, not certainties, but the sundaes are real.

This is the end of the Wicker Park and Bucktown tour. If you keep walking west along the 606, you'll eventually reach Logan Square, where the Logan Square tour begins.
