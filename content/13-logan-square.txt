title: Logan Square & the Boulevards
area: West & Northwest Sides
blurb: Chicago's emerald necklace of boulevards: greystones and mansions, Palmer Square, the Norwegian church that still sings in Norwegian, the Illinois Centennial eagle, and the old movie palace on Milwaukee Avenue.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour explores Logan Square, on the Northwest Side, about five miles from the Loop. It's built around one of the great features of Chicago that many visitors never see: the boulevard system.

In 1869, the Illinois legislature created park districts for the city's North, West, and South Sides, and they planned a ring of large parks around the edge of the city, linked by broad, landscaped boulevards. When it was finished, the system ran for more than twenty-five miles in a great arc, from Lincoln Park on the north through the West Side parks, Humboldt, Garfield, and Douglas, to Washington and Jackson Parks on the South Side. The West Side parks were designed by William Le Baron Jenney, the engineer of the Home Insurance Building, and later reshaped by the landscape architect Jens Jensen. The boulevards were meant to be pleasure drives for carriages, lined with elms and mansions.

Logan Square sits at a corner of that ring, where Logan Boulevard turns south and becomes Kedzie Boulevard. The neighborhood is named for General John A. Logan, a Civil War general and senator from Illinois, who helped establish Memorial Day. His statue stands in Grant Park.

The area was annexed to Chicago in 1889, and when the elevated railroad reached it in the 1890s, it filled with Scandinavian, German, and Polish families. Along the boulevards, wealthier families built mansions and handsome two- and three-flat buildings faced with gray Indiana limestone, which Chicagoans call greystones. In the second half of the twentieth century, Logan Square became a largely Latino neighborhood, with Puerto Rican, Mexican, and Cuban families. In the last twenty years, it's become one of the most popular neighborhoods for young Chicagoans, with the restaurants, bars, and rising rents that come with that.

We'll start at Palmer Square, walk north up Kedzie Boulevard to the square itself, and then east along Logan Boulevard.
=== STOP Palmer Square @ 41.9211,-87.7050
You're at Palmer Square, a long, oval park along the boulevard where Kedzie Boulevard and Humboldt Boulevard meet. It's named for Potter Palmer, the merchant and real estate developer who also built the Gold Coast.

Look at the houses and apartment buildings around the square. You'll see a remarkable mix: big Victorian mansions, Prairie School houses, and courtyard apartment buildings. These were built from the 1890s to the 1920s, when the boulevard was a prestigious address for the middle class and the newly prosperous.

Now look at the boulevard itself. Kedzie Boulevard runs north from here, with a wide grassy median in the middle and service drives on either side. The boulevards were designed to be parks in themselves, places to stroll and ride, and to give the neighborhoods along them a sense of grandeur.

Notice the greystones along the boulevard: two- and three-story buildings with flat fronts of rough or carved Indiana limestone. A greystone usually has one apartment on each floor. Families would often own the building, live on one floor, and rent out the others. The two-flat and three-flat were the backbone of Chicago's neighborhoods, a way for immigrant and working families to own property, and they're still everywhere in the city.

Most greystones are faced with Bedford limestone, quarried in southern Indiana, the same stone used for the Empire State Building and the Pentagon. After the Great Fire of 1871, Chicago required fireproof materials in much of the city, and the stone, which was easy to carve and relatively cheap to ship by rail, became the city's signature building material for middle-class homes. There are tens of thousands of greystones still standing in Chicago, and there's nothing quite like them in any other American city.

South of the square, Humboldt Boulevard runs about a mile to Humboldt Park, where the Division Street tour ends. Together, these boulevards are part of a single system, more than twenty-five miles long, and in 2018, the whole system was listed on the National Register of Historic Places.
=== STOP Minnekirken & the Illinois Centennial Monument @ 41.9284,-87.7068
Walk north along Kedzie Boulevard toward Logan Square. On the west side of the boulevard, near the square, is a red brick church with a tall steeple. This is the Norwegian Lutheran Memorial Church, known as Minnekirken, which means memorial church in Norwegian.

It was built in 1912, when Logan Square had a large Scandinavian population. Norwegian immigrants came to Chicago in great numbers in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and for a while, Chicago had one of the largest Norwegian communities outside Norway.

As Norwegian Americans moved to the suburbs and assimilated, most of the Norwegian churches switched to English, merged, or closed. But Minnekirken has kept holding services in Norwegian, making it one of the last churches of its kind in the United States.

Look along the boulevard for other signs of the old Scandinavian and German neighborhood: churches, lodges, and halls, many of them now used by Latino congregations or turned into apartments.

Walk to the center of Logan Square itself, the round plaza where Logan Boulevard, Kedzie Boulevard, and Milwaukee Avenue meet. In the middle stands a tall white marble column, topped with a bronze eagle.

This is the Illinois Centennial Monument, built in 1918 to mark the hundredth anniversary of Illinois becoming a state. It was designed by Henry Bacon, the architect of the Lincoln Memorial in Washington, and the sculptor Evelyn Longman. The column is about seventy feet tall.

Look at the carved reliefs around the base. They show figures representing the history of Illinois, including Native Americans, explorers, farmers, and laborers, symbols of the forces that built the state.

The square is the heart of the neighborhood. On Sundays, the Logan Square Farmers Market fills the streets around it, and in summer, there are concerts and festivals. Look around at the buildings facing the square, and you'll see some fine old commercial buildings and banks, from the time when Logan Square was a busy shopping district at the end of the elevated line.

The monument was paid for by the Ferguson Fund, a bequest left to the Art Institute by Benjamin Ferguson, a lumber merchant who wanted to fill Chicago with public sculpture. The same fund paid for many of the city's best-known monuments, including Lorado Taft's Fountain of Time, on the Midway in Hyde Park.

On the southeast side of the square, near Milwaukee Avenue, look for a small cottage-like building with a steep roof. It's an old comfort station, built in the 1920s as a public restroom and a shelter for people waiting for streetcars, and it's now a tiny arts space. Down below the square is the Logan Square station of the Blue Line. For decades, Logan Square was the end of the elevated line from downtown, and the busy terminal made the square a shopping center for the whole Northwest Side. In 1970, the line was extended northwest in a subway beneath Milwaukee Avenue, and later all the way to O'Hare Airport.
=== STOP The Logan Theatre & Milwaukee Avenue @ 41.9298,-87.7089
Walk northwest a short way up Milwaukee Avenue to the Logan Theatre, with its old vertical sign and its marquee. It opened in 1915, and it's one of the oldest movie theaters in the city still showing films.

Milwaukee Avenue was the main commercial street of the neighborhood. In the early twentieth century, it was lined with stores, banks, and movie theaters, and the elevated line brought shoppers from all over the Northwest Side. Below you, the Blue Line now runs in a subway, connecting Logan Square to downtown in one direction and O'Hare Airport in the other.

Like much of Logan Square, the theater went through hard times and was restored in 2012, with its original Art Deco details and stained glass. It's a good example of how the neighborhood has been revived.

Walk along Milwaukee Avenue, and you'll find everything from old Mexican taquerias and Puerto Rican bakeries to some of the most acclaimed new restaurants and bars in the city. The neighborhood has become a destination for food and drink, but the change has also pushed out many longtime residents, and many murals and community groups in the area speak about the fight against displacement.

Milwaukee Avenue is one of Chicago's diagonal streets. Most of the city is laid out on a strict grid, but a few streets run at an angle, because they follow old Native American trails that were later used as roads by settlers and wagons. Milwaukee Avenue follows the old trail northwest toward the city of Milwaukee, which is how it got its name.

Follow it about a mile farther northwest, and you'll come to Avondale, and the neighborhood that Polish Chicagoans call Jackowo, named for Saint Hyacinth Basilica, a huge church with a dome and towers that was completed in 1921. In the late twentieth century, Jackowo was the heart of Polish Chicago, with Polish delis, bakeries, travel agencies, and bookstores lining Milwaukee Avenue.

And about two miles west of here, in a neighborhood called Hermosa, Walt Disney was born, in 1901, in a modest wooden house on Tripp Avenue that his father, Elias, a carpenter, built himself. His mother, Flora, is said to have drawn up the plans. The family moved to a farm in Missouri when Walt was four, and later returned to Chicago, where he went to high school and took art classes before heading west.
=== STOP Logan Boulevard @ 41.9290,-87.7000
Walk back to the square and then east along Logan Boulevard. This stretch, running east toward the Kennedy Expressway, is one of the grandest residential streets on the Northwest Side.

Look at the mansions on the north side of the boulevard. Many were built in the early twentieth century by the neighborhood's prosperous families: Scandinavian and German businessmen, contractors, and professionals. You'll see Prairie School houses, Classical Revival homes with columns, and Romanesque mansions in stone.

Look also at the big apartment buildings, with their courtyards and limestone fronts. These courtyard buildings were a Chicago innovation of the early twentieth century, bringing light and air to every apartment, with a garden in front.

At the eastern end of the boulevard, the elevated tracks of the Blue Line and the Kennedy Expressway cut across the landscape. The construction of the expressway in the 1950s sliced through the neighborhood, and the boulevards and the expressway meet awkwardly here.

The expressway was the Northwest Expressway when it opened in 1960. A few days after President Kennedy was assassinated in November 1963, it was renamed the Kennedy Expressway, and it's now one of the busiest highways in the country, carrying traffic between downtown and O'Hare.

The boulevard system once continued from here toward the lake. From the end of Logan Boulevard at Western Avenue, carriages could turn onto Diversey Parkway and ride east all the way to Lincoln Park. On a Sunday afternoon in 1900, you could have driven a horse and buggy along an unbroken chain of parks and boulevards for miles around the edge of the city, from the lakefront on the North Side to the lakefront on the South Side. That was the dream of the men who planned the system in 1869, and much of it still survives, though most of the people who travel it today are in cars.

This is the end of the Logan Square tour. The Logan Square station on the Blue Line, back at the square, will take you downtown. Or, if you walk about a mile south along California Avenue, you'll reach the 606 trail, which you can follow east toward Bucktown and Wicker Park.
