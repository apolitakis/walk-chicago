title: Hyde Park & the White City
area: South Side
blurb: The 1893 World's Fair and the museum Sears money saved, Olmsted's Wooded Island, the Obama Presidential Center, the original Midway, Rockefeller's university, Wright's Robie House, and the birthplace of the atomic age.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour explores Hyde Park, on the lakefront about seven miles south of the Loop. It's home to the University of Chicago, two great parks, and some of the most important sites in American history and science.

Hyde Park began as a separate suburb in the 1850s, founded by a lawyer named Paul Cornell, who bought land here and persuaded the Illinois Central Railroad to build a station. It was a quiet, leafy place, with summer homes and hotels along the lake. Chicago annexed it in 1889.

Then two events transformed it. In 1890, the University of Chicago was founded here, with money from the oil magnate John D. Rockefeller. And in 1893, Chicago hosted the World's Columbian Exposition in Jackson Park, along the lake.

The fair celebrated the four hundredth anniversary of Columbus's voyage, a year late. It was Chicago's announcement to the world that it had recovered from the Great Fire and become a great city. Daniel Burnham was in charge of construction, and Frederick Law Olmsted, the designer of Central Park in New York, laid out the grounds. At its center was the White City, a group of enormous neoclassical buildings arranged around lagoons, painted white and lit up at night with electric lights. More than twenty-five million visits were recorded over six months.

The fair introduced visitors to the Ferris wheel, to electric lighting on a grand scale, and, according to company legend, to products like Cracker Jack and Juicy Fruit gum. It also had a dark side. Black Americans were largely excluded from its exhibits, and Ida B. Wells and Frederick Douglass published a pamphlet in protest. And in Englewood, to the west, a con man named H. H. Holmes was committing murders in a building he owned. How many people he killed, and how many of them were fairgoers, is still argued over, and the legends have grown with time.

In the twentieth century, Hyde Park became one of the most racially integrated neighborhoods in Chicago, through determined efforts by residents. It's also the neighborhood where Barack Obama lived, taught, and began his political career.

We'll start on the lakefront at Promontory Point, walk south to the Museum of Science and Industry and through Jackson Park, and then west along the Midway to the University of Chicago.
=== STOP Promontory Point @ 41.7959,-87.5772
You're on Promontory Point, a small peninsula sticking out into Lake Michigan at 55th Street, reached by a tunnel under Lake Shore Drive. It's one of the most beloved places on the South Side.

The Point is man-made. It was built on landfill in the 1930s, as part of a long project to extend the lakefront parks south from downtown. The landscape was designed by Alfred Caldwell, the same landscape architect who created the Lily Pool in Lincoln Park. Look at how he shaped it: a gentle meadow, rising toward the center, ringed with trees, with a stone field house and a small tower that looks like a lighthouse, and a council ring, a circle of stone benches meant for gathering around a fire. And around the edge, the shoreline is armored with huge limestone blocks laid in steps down to the water.

Those steps are what Hyde Parkers love most. On a summer day, people sit on them to sunbathe, read, picnic, and jump into the lake. In the early 2000s, the city planned to replace the old limestone with concrete, as it had along much of the lakefront. Neighbors fought the plan for years, arguing that the stone steps were part of the park's design and history, and the battle over the Point became a symbol of the neighborhood's stubborn independence.

Look north from the tip of the Point. You'll see one of the best views of the Chicago skyline, rising out of the lake about seven miles away, across the curve of the shoreline. Look south, and you'll see the dome of the Museum of Science and Industry, our next stop.
=== STOP Museum of Science and Industry @ 41.7907,-87.5829
You're in front of the Museum of Science and Industry, a vast Greek temple of a building facing the lagoon at the north end of Jackson Park.

This is the only major building from the 1893 World's Fair still standing in Jackson Park. It was the Palace of Fine Arts, designed by Charles Atwood, who also designed the Reliance Building downtown. Like the other buildings of the White City, most of it was built of a temporary material called staff, a mix of plaster and fibers. But because it held priceless art loaned from around the world, it had a solid brick structure underneath.

After the fair, it housed a natural history museum, which later moved downtown as the Field Museum. The building decayed. It was saved by Julius Rosenwald, the president of Sears, Roebuck and Company, the mail-order giant based in Chicago. Rosenwald had visited the Deutsches Museum in Munich and wanted Chicago to have a museum of science and technology. He put millions of dollars into rebuilding the Palace of Fine Arts in permanent limestone. The museum opened in 1933, during Chicago's second world's fair, the Century of Progress.

Rosenwald was also one of the great philanthropists of his time. Working with Booker T. Washington, he funded the construction of thousands of schools for Black children across the rural South, which became known as Rosenwald Schools.

Inside, look for a German submarine, the U-505, captured by the US Navy in 1944, a full-size replica of a coal mine, and a giant model railroad.

Rosenwald refused to let the museum be named after him, though for a short while people called it the Rosenwald Industrial Museum anyway. Nearly ninety years later, in 2019, the museum took the name of a donor after all: it's now officially the Griffin Museum of Science and Industry, after the financier Kenneth Griffin, who gave a gift of a hundred and twenty-five million dollars. Most Chicagoans still call it the Museum of Science and Industry, or just the MSI. Its coal mine exhibit, where visitors ride an elevator down into a recreated shaft, has been running since the museum opened in 1933.

As you walk south from the museum toward the Wooded Island, you'll cross a stone bridge over the lagoon. It's named for Clarence Darrow, the most famous trial lawyer of his time, who lived in Hyde Park. In 1924, Darrow defended Nathan Leopold and Richard Loeb, two brilliant, wealthy young men from neighboring Kenwood, who had murdered a fourteen-year-old boy, Bobby Franks, for the thrill of committing the perfect crime. Darrow saved them from the death penalty with a closing argument that lasted about twelve hours. When Darrow died in 1938, his ashes were scattered over this lagoon, and there's a legend that on the anniversary of his death, his ghost can be seen on the bridge.
=== STOP Wooded Island & the Statue of the Republic @ 41.7850,-87.5822
Walk south along the lagoon to the bridge to the Wooded Island, in the middle of Jackson Park.

During the fair, Olmsted designed this island as a quiet, natural retreat in the middle of the grand buildings, a place to rest your eyes. The Japanese government built a pavilion here, called the Ho-o-den, or Phoenix Hall, modeled on a famous temple. It made a deep impression on many Americans, possibly including the young Frank Lloyd Wright, who later spoke of his love for Japanese design. The pavilion burned in 1946. Today, a Japanese garden, called the Garden of the Phoenix, is on the island. Look for the sculpture called Sky Landing, by the artist Yoko Ono, which was installed in 2016.

Almost all of the White City was gone within a few years of the fair. Some buildings were destroyed by fire, others torn down. But south of here, at the end of what was once the fair's Grand Basin, there's a gilded statue of a woman with her arms raised. It's the Statue of the Republic, a smaller replica, put up in 1918, of the sixty-five-foot statue by Daniel Chester French that stood over the Grand Basin during the fair.

The White City inspired the City Beautiful movement, which shaped civic architecture across America for decades: the neoclassical courthouses, libraries, and train stations of the early twentieth century. Burnham's Plan of Chicago, in 1909, grew out of it.
=== STOP The Obama Presidential Center @ 41.7864,-87.5862
Walk west across the park toward Stony Island Avenue, near 60th Street. At the western edge of Jackson Park rises the Obama Presidential Center, designed by the New York architects Tod Williams and Billie Tsien, with a stone tower that rises above the park.

Barack Obama came to Chicago in 1985 as a community organizer, working with churches on the Far South Side after the steel mills closed. He went to Harvard Law School and came back. In 1989, as a summer associate at a downtown law firm, he met his mentor, a young lawyer named Michelle Robinson, who grew up in the South Shore neighborhood, just south of here. On their first date, they went to the Art Institute, saw the Spike Lee film Do the Right Thing, and had ice cream at a Baskin-Robbins on 53rd Street in Hyde Park. A plaque on a boulder at 53rd and Dorchester marks the spot of their first kiss.

Obama taught constitutional law at the University of Chicago for twelve years. He was elected to the Illinois state senate from Hyde Park in 1996, and to the US Senate in 2004. In 2008, he gave his victory speech as president-elect in Grant Park.

The Obama family home is in the Kenwood neighborhood, just north of Hyde Park, behind security barriers.

The decision to build the center here in Jackson Park, on public parkland, was controversial, and it was fought in court for years by preservationists. Supporters said it would bring visitors and investment to the South Side.
=== STOP The Midway Plaisance @ 41.7869,-87.5937
Walk west from Stony Island Avenue along the broad green strip running between 59th and 60th Streets. This is the Midway Plaisance, a mile-long parkway connecting Jackson Park in the east to Washington Park in the west.

During the 1893 fair, this was where the fun was. While the White City was devoted to culture and industry, the Midway was a mile of amusements, restaurants, and exhibits of peoples from around the world: a Cairo street, an Irish village, a German beer garden, and the famous belly dancer called Little Egypt. That's why carnivals and fairs still call their amusement area the midway.

The star attraction was the Ferris wheel, built by a young engineer named George Washington Gale Ferris Junior, as America's answer to the Eiffel Tower, which had been built for the Paris fair of 1889. It stood here on the Midway. The wheel was more than two hundred and sixty feet tall, and each of its thirty-six cars could carry about sixty people. It was a sensation.

Look west, to the far end of the Midway at Cottage Grove Avenue, and you may see a massive concrete sculpture of dozens of figures, called the Fountain of Time, by the sculptor Lorado Taft, completed in 1922. It's based on a poem line: "Time goes, you say? Ah no! Alas, Time stays, we go."

Today, the Midway is the southern edge of the University of Chicago campus. In winter, there's a skating rink.

The original Ferris wheel didn't stay here long. After the fair, it was taken down and moved to the North Side, and then shipped to St. Louis for the 1904 World's Fair there. In 1906, it was blown up with dynamite and sold for scrap. Ferris himself had died in 1896, at thirty-seven, nearly broke, after years of lawsuits over the money he was owed for the wheel.

At the western end of the Midway is Washington Park, the South Side's other great park, also designed by Olmsted and his partner Calvert Vaux. In the park is the DuSable Black History Museum, named for Jean Baptiste Point DuSable, Chicago's first permanent settler. It was founded in 1961 by the artist and teacher Margaret Burroughs and her husband, in the parlor of their home in Bronzeville, and it's one of the oldest museums of African American history in the country.
=== STOP Rockefeller Chapel & the University @ 41.7885,-87.5967
Walk north from the Midway on Woodlawn Avenue to 59th Street. The huge Gothic stone church on the corner is Rockefeller Chapel, completed in 1928. Its tower holds a carillon of seventy-two bells, one of the largest in the world.

The University of Chicago was founded in 1890, backed by John D. Rockefeller, the founder of Standard Oil, and the American Baptist Education Society. Rockefeller gave tens of millions of dollars to the university over the years. Its first president, William Rainey Harper, a young biblical scholar, set out to build a great research university from scratch, and he hired famous professors from across the country. Classes began in 1892.

Look north into the main campus quadrangles. The university's buildings, beginning with designs by Henry Ives Cobb, were built in a Gothic style modeled on Oxford and Cambridge, with gray limestone, towers, gargoyles, and enclosed quadrangles.

The University of Chicago has had an enormous influence. It has been connected with around a hundred Nobel Prize winners. Its economists, known as the Chicago School, transformed economic thinking and policy in the twentieth century. Its sociologists studied the city of Chicago itself as a laboratory, pioneering the study of urban neighborhoods, and its scientists and scholars made breakthroughs in many fields, including the one we'll visit at the end of the tour.

For a time, the University of Chicago was a football power. It was a founding member of what became the Big Ten conference, and its coach, Amos Alonzo Stagg, was one of the inventors of the modern game. The first winner of the Heisman Trophy, in 1935, was a Chicago halfback named Jay Berwanger. But in 1939, the university's young president, Robert Maynard Hutchins, abolished the football team, saying it distracted from the university's real purpose. Hutchins also introduced the Great Books program, built around reading the classic works of Western thought, which influenced colleges across the country. Stagg Field, the stadium where the team had played, stood empty, which is how its stands came to be used for a secret experiment in 1942.

Rockefeller, a devout Baptist, gave the university tens of millions of dollars but never wanted it named after him, and he visited only a handful of times. He is said to have called it the best investment he ever made. The chapel's original name was the University Chapel. It was renamed for him after his death in 1937.
=== STOP Robie House @ 41.7898,-87.5960
Walk north on Woodlawn Avenue to 58th Street. The long, low brick house on the corner, with its dramatic roofs stretching out far beyond the walls, is the Frederick C. Robie House, designed by Frank Lloyd Wright and completed in 1910.

Robie was a young businessman who ran a company that made bicycles and supplies for the new automobile industry. He wanted a modern house, full of light, with privacy from the street, and Wright gave him a masterpiece.

This is the greatest example of the Prairie style, Wright's attempt to create an American architecture inspired by the flat, wide landscape of the Midwest. Look at how strongly horizontal everything is. The long, thin Roman bricks are laid with deep horizontal joints and nearly invisible vertical joints, so the walls look like layers of stone. The roofs cantilever out about twenty feet past their supports, held up by hidden steel beams. The main living space is raised above the street, behind a wall, so you can see out without being seen.

Inside, the rooms flow into one another around a central fireplace, instead of being separate boxes. Wright designed everything, from the furniture to the lamps and the art glass windows.

The house was threatened with demolition twice, including in 1957, when it was going to be torn down for a dormitory. Wright, then nearly ninety, came to Chicago to protest. It was saved, and it's now a museum that offers tours.

Frederick Robie got to enjoy his house for only about a year. In 1910, his father died and left large business debts, and Robie's company went into trouble at the same time that his marriage was falling apart. He sold the house in 1911. It passed through several owners and then to a theological seminary, which twice planned to demolish it. The house has since been restored to look as it did when the Robies moved in.
=== STOP Nuclear Energy & the First Reactor @ 41.7925,-87.6011
Walk north and west to Ellis Avenue, between 56th and 57th Streets. In front of a library building, there's a dark bronze sculpture, shaped something like a skull or a mushroom cloud. This is Nuclear Energy, by the British sculptor Henry Moore.

It marks the site where, on December second, 1942, scientists achieved the first controlled, self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction. It happened in a squash court under the stands of the university's old football stadium, Stagg Field, which stood here.

The experiment was part of the Manhattan Project, the American effort to build an atomic bomb during the Second World War. The team was led by the Italian physicist Enrico Fermi, who had fled fascist Italy. They built a pile of graphite blocks and uranium, called Chicago Pile-1, and slowly withdrew control rods made of cadmium, which absorbed neutrons, until the reaction became self-sustaining. A physicist stood by with an axe, ready to cut a rope that would release an emergency control rod. After the reaction ran for a few minutes, Fermi shut it down. One of the scientists phoned a colleague with a coded message: The Italian navigator has landed in the new world.

The sculpture was unveiled on the twenty-fifth anniversary, at exactly the minute the reaction began. Moore said he meant it to suggest both a human skull and the protective dome of a cathedral, the destructive and creative sides of nuclear power.

Many of the scientists who worked on the bomb were troubled by what they had made. In 1945, a group of them, centered here in Chicago, founded a magazine called the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, to warn the public about the dangers of nuclear weapons. In 1947, its cover featured a clock, set at seven minutes to midnight, to show how close the world was to catastrophe. That was the Doomsday Clock. The Bulletin is still based in Chicago, and every year its experts announce where the hands of the clock now stand.

This is the end of the Hyde Park tour.
