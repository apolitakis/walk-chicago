title: Bronzeville
area: South Side
blurb: The Black Metropolis of the Great Migration: Mies's Crown Hall on the site of the Mecca, gospel at Pilgrim Baptist, the Stroll, Louis Armstrong at the Sunset, Ebony and the Defender, the Victory Monument, and Ida B. Wells.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour explores Bronzeville, on the South Side, a few miles south of the Loop. In the first half of the twentieth century, it was one of the most important Black communities in America, a city within a city that newspapers and sociologists called the Black Metropolis.

Its story is the story of the Great Migration. Between the First World War and 1970, about six million Black Southerners moved north and west, fleeing segregation, lynching, and poverty, and looking for jobs in the industrial cities. Chicago was one of the main destinations. The Illinois Central Railroad ran straight up from Mississippi and Louisiana to Chicago, and the Chicago Defender, a Black newspaper smuggled into the South by Pullman porters, urged readers to come north. Chicago's Black population grew from about forty-four thousand in 1910 to more than two hundred thousand by 1930.

But the newcomers faced discrimination in Chicago too. White property owners used restrictive covenants, real estate practices, and violence to confine Black residents to a narrow strip on the South Side, called the Black Belt, running along State Street. In July 1919, tensions exploded in a race riot, set off when a Black teenager, Eugene Williams, drowned after being stoned by white men at a segregated beach. Thirty-eight people died.

Confined to a small area, Black Chicagoans built their own world. There were Black-owned banks, insurance companies, newspapers, cosmetics makers, churches, theaters, nightclubs, and hospitals. Jazz and blues flourished here. Writers, artists, and intellectuals made it a center of what's sometimes called the Chicago Black Renaissance, including the novelist Richard Wright, the poet Gwendolyn Brooks, and the dancer and choreographer Katherine Dunham.

The name Bronzeville was coined in the 1930s by an editor at the Chicago Bee newspaper, who held a contest to elect a Mayor of Bronzeville. After the Second World War, much of the neighborhood was cleared for public housing and institutions, and it declined, but in recent decades it's been rediscovered and restored.

We'll start at the Illinois Institute of Technology, walk east and south through the old heart of the community, past the home of Ida B. Wells, and end at the tomb of Stephen Douglas, near the lake.
=== STOP Crown Hall & the Mecca @ 41.8332,-87.6272
You're on the campus of the Illinois Institute of Technology, near State Street and 34th Street. In front of you, set back on a lawn, is a long, low glass building with a steel frame. This is S. R. Crown Hall, completed in 1956, designed by Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, and home to the university's architecture school.

Mies designed the whole campus, starting in the 1940s, as a grid of simple steel, glass, and brick buildings. Crown Hall is his masterpiece. Look at the roof. It's hung from four enormous steel plate girders that sit on top of the building, so the inside can be one vast, open room, with no columns at all. Mies called it universal space, a room that could be used for anything.

Now consider what stood here before. On this very site stood the Mecca Flats, a huge apartment building built in 1892 with interior courtyards and skylit atriums. By the 1910s, it had become home to Black families, and it was famous across the South Side, celebrated in a song called the Mecca Flat Blues. Over the years, it became badly overcrowded, and the institute, which owned it, demolished it in 1952 to expand the campus, despite protests from the tenants. The poet Gwendolyn Brooks later wrote a long poem about it, called In the Mecca.

Look east to the station at State Street, where the elevated Green Line passes through a stainless steel tube over a student center designed by the Dutch architect Rem Koolhaas, opened in 2003.

The university itself has a storied Chicago origin. In 1890, a young minister named Frank Gunsaulus preached a sermon on what he would do if he had a million dollars: build a school where young people of any background could learn practical skills for the new industrial age. In the congregation was Philip Armour, the meatpacker, who reportedly came up to him afterward and offered to fund it. The Armour Institute opened in 1893 and later merged with another school to become the Illinois Institute of Technology, which hired Mies van der Rohe in 1938 to lead its architecture program and design its new campus.
=== STOP Pilgrim Baptist Church @ 41.8345,-87.6215
Walk east on 33rd Street to Indiana Avenue. On the corner stands what remains of Pilgrim Baptist Church, a stone building with a massive, solid base.

It was built in 1890 and 1891 as a synagogue for Kehilath Anshe Ma'ariv, one of Chicago's oldest Jewish congregations, and it was designed by Dankmar Adler and Louis Sullivan. Adler's father had been the congregation's rabbi. The building was known for its superb acoustics and for Sullivan's ornament. As the Jewish community moved south and the neighborhood became Black, the building was sold in 1922 to Pilgrim Baptist Church.

It was here that gospel music was born. In the 1930s, the church's music director was Thomas A. Dorsey, a former blues pianist who had played under the name Georgia Tom. After the death of his wife and newborn son, he wrote Take My Hand, Precious Lord, one of the most famous gospel songs ever written. Dorsey fused the rhythms of blues and jazz with religious lyrics, and his music, at first controversial in more traditional churches, swept the country. Mahalia Jackson, who came to Chicago from New Orleans as a teenager, sang here and worked with Dorsey. She became the greatest gospel singer of her time.

In January 2006, a fire destroyed the church's roof and interior. Only the stone walls survive. There have been plans to build a museum of gospel music on the site.
=== STOP The Stroll & the Overton Building @ 41.8286,-87.6262
Walk west and south to State Street near 36th Street. In the 1910s and 1920s, the stretch of State Street between roughly 26th and 39th Streets was called the Stroll. It was the main street of Black Chicago, crowded day and night with people going to theaters, cabarets, dance halls, and saloons. Jazz musicians from New Orleans came here, including King Oliver, Jelly Roll Morton, and a young Louis Armstrong.

On the east side of State Street, at number 3619, is the Overton Hygienic Building, completed in the early 1920s. It was built by Anthony Overton, a businessman who made a fortune with a line of cosmetics for Black women, including a face powder called High-Brown. The building also housed the Douglass National Bank, one of the first nationally chartered banks owned by Black Americans, and other Black-owned businesses.

Next door, at 3647 South State, is the Chicago Bee Building, an Art Deco gem with green and cream terra cotta, completed around 1931. It housed the Chicago Bee, a newspaper Overton founded. Today, it's a branch of the Chicago Public Library.

Overton's businesses were hit hard by the Depression, and his bank failed in 1932. But his buildings show what Black entrepreneurs built in Bronzeville in a time when they were shut out of most of the economy.

Louis Armstrong arrived in Chicago by train from New Orleans in 1922, at twenty-one, to play second cornet in King Oliver's Creole Jazz Band at a dance hall called Lincoln Gardens, on 31st Street, a short distance northeast of here. The records they made in 1923 are among the first great jazz recordings. Later, the center of nightlife moved south, to 47th Street, where the Regal Theater, a grand movie palace and stage, presented Duke Ellington, Nat King Cole, Ella Fitzgerald, and later James Brown and the Jackson Five.

A few blocks south, at 39th Street, is Wendell Phillips High School, which in the 1920s became the first predominantly Black high school in Chicago. Its students included the singer and pianist Nat King Cole, the soul singer Sam Cooke, and the jazz singer Dinah Washington. And in the 1920s, a group of its former basketball players formed a team that played at the Savoy Ballroom on 47th Street. They became the Harlem Globetrotters. They had never been to Harlem; the name was chosen to tell audiences that the players were Black.
=== STOP The Sunset Café @ 41.8309,-87.6188
Walk east on 35th Street to Calumet Avenue. On the north side of the street, at 315 East 35th Street, stood the Sunset Café, one of the most famous jazz clubs of the 1920s.

The Sunset was a black and tan club, a place where Black and white audiences mixed, which was unusual and sometimes illegal at the time. Louis Armstrong played here in the 1920s, with Carroll Dickerson's orchestra and the pianist Earl Hines. The club was managed by Joe Glaser, who became Armstrong's manager for the rest of his career. Young white musicians, like Benny Goodman, Gene Krupa, and Bix Beiderbecke, came to listen and learn.

Chicago in the 1920s was the jazz capital of the world. Musicians had come up from New Orleans, many on the Illinois Central, and the music changed here, from collective improvisation to the age of great soloists, and Armstrong's recordings with his Hot Five and Hot Seven bands, made in Chicago, defined it.

The building that housed the Sunset survives, and it later became a hardware store. Those who have been inside say that some of the original murals from the club are still on the walls behind the store's shelves.
=== STOP Victory Monument & Supreme Life @ 41.8307,-87.6169
Walk east on 35th Street to Martin Luther King Drive. In the middle of the boulevard stands the Victory Monument, unveiled in 1928, to honor the soldiers of the Eighth Illinois Infantry, a Black National Guard regiment that fought in France in the First World War as the 370th Infantry. Look at the bronze panels showing a soldier, a mother, and a figure of Columbia. The statue of a soldier on top was added in 1936. Each August, a few blocks south on King Drive, the Bud Billiken Parade, one of the largest African American parades in the country, celebrates the start of the school year.

On the northeast corner of the intersection, look at the building at 3501 South King Drive. This was the headquarters of the Supreme Liberty Life Insurance Company, one of the largest Black-owned businesses in the North. In the 1930s, a young man named John H. Johnson worked as an office boy here. In 1942, using the company's mailing list and a five-hundred-dollar loan secured by his mother's furniture, he launched a magazine called Negro Digest. In 1945, he started Ebony, which became the most widely read Black magazine in the world, and later Jet. Johnson Publishing became one of the most important Black businesses in America, and it was based in Chicago.

A few blocks north, at 3435 South Indiana Avenue, was the longtime headquarters of the Chicago Defender. Founded in 1905 by Robert S. Abbott, it campaigned against lynching and segregation and urged Southern Black families to come north, playing a major role in the Great Migration. In 1956, it became a daily paper.
=== STOP Ida B. Wells @ 41.8286,-87.6176
Walk south on Martin Luther King Drive to number 3624, a large stone house with a turret on the west side of the boulevard. This was the home of Ida B. Wells from 1919 to 1929.

Ida B. Wells was born into slavery in Mississippi in 1862. She became a teacher and then a journalist in Memphis, and in 1892, after three of her friends were lynched, she began investigating lynchings across the South. Her reporting showed that the usual justifications given for lynching were usually false, and that lynching was a tool of terror to keep Black Americans down. A mob destroyed the offices of her newspaper, and she was forced to leave Memphis.

She settled in Chicago, married a lawyer and newspaper publisher named Ferdinand Barnett, and raised a family while continuing her crusade. She was a founding member of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, and in 1913 she founded the Alpha Suffrage Club, one of the first suffrage clubs for Black women. When the national suffrage parade in Washington tried to make Black women march at the back, she stepped out of the crowd and joined the Illinois delegation in the front.

In 2020, she was awarded a posthumous Pulitzer Prize. A short walk south and east, at 37th Street and Langley Avenue, on the site of the Ida B. Wells Homes public housing project, stands a monument to her by the Chicago sculptor Richard Hunt, called the Light of Truth, dedicated in 2021.

Bronzeville's writers made the neighborhood famous. Richard Wright, who came from Mississippi, set his novel Native Son, published in 1940, in these streets. The poet Gwendolyn Brooks, who grew up here, wrote about the kitchenette apartments and street corners of what she called Bronzeville, and in 1950 she became the first Black writer to win a Pulitzer Prize. A few blocks southwest, at 3831 South Michigan Avenue, is the South Side Community Art Center, opened in 1940 as part of a New Deal arts program, and dedicated the next year by Eleanor Roosevelt. It's still a working art center, the last of its kind from that program.

The fight against housing segregation was waged here too. In 1937, a real estate broker named Carl Hansberry bought a house in a white neighborhood just south of Bronzeville, defying a covenant that banned Black residents. His family was met with a hostile mob, and the case went all the way to the Supreme Court, which ruled in the family's favor in 1940. His daughter, Lorraine Hansberry, drew on those memories for her play A Raisin in the Sun, which opened on Broadway in 1959, the first play by a Black woman ever produced there.
=== STOP Stephen A. Douglas Tomb @ 41.8318,-87.6084
Walk east on 35th Street, across Cottage Grove Avenue, to the small park near the lakefront. Here, on top of a tall granite column, stands a bronze statue of Stephen A. Douglas, one of the most powerful politicians in America before the Civil War. He's buried in the tomb at its base.

Douglas was a senator from Illinois, nicknamed the Little Giant for his short stature and enormous energy. He's remembered today mostly as Abraham Lincoln's great rival. In 1858, they ran against each other for the Senate, and their seven debates across Illinois, about whether slavery should be allowed to spread into the western territories, drew huge crowds and national attention. Douglas won the Senate seat. Two years later, they ran for president, and Lincoln won. When the Civil War began, Douglas rallied his supporters behind the Union. He died in Chicago in June 1861, at forty-eight.

Douglas had an estate here, on the lake, and he gave land nearby for the first University of Chicago, founded in 1857, which went bankrupt in the 1880s. The current university, in Hyde Park, was founded separately a few years later.

After his death, the land just west of here became Camp Douglas, a Union army training camp, and then a prison camp for captured Confederate soldiers. Conditions were terrible, with overcrowding, cold, and disease, and thousands of prisoners died here, by some estimates more than four thousand. Many of them are buried in a mass grave at Oak Woods Cemetery, a few miles south.

The statue on top of the column is by Leonard Volk, a sculptor who was related to Douglas by marriage. In 1860, Volk also made a plaster life mask of Douglas's rival, Abraham Lincoln, which is one of the most accurate records we have of Lincoln's face. Saint-Gaudens used a cast of it for the Standing Lincoln in Lincoln Park.

This is the end of the Bronzeville tour.
