=== TOUR
id: west-loop
title: West Loop, Haymarket & Hull House
area: West & Northwest Sides
blurb: Jane Addams's settlement house, Greektown, the church that survived the fire, Union Station, the Haymarket bombing, and the meatpacking blocks now home to Google, McDonald's and Chicago's restaurant row.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour explores the Near West Side, just across the South Branch of the river from the Loop. It's a part of the city where some of the most important chapters of American labor and social history took place, and where an old industrial district has been remade in the last twenty years.

In the late nineteenth century, the Near West Side was one of the most crowded immigrant neighborhoods in the country. Irish, Germans, Bohemians, Italians, Greeks, Russian and Polish Jews, and many others lived packed into small wooden houses and tenements, working in the factories, rail yards, and garment shops nearby. The streets were unpaved, the garbage was rarely collected, and disease was common.

It was here, in 1889, that Jane Addams opened Hull House, which became the most famous settlement house in America and a laboratory for reforms that changed the country, from child labor laws to playgrounds to juvenile courts.

It was here too, in 1886, that the Haymarket bombing took place, an event that shook the labor movement and is remembered around the world each year on May first.

The area north of Randolph Street was for a century the city's wholesale market district: produce on Randolph Street, and meat along Fulton Market, with cold storage warehouses, packing houses, and loading docks. Since the 2000s, that district has been transformed. Restaurants moved into old warehouses on Randolph, and then technology and corporate headquarters arrived, including Google and McDonald's.

We'll start where the Great Fire of 1871 began, walk west to Hull House at the University of Illinois Chicago, then north up Halsted Street through Greektown, past Old Saint Patrick's Church and Union Station, to the Haymarket, and finish in Fulton Market.
=== STOP Where the Great Fire Began @ 41.8695,-87.6419
You're on DeKoven Street, just west of Jefferson Street, in front of the Chicago Fire Academy, where the city trains its firefighters. Look for the tall bronze sculpture of twisting flames in front of the building, called the Pillar of Fire. It marks the spot where, on the night of October eighth, 1871, the Great Chicago Fire began, in a barn behind the cottage of Patrick and Catherine O'Leary.

The summer and fall of 1871 had been extremely dry. Chicago was a city of wood: wooden houses, wooden sidewalks, wooden barns full of hay, lumber yards, and even some streets paved with wooden blocks. The night before, a big fire had burned several blocks just north of here, and the exhausted firefighters were slow to respond. A strong wind from the southwest drove the flames north and east, into the heart of the city. The fire jumped the South Branch of the river, burned through the downtown, leapt the main river, and burned through the North Side for more than a day. About three hundred people died, around seventeen thousand buildings were destroyed, and about a hundred thousand people, a third of the city, were left homeless.

The story that Mrs. O'Leary's cow kicked over a lantern was made up by a newspaper reporter named Michael Ahern, who admitted it many years later. Catherine O'Leary, an Irish immigrant, was blamed for the rest of her life and hounded by reporters. Other theories have pointed to a neighbor nicknamed Peg Leg Sullivan, who first raised the alarm, or to men gambling in the barn. No one really knows. In 1997, the city council formally exonerated Mrs. O'Leary and her cow. Oddly enough, the O'Leary cottage itself survived. The wind carried the flames away from it.

It's fitting that the Fire Academy stands here. The disaster led Chicago to rebuild in brick, stone, and iron, and to rethink how it fought fires.
=== STOP Hull House @ 41.8717,-87.6474
You are at the corner of Halsted Street and Polk Street, in front of a red brick Italianate mansion with a wide porch. This is Hull House, built in 1856 as the country home of a real estate developer named Charles Hull, when this was the edge of the city.

By 1889, the neighborhood around it had become a crowded immigrant slum. That year, two young women, Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr, rented the house and moved in. Their idea, borrowed from a settlement house in London called Toynbee Hall, was that educated people should live among the poor as neighbors, learning about their needs and working with them on solutions.

Hull House grew into a complex of thirteen buildings covering most of a city block, with a kindergarten, a day nursery, a gymnasium, a theater, a music school, an art gallery, a public kitchen, and classes in English and citizenship. The women who lived and worked here, including Florence Kelley, Julia Lathrop, and the physician Alice Hamilton, became some of the most influential reformers in the country. They investigated child labor, sweatshops, and industrial disease, and they pushed for laws to address them. Hull House residents helped create the country's first juvenile court, here in Chicago in 1899.

Jane Addams became one of the most famous women in the world. She was also a committed pacifist, which made her deeply unpopular during the First World War. In 1931, she became the first American woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize.

In the 1960s, most of the Hull House complex was demolished to build the University of Illinois campus, over the protests of the neighborhood. Only the original mansion and the residents' dining hall survive, and they're now a museum.

Walk a block west and you'll be on Taylor Street, the heart of Little Italy, the remnant of the Italian neighborhood that once surrounded Hull House. Italian immigrants arrived in large numbers from the 1880s, and Hull House offered them English classes, kindergartens, and help with work. Much of the neighborhood was cleared for the university and the expressway, but Taylor Street still has old Italian restaurants, bakeries, the Shrine of Our Lady of Pompeii, and a lemonade stand, Mario's, that has been selling Italian ice from a sidewalk window every summer since the 1950s.
=== STOP Greektown & Old Saint Patrick's @ 41.8792,-87.6443
Walk north on Halsted Street, under the Eisenhower Expressway, into Greektown, the stretch of Halsted between Van Buren and Madison lined with Greek restaurants.

Greek immigrants settled on the Near West Side in large numbers in the early twentieth century, and many of them ran restaurants, candy shops, and produce stands. When the expressway and the university displaced much of the old Greek neighborhood, the restaurants on this strip remained. Greektown is said to be the birthplace of flaming saganaki, the cheese that's set alight at the table to cries of Opa. A restaurant here called the Parthenon claimed to have started the practice in the late 1960s.

Now turn east on Adams Street to Des Plaines Street. The church on the corner, with two towers of different designs, is Old Saint Patrick's Church. It was dedicated in 1856, and it survived the Great Fire, which burned to the north and east of here. That makes it the oldest public building in Chicago. It was built by Irish immigrants, many of whom had fled the famine.

Look at the two towers. One has a pointed spire, the other an onion dome. They're often said to represent the Western and Eastern traditions of the church. If you can go inside, the interior is a masterpiece of Celtic Revival art: stained glass windows and painted walls designed by the artist Thomas O'Shaughnessy in the early twentieth century, with intricate designs based on the illuminated Book of Kells.

Old Saint Pat's is known today for a hugely popular summer street party, which it has held since the 1980s, and for being one of the liveliest parishes downtown.
=== STOP Union Station @ 41.8787,-87.6403
Walk east on Adams Street to Canal Street. On your right is Union Station, the great railroad terminal of Chicago.

Chicago is the railroad capital of North America. In the age of the passenger train, almost every rail trip across the country involved changing trains in Chicago, because lines from the East ended here and lines to the West began here. Travelers had to cross the city, often by taxi or transfer coach, from one station to another.

Union Station opened in 1925, designed by the firm of Graham, Anderson, Probst and White, the successors of Daniel Burnham. Walk inside to the Great Hall, a vast waiting room with a barrel-vaulted skylight soaring about a hundred feet above the floor, Corinthian columns, and wooden benches. It's one of the grandest rooms in the city.

The station has appeared in many films. The most famous scene is in The Untouchables, from 1987, when a baby carriage bounces down the grand staircase during a shootout, a homage to a famous scene in the Russian film Battleship Potemkin.

Today, Union Station is Amtrak's hub for the Midwest and the busiest station for the Metra commuter trains. Long-distance trains still leave from here for New York, New Orleans, Seattle, Los Angeles, and San Francisco.

The first railroad out of Chicago, the Galena and Chicago Union, ran its first train in 1848 on a few miles of track heading west. Within a decade, Chicago was the hub of the American railroad network, and it still is. More freight lines meet here than anywhere else in North America, and about a quarter of all freight rail traffic in the country passes through the Chicago area. Union Station once had a matching concourse building on the east side of Canal Street, with a vast glass and steel roof. It was demolished in 1969 and replaced by an office tower, a loss that helped spur the preservation movement in Chicago.
=== STOP Haymarket Square @ 41.8849,-87.6442
Walk north on Des Plaines Street, across Washington and Randolph. Just north of Randolph, on the east side of the street, is a bronze sculpture of a wagon with figures standing on it. This marks the site of the Haymarket affair.

In the 1880s, a nationwide movement was campaigning for an eight-hour workday. On May first, 1886, tens of thousands of workers struck in Chicago. On May third, police fired on strikers at the McCormick Reaper Works, killing at least two. Anarchist leaders called a protest meeting here, at the Haymarket, for the evening of May fourth.

The meeting was peaceful and smaller than expected. The mayor, Carter Harrison, attended for a while and then went home. But as the last speaker was finishing, around a hundred and eighty police officers marched in to break up the crowd. Someone threw a bomb into their ranks. It killed one officer instantly, and in the chaos that followed, police opened fire. Seven officers died, most of them from the bomb or from police bullets fired in confusion, and at least four civilians were killed.

The bomb thrower was never identified. Eight anarchists were tried and convicted, largely for their speeches and writings rather than for any proof that they were involved in the bombing. Four were hanged in November 1887, including Albert Parsons, the husband of Lucy Parsons. Another took his own life in jail. In 1893, Governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned the three survivors, calling the trial unjust, an act that ended his political career.

The Haymarket affair became a symbol for labor movements around the world, and it's one reason that International Workers' Day is celebrated on May first in many countries. This sculpture, by Mary Brogger, was dedicated in 2004. A statue of a police officer that used to stand here was blown up twice by radicals in 1969 and 1970, and it now stands at police headquarters.
=== STOP Restaurant Row & What Chicago Eats @ 41.8845,-87.6490
Walk west on Randolph Street. This is Restaurant Row, one of the city's busiest dining strips. For a century, this stretch was the Randolph Street Market, Chicago's wholesale produce market, where farmers and wholesalers sold fruit and vegetables from wagons and then from trucks. Starting in the late 1990s, chefs began opening restaurants in the old market buildings, and the street became one of the most celebrated restaurant districts in the country.

This is a good place to think about how much of what America eats came out of Chicago. Kraft cheese started in 1903, when James L. Kraft began selling cheese to Chicago grocers from a horse-drawn wagon. Cracker Jack was popularized by the Rueckheim brothers, German immigrants who sold popcorn on Chicago's streets. The Twinkie was invented in the Chicago area in 1930. The Baby Ruth candy bar and Lemonheads are Chicago products. For decades, when the wind blew right, the Loop smelled of chocolate from the Blommer Chocolate factory, on the river just north of here.

Then there's the food Chicago is famous for. The Chicago-style hot dog is an all-beef frankfurter on a poppy seed bun, dragged through the garden, as people say: yellow mustard, bright green relish, chopped onion, tomato slices, a pickle spear, sport peppers, and celery salt, but never ketchup. The Italian beef sandwich, thin-sliced roast beef soaked in its juices on a long roll, topped with sweet or hot peppers, was born in the Italian neighborhoods of the Near West Side. And deep-dish pizza was created in the 1940s, by most accounts at Pizzeria Uno, near Michigan Avenue, though there are rival claims.

In the last few decades, Chicago has also become one of the world's great cities for fine dining, from Charlie Trotter's, which opened in 1987, to Alinea, in Lincoln Park, and the restaurants of this street.
=== STOP Fulton Market @ 41.8869,-87.6524
Walk west on Randolph Street. This is Restaurant Row, one of the city's busiest dining strips. For a century, this stretch was the Randolph Street Market, Chicago's wholesale produce market, where farmers and wholesalers sold fruit and vegetables from wagons and then from trucks.

Walk one block north to Fulton Market, the street of the old meatpacking district. Look at the low brick buildings with their steel canopies over the sidewalks, built to shelter loading docks where carcasses were moved into cold storage. A few meat and food wholesalers are still here. But in the last fifteen years, the district has transformed into one of the most expensive parts of the city.

At 1000 West Fulton Market, on the corner of Morgan Street, look for the tall brick building that was once the Fulton Market Cold Storage warehouse. Since 2015, it's been Google's headquarters in Chicago. Google's arrival is widely credited with setting off the transformation of the neighborhood, as other companies followed.

The area also became a center for hotels, galleries, and restaurants. Chicago's famous chefs opened restaurants in the old warehouses, and the neighborhood is now one of the most important dining destinations in the country.
=== STOP McDonald's Headquarters & Harpo Studios @ 41.8839,-87.6536
Walk south on Carpenter Street to Washington Boulevard. The glass building at 110 North Carpenter Street is the global headquarters of McDonald's, which moved here from its suburban campus in Oak Brook in 2018.

McDonald's has deep roots in the Chicago area. Ray Kroc, a milkshake mixer salesman from the Chicago suburb of Oak Park, met the McDonald brothers in California and in 1955 opened his first franchise restaurant in Des Plaines, Illinois, just northwest of Chicago. From there he built it into the biggest restaurant chain in the world. On the ground floor here, there's a McDonald's restaurant that serves a rotating menu of items from McDonald's around the world.

This site has an even more famous past. It was the home of Harpo Studios, owned by Oprah Winfrey. Harpo is Oprah spelled backwards. From 1990 to 2011, The Oprah Winfrey Show was taped here, in a converted old armory, and it was the most watched talk show in American television history. Oprah came to Chicago in 1984 to host a struggling morning show, AM Chicago, and within a year it had become her show. She was one of the most influential people in American media, and she made Chicago the home of her empire.

The building Harpo used had a grim earlier history. It was originally the Second Regiment Armory, and in July 1915, after the Eastland capsized in the Chicago River, it served as the city's temporary morgue, where families came to identify hundreds of bodies laid out on the floor. Some of the people who worked for Harpo said the building was haunted, and the story was told for years.

Harpo's arrival in 1990 was a sign of the West Loop's future. When Oprah moved here, the area was full of warehouses and empty lots, and few people lived here. Harpo Studios closed in 2015, and its buildings were torn down to make way for the McDonald's headquarters. Today, the West Loop is one of the fastest-growing neighborhoods in the city.

This is the end of the West Loop tour. The Morgan station on the Green and Pink Lines, a couple of blocks north on Lake Street, will take you back to the Loop.
