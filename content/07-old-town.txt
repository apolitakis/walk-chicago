title: Old Town
area: North Side
blurb: German truck farmers, a church that rose from the fire, cottages and courtyards, the Old Town School of Folk Music, and the comedy theater that trained a generation.
=== OVERVIEW
This short tour explores Old Town, a small neighborhood of narrow streets and old cottages just west of Lincoln Park, around the crossing of North Avenue and Wells Street.

In the 1850s and 1860s, this was the northern edge of Chicago, and it was settled largely by German immigrants. Many of them were truck farmers who grew vegetables for the city's markets, and the area was nicknamed the Cabbage Patch. The heart of the community was a German Catholic parish, Saint Michael's, which still towers over the neighborhood.

In October 1871, the Great Fire burned all the way up here. Nearly everything was lost, except for the shell of Saint Michael's Church. The residents rebuilt quickly, many of them in small, modest cottages on narrow lots, and a lot of those cottages are still here, which gives the neighborhood its intimate, village-like scale.

The neighborhood went through hard times in the early twentieth century, and then in the 1950s and 1960s, it was rediscovered. Artists, writers, and young people moved into the cheap old houses. Wells Street became a strip of coffee houses, folk clubs, bars, and head shops, Chicago's answer to Greenwich Village and Haight-Ashbury. The name Old Town itself was popularized by an art fair, the Old Town Art Fair, which started in 1950 and is still held every June.

Two institutions that came out of that era are world famous: the Second City comedy theater, which opened in 1959, and the Old Town School of Folk Music, which opened in 1957. We'll visit the first and pass where the second began.

We will start at the Moody Church, at Clark Street and North Avenue, walk west to Wells Street, wind through the Old Town Triangle, and finish at Saint Michael's and the Buddhist temple beside it.
=== STOP The Moody Church @ 41.9120,-87.6327
You're at the corner of Clark Street and North Avenue, beside a massive brick church with arched windows and a broad, tiled roof, in a style inspired by the early churches of Byzantium and Italy. This is the Moody Church, completed in 1925. Its sanctuary seats close to four thousand people, which made it one of the largest Protestant churches in America.

It's named for Dwight L. Moody, one of the most famous preachers of the nineteenth century. Moody came to Chicago from Massachusetts in 1856, at nineteen, to sell shoes. He was a born salesman, and he soon turned his talents to religion, gathering poor children from the streets of the North Side into a Sunday school that grew into a church. After the Great Fire destroyed it, he rebuilt, and then he and the gospel singer Ira Sankey went on preaching tours of Britain and America that drew enormous crowds. He was, in a sense, the first modern mass evangelist.

In 1886, Moody founded a school to train ordinary men and women as missionaries and Bible teachers. It became the Moody Bible Institute, which is still in the Near North Side, and which started one of the first Christian radio stations in the country in the 1920s. The church that bears his name has been here since 1925.

Chicago's history is full of businessmen like Moody who brought the same energy to religion and reform that others brought to grain and steel. As you walk west along North Avenue, you're entering the old German North Side.
=== STOP The Second City @ 41.9117,-87.6348
You are on Wells Street, just north of North Avenue. The theater at 1616 North Wells, with its ornate stone facade, is the home of the Second City, the most influential comedy theater in America.

It opened in December 1959, a few blocks north on Wells Street, and moved here in 1967. It was founded by Bernard Sahlins, Howard Alk, and Paul Sills. The name was a joke, borrowed from a snide series of magazine articles by the New Yorker writer A. J. Liebling, who had called Chicago the Second City. Its roots go back a few years earlier to a group called the Compass Players, founded by students and former students around the University of Chicago, which pioneered a new kind of theater built on improvisation. Many of the improvisation games came from Viola Spolin, a teacher who happened to be Paul Sills's mother.

Look at the stone facade with its carved figures. It was salvaged from an older building downtown, the Chicago Garrick Theater, designed by Adler and Sullivan, which was demolished in 1961. Some of its ornament was reused here.

The list of performers who came through the Second City is astonishing: Alan Arkin, Joan Rivers, John Belushi, Bill Murray, Gilda Radner, Dan Aykroyd, John Candy, Mike Myers, Tina Fey, Amy Poehler, Stephen Colbert, Steve Carell, and many more. When Saturday Night Live started in 1975, a large part of its early cast came from Second City.

Next door is Piper's Alley, a passage named for Henry Piper, who ran a bakery here in the late nineteenth century. In the 1960s it was a hippie bazaar, and today it holds the theater's training center and other stages.

Second City is only part of Chicago's comedy story. In 1981, a producer named Charna Halpern founded a theater that became known as iO, and she was soon joined by a brilliant, eccentric improviser named Del Close. Together they developed long-form improvisation, in which a team spins a whole evening out of a single audience suggestion. Close taught many of the comedians who went on to Saturday Night Live and beyond. Today Chicago has dozens of improv theaters and training centers, and thousands of students take classes every year, hoping to follow the same path.
=== STOP Wells Street & the Folk Revival @ 41.9108,-87.6372
Walk back to North Avenue and west toward Orleans Street. In the 1960s and 1970s, Wells Street behind you and the blocks around here were crowded with folk music clubs, coffee houses, and bars.

One of the most important places was the Earl of Old Town, a bar and folk club on Wells Street, across from the Second City. It was where Steve Goodman, who wrote the song City of New Orleans, and his friend John Prine, a mailman from the western suburbs, played their early sets. Prine had been writing songs in his head while walking his mail route.

Right about here, at 333 West North Avenue, the Old Town School of Folk Music opened in December 1957. It was founded by Win Stracke, a folk singer, and Frank Hamilton, a guitar teacher. The school's idea was that anyone could learn to play, in group classes, by ear, and that folk music belonged to everyone. It grew into one of the largest community music schools in the country, and it has since moved to bigger buildings in the Lincoln Square neighborhood, but it began here.

Back toward Wells, at 219 West North Avenue, is the Old Town Ale House, a bar that has been open since 1958, famous as a hangout for writers, reporters, and actors, including the film critic Roger Ebert. Its walls are covered with paintings by its late owner, many of them caricatures of politicians.

Today, Wells Street is more upscale, with restaurants and boutiques, but the old storefronts are still there, and the street remains lively at night.

Steve Goodman was a lifelong, long-suffering Cubs fan. He wrote a song called A Dying Cub Fan's Last Request, and in 1984, he wrote Go, Cubs, Go, which is still played at Wrigley Field after every Cubs win. He died of leukemia that fall, just days before the Cubs clinched their first championship of any kind in almost forty years, and some of his ashes were said to have been scattered at Wrigley Field. John Prine went on to become one of the most admired songwriters in America, and he played Chicago for the rest of his life.

Old Town had jazz, too. A few blocks south, on Wells Street, was a club called the Plugged Nickel, where Miles Davis and his great quintet played a famous run of shows in December 1965. The recordings, released years later, are considered some of the finest live jazz ever captured.
=== STOP Crilly Court & the Old Town Triangle @ 41.9134,-87.6359
Walk north to Eugenie Street and look for a short street called Crilly Court, running north between Eugenie Street and Saint Paul Avenue.

It was developed in the mid-1880s by Daniel Crilly, a builder who wanted to create a little enclave of rowhouses and flats in the middle of the block. On one side are brick rowhouses, and on the other, flats with balconies. Look for the names carved over doorways; Crilly named some of the buildings after his children.

This part of Old Town is known as the Old Town Triangle, a district bounded by North Avenue, Clark Street, and the diagonal of Lincoln Avenue. Wander the narrow streets around here, such as Menomonee, Wisconsin, and Orleans, and you'll see a remarkable variety of houses: small wooden worker's cottages built in the years after the fire, brick Italianate rowhouses, and large Victorian homes. Some cottages sit below the level of the sidewalk. That's because, in the decades after they were built, the city raised the streets to put in sewers, and some houses were never jacked up to match.

At 1710 North Crilly Court, the house where Henry Gerber lived in 1924 is a city landmark. He founded the Society for Human Rights, the earliest documented gay rights organization in the United States, and it was shut down by police within months.

Look for the small, tucked-away gardens and alleys, which make this one of the most charming places to wander in Chicago.

In the 1960s, the Triangle was one of the first neighborhoods in Chicago to fight back against urban renewal, which had leveled large parts of the nearby Near North Side. Residents organized and won protection for the old houses, and the area is now a city landmark district.

Henry Gerber was a German immigrant and a veteran of the American army of occupation in Germany after the First World War, where he'd seen a gay rights movement taking shape. He came back to Chicago determined to start one here. The state of Illinois gave his society a charter in December 1924. Within months, police raided his home, arrested him, and seized his papers. He lost his job at the post office and his savings, but he never stopped writing and arguing for gay rights. His house was made a city landmark in 2001, and in 2015, it was named a National Historic Landmark.
=== STOP Saint Michael's Church @ 41.9120,-87.6406
Walk west to Cleveland Avenue and Eugenie Street. In front of you stands Saint Michael's Church, with its tall brick bell tower topped with a green copper spire.

The church was built by German immigrants and completed in 1869. It was one of the largest churches in the city. Two years later, the Great Fire roared through, and Saint Michael's burned. But its thick brick walls survived, and the parishioners, many of whom had lost everything, rebuilt the church inside those same walls. It reopened in 1873. The bell tower was raised to its current height afterward.

There is a well-known saying here that you're a true Old Towner only if you were born within the sound of Saint Michael's bells. That's an echo of the old London saying about the bells of Saint Mary-le-Bow and Cockneys.

Step inside if it's open. The interior is a feast of German Baroque decoration: carved altars, stained glass made in Munich, murals, and statues. For generations, masses here were said in German.

The parish is run by the Redemptorist order, which has served here since the 1860s. The neighborhood around it has changed many times over, from German farmers to artists and hippies and now affluent families, but Saint Michael's is still the anchor of Old Town.

The fire of 1871 burned north for more than a day, through Old Town and into what's now Lincoln Park. It finally died out near Fullerton Avenue, about a mile north of here, when it ran out of buildings to burn and a light rain began to fall. Thousands of people fled into the cemetery that then occupied the south end of Lincoln Park, and some waded into the lake to escape the heat. The burned district stretched about four miles from south to north.
=== STOP The Midwest Buddhist Temple @ 41.9144,-87.6399
Walk a block north to Menomonee Street. At number 435, behind a garden, is a low building with a sweeping, Japanese-style roof. This is the Midwest Buddhist Temple, built in 1971.

Its story begins in the Second World War. In 1942, after Pearl Harbor, the federal government forced about a hundred and twenty thousand people of Japanese ancestry, most of them American citizens, out of their homes on the West Coast and into incarceration camps in the interior. Starting in 1943, the government began releasing some of them on the condition that they resettle away from the coast. Chicago, with its jobs and its distance from the Pacific, became the biggest destination. By the end of the war, about twenty thousand Japanese Americans had moved here.

Many of them settled on the Near North Side, around Clark Street and Division Street, and later in Lakeview and Uptown. In 1944, a group of them founded a Buddhist congregation in Chicago, which eventually built this temple here in Old Town. Its members also helped revive the neighborhood in the 1960s and 1970s.

The temple's best-known event is the Ginza Holiday, a Japanese cultural festival held each summer, with food, taiko drumming, martial arts, and traditional crafts. It's a small, quiet reminder of a chapter of Chicago's history that's often forgotten.

This is the end of the Old Town tour. Lincoln Park is a few blocks to the east, where the next tour begins.
