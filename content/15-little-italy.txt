blurb: The open-air market where Chicago's blues went electric, the police station from Hill Street Blues, the church that survived the Great Fire, Taylor Street's Italian lemonade, the first public housing, and the hospital that invented the blood bank.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour explores the Near West Side, just southwest of the Loop, around Halsted Street, Roosevelt Road, and Taylor Street. Few places in America have seen so many immigrant communities come and go, and few have been so completely remade, again and again.

In the late nineteenth century, the Near West Side filled with the poorest newcomers to the city: Irish and Germans first, then Bohemians, and then, in the 1880s and 1890s, Italians, Greeks, and Jews from Eastern Europe. They crowded into wooden cottages and tenements, often several families to a house, with outhouses in the yards and the smells of the river and the factories in the air. In 1889, Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr opened Hull House, on Halsted Street, to serve them.

The Jewish community centered on Maxwell Street and its famous open-air market. The Italians centered on Taylor Street, which is still called Little Italy. The Greeks settled to the north, along Halsted Street, in what became Greektown. In the twentieth century, Mexican families and Black migrants from the South moved in, as the earlier groups moved on to other neighborhoods.

Then, starting in the 1930s, government planners began to clear the neighborhood. First came public housing, then the expressways, and then, in the 1960s, the new Chicago campus of the University of Illinois, which wiped out much of the old Italian neighborhood and most of Hull House. In the 1990s, the university expanded again, south over Maxwell Street. Yet pieces of the old Near West Side survive: churches, a police station, an Italian lemonade stand, and a sausage stand that never closes.

We'll start at Maxwell Street, walk north to Holy Family Church and Taylor Street, and then west to the old Cook County Hospital.
=== STOP Maxwell Street @ 41.8646,-87.6470
You're on Halsted Street at Maxwell Street, about a mile southwest of the Loop. Look around: you'll see university dormitories, shops, and parking garages. But for about a century, this was the site of the Maxwell Street Market, the most famous open-air market in America, and one of the birthplaces of the Chicago blues.

The market grew up in the 1880s, when Jewish immigrants from Eastern Europe settled here and began selling goods from pushcarts and stands along the street. By the early twentieth century, Maxwell Street was a crowded bazaar where you could buy almost anything, new, used, or stolen: clothes, shoes, tools, pots and pans, live chickens, and hardware, from peddlers shouting and haggling in Yiddish, English, and a dozen other languages. People used to say that you could have your hubcaps stolen at one end of the street and buy them back at the other. Sunday mornings were the busiest, and the crowds were enormous.

As the Jewish families prospered and moved west, Black migrants from the South moved in, and in the 1940s and 1950s, musicians from the Mississippi Delta came to play on the street for tips. To be heard over the noise of the crowds, they plugged electric guitars and amplifiers into outlets in the storefronts, with long extension cords. Some historians say that's one reason the Chicago blues became so loud and so electric. Little Walter, Bo Diddley, and many other musicians played here when they were starting out. In the 1980 movie The Blues Brothers, John Lee Hooker plays on Maxwell Street, and Aretha Franklin sings in a diner nearby.

Maxwell Street also gave Chicago one of its signature foods: the Maxwell Street Polish, a grilled Polish sausage on a bun, piled with grilled onions, yellow mustard, and hot sport peppers. It's credited to a Macedonian immigrant named Jimmy Stefanovic, who took over a hot dog stand at the corner of Maxwell and Halsted in 1939. His stand, Jim's Original, has moved a block east, to Union Avenue, but it still serves Polish sausages and pork chop sandwiches, around the clock.

In 1994, the city closed the old market and moved it east, toward the river, to make way for the expansion of the University of Illinois at Chicago. Blues fans, historians, and preservationists fought to save the street, but almost all of its buildings were torn down. Look for the few old facades that were saved along Halsted Street, and for the plaques that tell the market's story.
=== STOP The Maxwell Street Police Station @ 41.8645,-87.6507
Walk west along Maxwell Street to number 943, a sturdy old building of brick and stone with a big arched doorway. This is the old Maxwell Street police station, built in 1888. For decades, it was the headquarters of one of the busiest police districts in Chicago, in what was then one of its poorest and most crowded neighborhoods. The newspapers called the district Bloody Maxwell.

If the building looks familiar, you may have seen it on television. In the 1980s, it appeared in the opening credits of the police drama Hill Street Blues, as the outside of the show's fictional station house. Today, it's the headquarters of the university's police department.

The streets around here produced an extraordinary number of famous people, many of them the children of poor Jewish immigrants. Benny Goodman, the King of Swing, grew up nearby and learned to play the clarinet in the boys' band at Hull House. Arthur Goldberg, the son of a peddler, grew up here and became a justice of the Supreme Court. And so did Jacob Rubenstein, who changed his name to Jack Ruby, ran nightclubs in Dallas, and in 1963 shot Lee Harvey Oswald, the assassin of President Kennedy.

By the 1920s, most of the Jewish families had moved west, to Lawndale and beyond, but the market stayed, and so did many of the Jewish merchants, who kept their shops on the street and drove in every morning. For decades, Maxwell Street was a place where Black and white, rich and poor, immigrant and native-born Chicagoans rubbed shoulders, in a city that was otherwise sharply divided.
=== STOP Holy Family Church & Saint Ignatius @ 41.8675,-87.6547
Walk north to Roosevelt Road and west to May Street. The tall Gothic church with the slender spire is Holy Family Church, and next door, the elaborate building with the mansard roof is Saint Ignatius College Prep. They're two of the very few buildings in Chicago older than the Great Fire of 1871.

Holy Family was founded in 1857 by Father Arnold Damen, a Dutch Jesuit priest, to serve the Irish immigrants who were then pouring into this part of the city, many of them fleeing the famine in Ireland. When the church was built, between 1857 and 1860, this was still thinly settled land at the edge of the city, and people asked Damen why he was building such a big church out here. Within a couple of decades, Holy Family was one of the largest English-speaking parishes in the country. It's the second-oldest church building in Chicago, after Old Saint Patrick's.

On the night of October 8th, 1871, the Great Fire started at the O'Leary barn on DeKoven Street, less than a mile to the east. The O'Learys were members of this parish. A strong wind from the southwest drove the fire north and east, toward the downtown, and away from Holy Family, and the church survived. According to parish tradition, Father Damen, who was away preaching in New York, prayed all night before an image of Our Lady of Perpetual Help, promising that if his church was spared, lights would burn before her image forever. It's a legend, but the parish has kept the promise, and lights still burn before her image in the church today.

Next door, Saint Ignatius College opened in 1870, the year before the fire. It was the beginning of what became Loyola University Chicago, which later moved to the North Side, while the high school stayed here. In the late twentieth century, Holy Family was in such bad shape that it was nearly torn down. Parishioners, former parishioners, and preservationists raised millions of dollars to save it, and it has been restored.
=== STOP Taylor Street & Little Italy @ 41.8697,-87.6538
Walk north on May Street to Taylor Street, and turn right. You're in Little Italy. At number 1068, look for a tiny red, white, and green stand. This is Mario's Italian Lemonade, which has been serving Italian ice, made with bits of real lemon, every summer since 1954. On hot nights, the line stretches down the block.

Italians began settling on the Near West Side in the 1880s and 1890s, many of them from the villages of southern Italy and Sicily, and others from Tuscany and the north. They worked as laborers, peddlers, and railroad workers, and by the early twentieth century, this was one of the largest Italian communities in Chicago, with its own churches, bakeries, social clubs, and bocce courts. Taylor Street was its main street.

The neighborhood had a darker side, too. In the late 1920s, a gang of teenagers from these streets, called the 42 Gang, became a training ground for the Chicago mob, and several of its members rose high in the Outfit, among them Sam Giancana, who became the boss of the Chicago mob in the 1950s.

Then, in 1961, Mayor Richard J. Daley announced that the new Chicago campus of the University of Illinois would be built here, on the north side of the neighborhood. To make room, the city would clear dozens of blocks, home to thousands of Italian, Greek, Mexican, and Black families, and almost all of Hull House. The residents fought back. Their leader was Florence Scala, the daughter of an Italian tailor, who had grown up going to classes at Hull House. She organized protests and sit-ins at City Hall, and the fight went all the way to the United States Supreme Court, which refused to hear the case. Scala lost. The campus opened in 1965, a complex of concrete buildings designed by Walter Netsch, of Skidmore, Owings and Merrill. Studs Terkel interviewed Florence Scala for his book Division Street: America, and her account of a neighborhood destroyed in the name of progress is one of the most moving in the book.

Some of Little Italy survived south of the campus, and you'll still find Italian groceries, bakeries, and restaurants along Taylor Street, including Al's, which claims to have been making Italian beef sandwiches since 1938.
=== STOP The Jane Addams Homes @ 41.8694,-87.6603
Walk west on Taylor Street, past Racine Avenue, to Ada Street. On the north side of the street stands a three-story building of red brick, the last surviving piece of the Jane Addams Homes, which opened in 1938 as one of the first public housing projects in Chicago.

The Jane Addams Homes were built by the federal government during the New Deal, and named for the founder of Hull House, which stood a short walk to the northeast. They were low-rise apartment buildings arranged around grassy courtyards, with modern kitchens and bathrooms, and for the working families who moved in, they were a huge improvement over the tenements and shacks they replaced. The courtyard was decorated with stone sculptures of animals, by the Chicago artist Edgar Miller, made under a New Deal art program, and some of them have been preserved. Like nearly all public housing at the time, the project was segregated.

In the decades that followed, the Chicago Housing Authority built much bigger projects, including the high-rise towers of Cabrini-Green and the Robert Taylor Homes, almost all of them in Black neighborhoods, because white aldermen blocked public housing in their wards. The projects concentrated poverty, the buildings were poorly maintained, and by the 1990s, many had become dangerous places to live. In 2000, the city began tearing them down, under what it called the Plan for Transformation, and replacing many of them with mixed-income housing. The Jane Addams Homes were demolished too, except for this one building, saved by former residents and historians. It's now home to the National Public Housing Museum, which tells the story of public housing in America through the lives of the people who lived in it.

As you continue west along Taylor Street, look for a small plaza with a bronze statue of a baseball player in mid-swing. It's Joe DiMaggio, the Yankee Clipper, the son of Italian immigrants, standing outside the National Italian American Sports Hall of Fame, a reminder of how much this neighborhood once meant to Italian Americans across the Midwest.
=== STOP Cook County Hospital @ 41.8738,-87.6727
Walk west on Taylor Street to Ashland Avenue, north to Harrison Street, and west past Wood Street. You're now in the Illinois Medical District, one of the largest concentrations of hospitals and medical schools in the country. The long, grand building with the ornate facade of stone and terra cotta is the old Cook County Hospital, completed in 1914.

For most of the twentieth century, County, as everyone called it, was the hospital for the poor of Chicago, open to anyone, whether they could pay or not. It was enormous, with thousands of beds, and it was famous for training young doctors, who saw more patients, and more kinds of illness and injury, in a few months than many doctors elsewhere saw in years.

Many medical firsts happened here. In 1937, a doctor named Bernard Fantus set up a way to collect, store, and preserve donated blood, so it would be ready when patients needed it. He called it a blood bank, and it was the first of its kind in an American hospital. In 1966, County opened one of the first trauma units in the country, dedicated to treating the victims of shootings, stabbings, and accidents. The hospital also inspired the long-running television drama ER, set in the emergency room of a Chicago hospital called County General.

In 2002, a new county hospital opened next door, and the old building stood empty for almost twenty years. Preservationists fought off plans to demolish it, and in 2020, it reopened as two hotels and medical offices, with its facade restored.

This is the end of the Little Italy and Maxwell Street tour. The Illinois Medical District station on the Blue Line is nearby, and it will take you back downtown.
