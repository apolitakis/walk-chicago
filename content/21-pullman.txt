=== TOUR

title: Pullman: the Company Town
area: South Side
blurb: George Pullman's model town and the strike that broke it: the clock tower of the car works, the Hotel Florence, the Arcade and Greenstone Church, the workers' rowhouses, and the porters who built a union.
=== OVERVIEW
This tour takes you to Pullman, on the Far South Side, about fourteen miles south of the Loop. You can get here on the Metra Electric line to the 111th Street station. It's a long way from downtown, but it's one of the most important places in the history of American industry and labor, and it's now a national historical park.

George Pullman came to Chicago from New York in 1859. His first business here was raising buildings. Chicago was lifting its streets several feet to put in sewers, and Pullman helped jack up whole brick buildings to the new street level, sometimes with the occupants still inside. Then he turned to railroad cars. Long-distance train travel was miserable, and Pullman built sleeping cars that were luxurious: carpets, chandeliers, fine wood, comfortable berths, and dining cars. The Pullman Palace Car Company didn't sell most of its cars. It owned them and operated them on the railroads, with its own staff. Within a few decades, a Pullman car was the standard of comfort on American railroads.

In 1880, Pullman bought about four thousand acres of land here, near Lake Calumet, and began to build a new factory, and around it, a whole new town for his workers. He believed that if workers lived in clean, attractive, orderly surroundings, they would be healthier, happier, and more productive, and they wouldn't strike. The town had brick houses with indoor plumbing and gas, parks, a church, a library, a theater, a market, and a hotel. It was praised around the world as a model. But everything was owned by the company. Residents rented. There were no saloons, no independent newspapers, and no elected local government. Company inspectors could enter homes.

In 1893, a deep economic depression hit. Pullman cut wages sharply, but he didn't lower the rents. In 1894, his workers went on strike, and the strike spread across the country. It became one of the most important labor conflicts in American history.

We'll start at the clock tower of the old factory, walk through the heart of the town past the Hotel Florence, the market, and the church, to the workers' rowhouses, and end with the story of the Pullman porters.
=== STOP The Clock Tower & the Car Works @ 41.6942,-87.6090
You're north of 111th Street, looking at a long brick building with a tall clock tower in the middle. This was the Administration Building of the Pullman Car Works, built in 1880 and designed, like most of the town, by a young architect named Solon Spencer Beman.

Behind it, stretching north, stood the factory buildings where Pullman cars were built: erecting shops, paint shops, foundries, and lumber sheds. At its height, the works employed thousands of people. The machinery was powered for years by the Corliss engine, a giant steam engine that had been the star of the 1876 Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia, which Pullman bought and brought here.

In front of the building there was once a small ornamental lake, called Lake Vista, which made the factory look like a country estate to passengers on the trains passing by. Pullman wanted the town itself to be an advertisement for his company.

In December 1998, a fire gutted the clock tower building. The state of Illinois, which owned it, restored it. In 2015, President Barack Obama designated the factory and the town as a national monument, and it has since become a national historical park. The restored building now holds exhibits about the company, the town, and the strike.

On May 11th, 1894, workers walked out of the shops here. Their union, the American Railway Union, led by Eugene V. Debs, then called on railroad workers across the country to refuse to handle trains with Pullman cars. Rail traffic across much of the nation stopped. The railroads hired strikebreakers, and President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago, over the objections of the governor of Illinois, John Peter Altgeld. Riots broke out in the rail yards, and dozens of people were killed across the country before the strike was crushed. Debs went to prison and came out a socialist. That summer, Congress made Labor Day a national holiday.

In the 1880s and early 1890s, the town of Pullman was famous around the world. Journalists, reformers, and visiting royalty came to see it, and at an international exhibition on health and hygiene in Prague in 1896, it was named the world's most perfect town. The workers who lived here had a different view. As one of them put it, they were born in a Pullman house, fed from the Pullman shops, taught in the Pullman school, catechized in the Pullman church, and when they died, they'd go to the Pullman hell.

In 2021, the restored building opened as the visitor center of Pullman National Historical Park, and it's the best place to start a visit. Stand in front of it, look south over the town, laid out just as Pullman and Beman planned it, and imagine the sound of the factory whistle calling thousands of workers to the shops each morning.
=== STOP The Hotel Florence @ 41.6924,-87.6086
Cross 111th Street to the red brick building with the gabled roofs and the long porches. This is the Hotel Florence, built in 1881 and named for George Pullman's favorite daughter.

The hotel was built for the businessmen, salesmen, and railroad officials who came to visit the works, and for tourists who came to see the famous model town. It was elegant, with fine woodwork, and Pullman kept a suite here for himself.

Its bar was the only place in the town of Pullman where alcohol was sold, and it was meant for guests, not for the workers. Pullman didn't want saloons in his town. Workers who wanted a drink walked south to the neighboring town of Kensington, where saloons lined the street just outside the company's property line. It was called Bumtown.

The hotel shows the contradiction at the heart of the town. Pullman built far better housing and amenities than most workers in America had. But he also believed he had the right to control how his workers lived, and he expected the town to turn a profit, as a business. He told critics that the town was not a charity.

The hotel is owned by the state of Illinois, and it's been under restoration. Look at the building's wide porches and its tower, and imagine visitors arriving from downtown on the Illinois Central trains, which stopped right at the station on the other side of the park.

Florence Pullman, for whom the hotel was named, married Frank Lowden, a lawyer who became governor of Illinois. And after George Pullman died in 1897, the man who took over as president of the Pullman Company was Robert Todd Lincoln, the only son of Abraham Lincoln to survive to adulthood. He led the company for fourteen years, as it became the largest manufacturer of railroad cars in the world.
=== STOP Arcade Park, Market Hall & Greenstone Church @ 41.6909,-87.6078
Walk south through Arcade Park, the green square in front of the hotel. On this site stood the Arcade Building, a large brick structure that was the town's shopping center, an indoor mall before there was such a thing. It held shops, a bank, the post office, a library with thousands of books donated by Pullman, and a theater. Residents had to pay to use the library. The Arcade was demolished in 1926.

Walk south to the corner of 112th Street and Saint Lawrence Avenue. The church with the green stone walls and the tall steeple is the Greenstone Church, built in 1882. Its stone is a green serpentine rock, brought from Pennsylvania. Pullman built just one church for the whole town, and he intended to rent it to a single congregation. But the rent was so high that no congregation could afford it for years, and it stood empty while workers worshipped in other buildings. Today it's a Methodist church.

Just to the east, on 112th Street, is Market Hall, which stands in the center of a square surrounded by curved, colonnaded buildings. The original market burned and was rebuilt in the 1890s. Here, residents bought meat, fruit, and vegetables from vendors who rented stalls from the company. The upper floor was used as a meeting hall. The hall has been damaged by fires over the years, and only part of its structure survives, but the colonnaded buildings around the square give you a sense of how carefully Beman planned the town's public spaces.

During the strike, the workers' union held meetings in halls in Kensington, because they couldn't meet on company property.
=== STOP The Workers' Rowhouses @ 41.6882,-87.6084
Walk south along Saint Lawrence Avenue toward 113th Street. You're surrounded by the houses where Pullman's workers lived: long rows of attached brick houses, with slate roofs, decorative trim, and small front yards.

Notice how the houses vary. Skilled workers and foremen lived in larger houses with more rooms, often on the wider streets, while unskilled laborers lived in smaller rowhouses and in apartment buildings called block houses. Managers and executives lived in the biggest houses, near the hotel and the factory. Every house was planned by Beman, and the varied rooflines and details were designed to keep the streets from looking monotonous.

Pullman's town had about twelve thousand residents by the early 1890s. The houses had gas, water, and indoor toilets, which was rare for working-class housing at the time. But rents were higher than in surrounding neighborhoods, and when wages were cut in 1893, many families could barely afford to eat.

After the strike, a state investigation criticized the company, and in 1898, the Illinois Supreme Court ordered it to sell the parts of the town that weren't needed for its factory. Pullman never saw it happen. He died in 1897. The houses were sold to private owners, many of them to the people who lived in them.

In 1960, there was a plan to demolish the town for an industrial park. The residents organized, fought the plan, and won landmark protection for the historic district. Many of these houses have been lovingly restored by their owners. It's one of the best-preserved nineteenth-century industrial towns in the country.

Eugene Debs, the strike's leader, read the works of socialist writers while in jail, and he came out convinced that workers needed their own political party. He ran for president five times as a Socialist, the last time in 1920, from a federal prison cell, where he was serving a sentence for speaking out against the First World War. He won nearly a million votes.

George Pullman, meanwhile, never understood why his workers had turned against him. When he died in 1897, his family feared that angry former workers might dig up his body, so he was buried at night, in Graceland Cemetery, in a pit lined with concrete and steel rails.
=== STOP The Pullman Porters @ 41.7054,-87.6031
The last stop is about a mile north, in North Pullman, at 10406 South Maryland Avenue, the A. Philip Randolph Pullman Porter Museum. It's a long walk, or a short drive.

The Pullman Company's sleeping cars were staffed by porters, and almost all of them were Black men. Pullman began hiring formerly enslaved men after the Civil War, and by the 1920s, the Pullman Company was one of the largest employers of Black Americans in the country. A porter's job was to make up the berths, carry bags, shine shoes, and serve passengers around the clock. The pay was low, much of it from tips, and the hours were brutally long. Passengers often called every porter "George," after George Pullman, as if the men were his property.

But the job also had prestige. Porters traveled the country, were often well read, and were pillars of their communities. They carried news, music, and newspapers like the Chicago Defender from North to South, and they helped spread word of jobs in the North during the Great Migration.

In 1925, the porters organized a union, the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, led by A. Philip Randolph, a labor organizer from New York. The Pullman Company fought it fiercely for twelve years. In 1937, the Brotherhood won a contract, the first major agreement between a union led by Black Americans and a large American corporation. Randolph went on to become one of the leaders of the civil rights movement. He was the original organizer of the 1963 March on Washington.

The museum tells the porters' story. Pullman sleeping car service ended in 1968, and the company's last factory closed in the following decades, but the porters' legacy lives on in the civil rights movement they helped build.

Randolph's Chicago organizer was Milton Webster, a former porter who had been fired for his union activity, and who built the Brotherhood's strongest local here. In 1941, Randolph threatened to bring a hundred thousand Black Americans to march on Washington to protest discrimination in the defense industries that were gearing up for war. President Franklin Roosevelt, to head off the march, issued an executive order banning discrimination in defense plants. It was one of the first federal actions against racial discrimination in employment since Reconstruction.

The museum itself was founded in 1995 by a historian named Lyn Hughes, who was determined that the porters' story, and their union's victory, not be forgotten. It's a small museum on a quiet residential street, full of the uniforms, photographs, and memories of the men who made the sleeping cars run.

This is the end of the Pullman tour.
