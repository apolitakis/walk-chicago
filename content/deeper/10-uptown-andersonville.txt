=== STOP The Green Mill & the Uptown Theatre
=== DEEPER Pop Morse's Roadhouse | history
The Green Mill began as a place for mourners. In 1907, when this part of the North Side was still thinly built up, a roadhouse here served drinks and food to people coming back from funerals at Graceland and Saint Boniface, the two big cemeteries a short ride away. It was known as Pop Morse's Roadhouse.

In 1910, a businessman named Tom Chamales bought it and turned it into a pleasure garden, the Green Mill Gardens, with a big sunken garden for outdoor dancing and entertainment. As the elevated trains brought crowds to Uptown, it became one of the busiest nightspots on the North Side. Over the years the gardens disappeared, and the Green Mill shrank to the snug cocktail lounge you see today.

Then came the long slide. Uptown sank into poverty after the Second World War, and the Green Mill sank with it. A man named Steve Brend, who had started working there in 1938, bought it in 1960 and kept it going through the lean years, when the neighborhood was better known for its flophouses than its nightlife.

In 1986, a young bar owner named Dave Jemilo bought the club. Instead of gutting the old room, he cleaned it up and restored it, and he made it a serious jazz room again. That bet paid off. The Green Mill became one of the places where Chicago's jazz musicians grew up. The pianist and singer Patricia Barber has held down Monday nights here for years, and the singer Kurt Elling was a regular here early in his career.

The best reason to come is simple. Late at night, with the lights low and a band playing, the Green Mill is about as close as you can get to stepping into the city's past while it's still going on around you.
=== DEEPER The Slam | culture
On a Sunday night at the Green Mill, you can still see the show where it all started.

Marc Smith found most poetry readings deadly. Poets mumbled into their manuscripts, and audiences sat in polite silence. In late 1984 he started a Monday night open mic at a bar called the Get Me High Lounge, and he began experimenting with ways to wake things up. The idea that stuck was competition, scored like a boxing match or an Olympic event. In 1986 the show moved here, as the Uptown Poetry Slam, at about the time Dave Jemilo bought the place, and it has been here ever since.

The format was simple and deliberately rowdy. Poets got a few minutes each, and the audience was encouraged to react out loud, with cheers, groans, and snapping fingers. In the version of the slam that spread to other cities, judges picked at random from the crowd hold up scores from zero to ten, and the poet with the highest total wins. Nobody was supposed to take the scores too seriously. As slam poets like to say, the points are not the point; the point is the poetry.

From Chicago, slams spread to other cities, and by the early 1990s there was a national championship. They helped turn spoken word into an art form of its own, they gave a stage to poets who had never been near a university writing program, and they carried poetry into high schools, where youth slams now draw crowds of teenagers who would never have sat through a traditional reading.

Smith, for his part, kept hosting the Sunday show for decades, still insisting that a poem should be performed, not just recited, and that the audience has a right to talk back.
=== DEEPER The Sleeping Palace | architecture
Across Broadway, the Uptown Theatre was the biggest of all the palaces Balaban and Katz built, and it's often described as the largest freestanding theater in North America. Freestanding is the key word. Downtown, many movie palaces were squeezed into office buildings, with the auditorium tucked behind a narrow entrance. Out here, where land was cheaper, the Rapp brothers could make the whole thing one monumental building, taking up a big piece of a city block.

The plan was a procession. From the street you passed under the tall sign into the outer lobby, then into that cathedral of a grand lobby, and up broad staircases toward balconies that seemed to hang in midair. The style is a lavish Spanish Baroque, with plaster ornament on nearly every surface. Even waiting was part of the show. The crowd for the next screening could fill those lobbies and feel like guests in a palace, not customers in a line.

The last show here was a concert by the J. Geils Band, in 1981. Then the doors closed, and the building sat dark for decades, its plaster and paint suffering from neglect. Preservationists campaigned for years to save it, and the building survived, which in a city that has lost most of its movie palaces is a small miracle.

In 2008, after a foreclosure, the Chicago concert promoters Jam Productions bought the Uptown for about three million dollars. In 2018 the city announced a plan to restore it, at a cost then estimated at seventy-five million dollars, and the estimates have kept climbing since, well past a hundred million. Restoring a building like this means recasting ornament by hand, rebuilding systems that are a century old, and bringing it all up to modern codes.

For now, the best you can do is stand on Broadway, look up at the facade and the sign, and imagine the crowds lining up on a Saturday night in 1925.
=== STOP The Aragon Ballroom
=== DEEPER The Karzas Brothers | companies
Andrew and William Karzas made their first fortune in the early movie business, running nickelodeons and then movie theaters. In the early 1920s they saw a bigger opportunity in dancing.

Young people were pouring into the city to work in offices, stores, and factories, and dance halls were booming. But many of the halls had bad reputations, for liquor, fights, and worse, and reformers campaigned against them. The Karzas brothers built palaces instead, and they sold respectability as much as music.

The first was the Trianon, designed by the Rapp brothers, which opened in December 1922 at 62nd Street and Cottage Grove Avenue, in Woodlawn on the South Side. Its decorations were borrowed from the palace of Versailles, its owners advertised it as the most beautiful ballroom in the world, and it could hold about three thousand dancers. By one account, the band had to play a waltz every third number. The Aragon followed in 1926, on the North Side, so the brothers had the city covered from both ends.

They ran their ballrooms like a well-mannered business. Bands were chosen for sweet, danceable music rather than hot jazz, and the Trianon, according to some histories, booked only white bands. Admission was kept cheap enough for a stenographer or a shop clerk. The Trianon even had its own radio station, which helped spread its name across the Midwest.

The Trianon is long gone, torn down in 1967 to make room for low-income housing. The Aragon has lasted far longer. In the summer of 2026 it celebrated its hundredth birthday, still drawing crowds, though the music would have startled the Karzas brothers.
=== DEEPER Live from the Aragon | culture
For years, millions of Americans who never set foot in Chicago knew this ballroom's name. Starting in the late 1920s, the radio station WGN broadcast an hour of music from the Aragon almost every night, to listeners across the United States and Canada. A family in Kansas or Ontario could roll back the rug and dance to the same band that was playing here.

No bandleader was more closely tied to the Aragon than Wayne King, known as the Waltz King. He played the saxophone and led a smooth, sweet orchestra that specialized in waltzes, and his theme song, The Waltz You Saved for Me, became one of the most familiar melodies on the air. His orchestra played the Aragon from 1927 into the middle of the 1930s, and in those years King was among the most popular bandleaders in the country, with a radio show of his own sponsored by a cosmetics company.

After the Second World War, Americans stayed home with their television sets, and the great ballrooms emptied one by one. Fittingly, when the Aragon's ballroom era ended, Wayne King was booked to play the last dances, in February 1964. In the late 1960s, the Aragon spent a couple of years as a nightclub called the Cheetah, before the rock concerts took over.

If you go to a show here, look up. The make-believe Spanish night that the dancers saw is still overhead, so the crowd at a heavy metal concert is standing where couples once waltzed to Wayne King. The room is also notorious among concertgoers for its echo. It was built for saxophones and violins, not stacks of amplifiers, and bands have been wrestling with its sound for half a century.
=== DEEPER Hotels for the Young and Single | architecture
Look at the big brick and terra cotta buildings along Lawrence Avenue and Sheridan Road. Many of them went up in the 1920s as apartment hotels, a kind of housing that Uptown specialized in.

An apartment hotel was halfway between a hotel and a home. Tenants rented by the week or the month, often furnished, in units of one or two rooms. A bed might fold up into a closet during the day to turn the bedroom into a living room. There might be a tiny kitchenette or no kitchen at all, because downstairs were the services of a hotel: a front desk, maid service, a dining room or coffee shop, and sometimes a ballroom or a swimming pool in the grander buildings.

The customers were the young office workers who were pouring into Chicago in the 1920s: secretaries, clerks, salesmen, and teachers, many of them living on their own for the first time. They wanted to be near the lake, the elevated trains, and the nightlife, and they didn't want to cook or keep house. Uptown gave them all of it.

The Depression and the war changed everything. Apartments were cut into smaller and smaller units, and by the 1960s many of these buildings had become rooming houses, with shared bathrooms down the hall, renting cheaply by the week to people with nowhere else to go: migrants from the South, old men living on pensions, and later, people released from the state mental hospitals as they emptied out.

Since the 1990s, many of those buildings have been renovated into market-rate apartments, and the fight over what that means for poor residents has been one of Uptown's longest political battles. One of the biggest, the Lawrence House, a short walk east of the Aragon, shows both sides of the story. For decades it rented cheap rooms to people with few other choices. In the 2010s it was renovated into stylish apartments, and most of its old tenants had to find homes elsewhere.
=== STOP Argyle Street
=== DEEPER The Chinatown That Wasn't | companies
In the early 1970s, a businessman named Jimmy Wong had a big idea for this street. Wong worked with the Hip Sing Association, one of the old Chinese American fraternal organizations, which moved its Chicago offices here, to Argyle, in 1971. Across the country, the Hip Sing had long been rivals of the On Leong, the association that dominated the old Chinatown on the South Side, which may help explain the appeal of a Chinatown of their own. By 1974, Wong and his allies controlled most of the property along these few blocks.

Wong's plan was grand: a new Chinatown, complete with a shopping mall, pagodas, and reflecting ponds, to rival the crowded old one. It never happened. An injury forced Wong to retire, and the project stalled.

Then the refugees came, as you heard at the stop, and many of them were exactly the tenants the plan needed. The ethnic Chinese from Vietnam usually spoke Cantonese or another Chinese language as well as Vietnamese, so they could deal easily with Chinese American landlords. And many of them already knew how to run a shop, because in Vietnam, the Chinese minority had long been prominent in trade.

The street still needed customers from the rest of the city. A community booster named Charlie Soo took up the cause in 1979, promoting Argyle to Chicagoans who had never been there, and in 1981 he started an annual food festival on the street.

So a second Chinatown did arrive, in a way, just not the one anyone had planned. It was built not by developers with a master plan but by families running small businesses, often working long hours, with their children doing homework at the back tables.

Today a younger generation runs many of those businesses, and new restaurants mix with the old. But the street still feels like what it became in those years, a place where newcomers could find their footing.
=== DEEPER A Bowl of Pho | food
If you eat on Argyle Street, there's a good chance you'll end up with a bowl of pho, so here's a little background.

Pho was born in northern Vietnam early in the twentieth century, around Hanoi, and food historians still argue about its ancestors. Some point to the French, who ruled Vietnam and brought a taste for beef. Others point to Chinese noodle soups. The spices in the broth, star anise, cinnamon, cloves, and charred ginger and onion, suggest a bit of both.

When Vietnam was divided in 1954, hundreds of thousands of people fled the communist north for the south, and pho went with them. In Saigon it became sweeter and more lavish, served with a plate of fresh herbs, bean sprouts, lime, and chilies, and with bottles of hoisin and hot sauce on the table. That southern style is what refugees brought to America after 1975, and it's what you'll find on most menus here.

To eat it like a regular, taste the broth first, before you add anything. Then tear in the basil, add sprouts and a squeeze of lime, and work with chopsticks in one hand and the spoon in the other. Don't be shy about slurping.

Then there's banh mi, the Vietnamese sandwich. It starts with a light, crackly baguette, another inheritance from the French, stuffed with pâté, cold cuts or grilled meat, pickled carrots and daikon radish, cucumber, cilantro, and sliced chilies. And look for Vietnamese iced coffee, strong and dark, dripped slowly through a little metal filter over sweetened condensed milk, then poured over ice.

Argyle isn't only Vietnamese. Look for Chinese barbecue shops with roast ducks and slabs of pork hanging in the window, and for bakeries that sell mooncakes in the fall, around the time of the harvest moon festival. Few streets in the city let you eat this well for so little.
=== DEEPER Hillbilly Heaven | politics
Why did so many mountain people end up in Uptown? Back home in Appalachia, machines were replacing coal miners and small farms were failing, and after the Second World War, a steady stream of families headed north to Chicago, Detroit, Cincinnati, and other industrial cities. Uptown, with its cheap furnished rooms and its day-labor offices, was often their first stop. Plenty of Chicagoans looked down on them, and landlords, employers, and the police often treated them as a problem to be managed.

In the mid-1960s, a group of college students from Students for a Democratic Society moved into Uptown to organize the poor. Their project was called Jobs or Income Now. They organized tenants and people on welfare and protested against slumlords and police abuse. But some of the young men from the neighborhood felt that middle-class students from elsewhere were running the show.

In 1968, several of them, including Junebug Boykin and Doug Youngblood, founded their own group, the Young Patriots. They were poor, Southern, and white, and they talked like the neighborhood. They took on landlords who let buildings rot, police who treated young Southerners as suspects, and city plans to clear blocks of cheap housing. In 1970 they led a sit-in at a city health clinic in Uptown, demanding that it stay open at hours when working people could actually use it. Their alliance with the Black Panthers and the Young Lords, the Rainbow Coalition, you heard about at the stop.

By 1973 the Young Patriots had mostly faded away. In recent years, a few of the surviving members have written and spoken about that time, a surprising chapter in the history of both the New Left and the white working class.

Uptown's habit of organizing didn't fade with them. In the decades since, tenants' groups, legal aid clinics, and churches here have fought evictions and the loss of cheap housing, often against the same pressures of renewal and rising rents that the Patriots fought.
=== DEEPER Native Uptown | history
In the 1950s, the federal government launched a program to move Native Americans off reservations and into cities. The Bureau of Indian Affairs offered travel money, a little help, and the promise of jobs in places like Denver, Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Chicago. In Washington, the program was seen as a way to fold Native people into the mainstream and shrink the government's obligations on reservations. For many who came here, it meant a single check, a month's rent, and directions to Uptown.

By the census count, Chicago's Native population grew from fewer than eight hundred people in 1950 to more than six thousand five hundred in 1970, and Uptown became its center. In 1953, the American Indian Center opened here, one of the first urban Native community centers in the country. It became a place for powwows, meals, social services, and simply meeting people from dozens of nations who had landed in the same strange city.

The housing was often terrible. In May 1970, a Menominee woman named Carol Warrington, who had been withholding rent because her landlord refused to make repairs, was evicted from an apartment near Wrigley Field. Her neighbors and supporters answered by pitching tents in a parking lot next to the ballpark. They called the camp the Chicago Indian Village, and they demanded decent housing. The protests moved from site to site for about two years, and they drew national attention to the lives of Native people in American cities, at the same moment that activists were occupying Alcatraz Island in San Francisco Bay.

As Uptown gentrified, many Native families moved away, and the American Indian Center eventually moved farther northwest. But the Chicago area still has one of the largest urban Native communities in the country, with people from well over a hundred tribal nations, and Uptown is where much of that story began.
=== STOP Essanay Studios
=== DEEPER Broncho Billy | people
Gilbert Anderson, the A in Essanay, was born Max Aronson in Little Rock, Arkansas, in 1880. He drifted into show business as a young man, and in 1903 he landed several small parts in The Great Train Robbery, the short Western made by Thomas Edison's company that became one of the first big hits of the movies. Anderson saw the future in it.

After he and George Spoor founded Essanay, Anderson ran the company's Westerns. He wrote, directed, and starred in a long string of short films as Broncho Billy, a rough but good-hearted outlaw who usually did the right thing by the last reel. Since you can't shoot the Wild West in a Chicago January, he took his crews on the road, and in 1912 Essanay built a western studio in Niles, California, at the foot of Niles Canyon, east of San Francisco Bay.

Niles is where Chaplin went after his miserable Chicago winter, and where he made several of his Essanay films, including The Tramp, one of the films that defined his famous character.

Anderson sold his share of Essanay in 1916 and gave up acting. He tried producing, without much luck, though one of the short comedies he produced, The Lucky Dog, is remembered today as the first film in which Stan Laurel and Oliver Hardy appeared together, years before anyone thought of them as a team.

For decades afterward, Anderson was mostly forgotten. Then, in 1958, the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences gave him an honorary award for his contributions to the movies. He died in 1971, at the age of ninety.

The town of Niles, now part of Fremont, California, has kept his memory alive with a museum and a silent film festival named for Broncho Billy. Chicago, where it all started, has this building, and the chief over the door.
=== DEEPER The Trust and the Rebel | companies
Essanay grew up in a business that was trying hard to become a monopoly. In 1908, Thomas Edison's company, which held key patents on movie cameras and projectors, joined with its main rivals to form the Motion Picture Patents Company, which critics called the Trust. Its members included Biograph, Vitagraph, and both of Chicago's big studios, Essanay and Selig. Only members could make movies with the patented equipment, Eastman Kodak agreed to sell raw film only to them, and theaters had to pay a weekly fee to show their pictures.

Chicago was also home to the Trust's most stubborn enemy. Carl Laemmle was a German immigrant who had managed a clothing store in Oshkosh, Wisconsin. In 1906 he put most of his savings into a nickelodeon on Milwaukee Avenue, the White Front, and soon he was renting films to other theaters from Chicago. When the Trust tried to squeeze him, he refused to pay, and in 1909 he began making pictures of his own. The Trust took him to court again and again.

Laemmle fought back with publicity. In 1910, the story goes, a rumor spread that a popular actress known only as the Biograph Girl had been killed in a streetcar accident. Laemmle announced that she was alive, well, and working for him under her real name, Florence Lawrence. Historians still argue over whether he started the rumor himself, but the stunt helped create something new: the movie star, known to the public by name.

The independents won. In 1912 Laemmle and his partners formed Universal, and in 1915 a federal court ruled that the Trust had broken the antitrust laws. By then the independents were heading for Southern California, for its sunshine, its cheap land, and, by some accounts, its distance from the Trust's lawyers. Universal opened its own studio city near Los Angeles that same year.

Essanay, a charter member of the Trust, never really adjusted to the new world, and within a few years it was gone.
=== DEEPER Essanay's Stars | culture
Charlie Chaplin was the biggest name to pass through this building, but he wasn't the only one. Essanay's Chicago company was full of people who'd go on to fame.

Francis X. Bushman was the studio's matinee idol, a former sculptor's model who became one of the first romantic leading men of the movies, often paired with the actress Beverly Bayne. Ben Turpin, the cross-eyed comic, worked here for years and played opposite Chaplin in His New Job. Wallace Beery, a big, rough-hewn young actor, made a series of comedies here as, of all things, a Swedish maid named Sweedie. Gloria Swanson, the teenage extra you heard about at the stop, later married him, briefly. Beery went on to win an Academy Award for The Champ.

Behind the camera, a young writer named Louella Parsons worked on scenarios for the studio, years before she became the most feared gossip columnist in Hollywood. Essanay also filmed the comic fables of the Chicago humorist George Ade.

Most of what Essanay made is gone. Silent films were printed on flammable nitrate stock, and once sound arrived, studios saw little reason to keep them. A Library of Congress study in 2013 estimated that about three quarters of American silent feature films are lost.

But sometimes one comes back. In 1916, Essanay filmed Sherlock Holmes, starring William Gillette, the actor who had made the detective famous on stage, and whose curved pipe helped fix the image of Holmes for good. For nearly a century the film was thought lost. Then, in 2014, a print turned up in the vaults of the Cinémathèque française, in Paris. After a careful restoration, it was shown again in 2015. It's the only film Gillette ever made, and the only record of the performance that had defined Sherlock Holmes for a generation of theatergoers.
=== STOP Andersonville & the Swedish American Museum
=== DEEPER Leaving Sweden | history
Between the middle of the nineteenth century and 1930, more than a million Swedes left for America, by some estimates about one in five of the people in the country.

The great push came at the end of the 1860s. Three bad harvests in a row, from 1867 to 1869, brought famine to parts of the Swedish countryside. Underneath the famine was a deeper problem. Sweden's population had grown fast, thanks, as one Swedish poet famously put it, to peace, vaccine, and potatoes, and there wasn't enough land to go around. Farms were divided into ever smaller plots, and many younger sons and daughters had no hope of land of their own.

America offered land, jobs, and, for religious dissenters, freedom of worship. Letters from relatives who had already made the crossing, and the agents of railroads and steamship lines, did the rest.

Many Swedes went on to the farms of Minnesota and the Dakotas, but a great many stopped in Chicago. By 1900, it was often said, only Stockholm had more Swedes than Chicago. The first Swedish neighborhood grew up north of the river, around Chicago Avenue, and people called it Swede Town. As families saved money and the city grew, they moved north and west, to Lake View, to here, and beyond.

Most of them were young, and many were single. Swedish women were so much in demand as household help that the Swedish maid became a stock figure in American humor, and Swedish men's skill with wood and tools made them a force in the building trades.

The name Andersonville comes, by the usual account, from John Anderson, a Swedish-born farmer who bought land near what's now Clark Street and Foster Avenue in the 1840s, long before most of the Swedes arrived. A local school later carried the name. Decades after that, the business owners on Clark Street revived it for their shopping district, and it sounded perfectly Swedish.
=== DEEPER From Lutefisk to Lingonberries | food
Swedish food follows the Swedish calendar, so what you'll find depends on the season.

At Christmas, the centerpiece is the julbord, the Christmas table, a buffet of pickled herring in several sauces, cured salmon called gravlax, meatballs, ham, sausages, and potatoes. Then there's lutefisk, the dish Swedish Americans love to argue about: dried cod soaked for days in lye, then rinsed and boiled until it turns translucent and trembling. In the Swedish tradition, it's served with white sauce, peas, and potatoes. Wash it down with glögg, hot red wine spiced with cinnamon, cloves, and cardamom, poured over raisins and almonds.

On Saint Lucia's Day, December thirteenth, the Lucia procession comes with saffron buns, curled into the shape of an S and called Lucia cats.

All year round, look for limpa, a dark rye bread sweetened with molasses and flavored with orange peel and anise or fennel. And look for cardamom in everything. Swedes put it in buns, cookies, and coffee breads, and the habit of a coffee break with something sweet, called fika, is close to a national institution.

Those Swedish pancakes on the menus nearby are thin, like French crepes, and the lingonberries that come with them are a tart red berry from the northern forests, cooked into a jam. In Sweden, pancakes are traditionally served on Thursdays, after a bowl of yellow split pea soup, a custom that goes back centuries.

The old Swedish bakeries and delis on Clark Street have mostly closed, as families moved away and the neighborhood changed. But the traditions hold on, on a few menus, at the museum's holiday programs, and at the festivals that bring Swedish Americans back to Clark Street every year.
=== DEEPER The Mission Friends | culture
The Swedes who built Andersonville didn't all worship the same way.

In Sweden, almost everyone belonged to the state Lutheran church. But in the nineteenth century, a revival movement swept the countryside. Its followers, first called readers and later Mission Friends, met in homes to read the Bible and pray together, often without a clergyman, and they stressed a personal experience of faith over the formal rituals of the state church. For years, such meetings were frowned on by the authorities, and they could even be against the law.

In America, with no state church to answer to, Swedish immigrants sorted themselves out. Many joined the Augustana Synod, a Swedish Lutheran church founded in 1860. Others became Methodists or Baptists. And in 1885, in Chicago, the Mission Friends founded a denomination of their own, the Swedish Evangelical Mission Covenant.

You heard that the Covenant built a hospital and a college a couple of miles west of here. The college began in Minneapolis and moved to Chicago in 1894, onto land along Foster Avenue, where it became North Park College, now North Park University. For generations, Swedish American pastors and teachers trained there.

For decades, these churches held their services in Swedish, and they were the heart of immigrant life, with choirs, youth groups, temperance societies, and Sunday schools. The switch to English came gradually, mostly in the 1920s and 1930s, as the American-born children grew up and the flow of new immigrants slowed.

The Covenant, now called the Evangelical Covenant Church, has long since spread far beyond its Swedish roots, with congregations across the country and around the world. But it's still headquartered in Chicago, and its story is one reason the Swedish flavor of this part of the city has lasted so long.
=== STOP The Edgewater Beach Apartments
=== DEEPER Cochran's Edgewater | names
The neighborhood around you was invented, name and all, by one man. John Lewis Cochran grew up in Philadelphia and came to Chicago as an agent for a tobacco company. In the mid-1880s he bought land along the lake here, well north of the city, and laid out a subdivision he called Edgewater, for the obvious reason: it ran right to the water.

Cochran sold Edgewater as a thoroughly modern suburb. It was the first planned development in the area with paved streets, proper drainage, and electric lights, powered by an electric company Cochran set up for the purpose, at a time when most streets in the region were still lit by gas or not at all.

Now look at the street signs: Berwyn, Bryn Mawr, Ardmore, Rosemont, Devon. Every one of them is also the name of a station on the Main Line, the string of wealthy railroad suburbs west of Philadelphia. The usual explanation is that Cochran named the streets of his Chicago suburb after the stops he knew from home, to give the new place a little of the old money and polish of the Philadelphia suburbs.

Edgewater became part of Chicago with the rest of Lake View in the great annexation of 1889. For decades it was a quiet, prosperous neighborhood of big houses, until the 1920s, when the lake views attracted the apartment hotels and high-rise apartment buildings you see along Sheridan Road.

For most of the twentieth century, the city counted Edgewater as part of Uptown. Residents never liked that, and after years of lobbying, Edgewater became an official community area of its own in 1980, the newest of the city's seventy-seven. So the name Cochran made up in the 1880s has outlasted his electric company, his suburb, and even the great resort hotel that made it famous.
=== DEEPER Where the Park Runs Out | underfoot
When the Edgewater Beach Hotel opened, its guests could step out onto the sand. Today, from here, the lake is a walk away, across a busy highway and a strip of park. Nearly everything between Sheridan Road and the water is landfill.

Over the course of a century, the city pushed Lincoln Park north by filling in the lake, a stretch at a time. Stone, sand, and fill were dumped behind new breakwaters and then shaped into lawns, beaches, harbors, and drives. The last piece, past this point to Hollywood Avenue, came in the 1950s with the extension of the Drive, the change that, as you heard, cut the hotel off from its beach.

Now walk a block or so north, to Hollywood Avenue, where Lake Shore Drive comes to an end and turns into Sheridan Road. North of there, the pattern breaks. The parkland stops, and for most of the way to Evanston, apartment towers and houses stand right at the edge of the water, behind their own seawalls. In Rogers Park, farther north, the ends of many streets become small public beaches, little openings in a wall of private property.

The contrast tells you what the landfill did. Most of Chicago's lakefront south of this point is public parkland, a front yard that belongs to everyone, which is unusual among the world's great waterfront cities. North of Hollywood, the shore went to private owners before the park could reach it. Park advocates have proposed extending the park all the way to the city limits, but so far the idea has stayed on paper.

So when you stand at the end of the Drive, you're standing at the seam between two different ideas of what a lakefront is for.
=== DEEPER The Natural | culture
Ruth Ann Steinhagen's obsession with Eddie Waitkus began when he was a Cub. She was a teenager who went to Wrigley Field again and again to watch him, and she filled her room with his pictures. When the Cubs traded him to Philadelphia after the 1948 season, she was devastated.

When the Phillies came to town in June 1949, she took a room at the Edgewater Beach Hotel, where the team was staying, and sent him the note. After the shooting, she reportedly told the police that she had decided that if she couldn't have him, nobody else would.

Waitkus nearly died. It took several operations to save him, but he came back, and in 1950 he was playing first base for the Phillies' young team, the Whiz Kids, who won the National League pennant. Friends said he was never quite the same afterward. He died in 1972, at fifty-three.

Steinhagen never stood trial. Less than three weeks after the shooting, a judge declared her insane and sent her to Kankakee State Hospital. In 1952 she was found sane again, and Waitkus, who had no wish to see her prosecuted, let the charges go. She lived the rest of her long life quietly in Chicago, and when she died, in December 2012, at eighty-three, the newspapers didn't notice for almost three months.

The writer Bernard Malamud turned the story into a kind of myth. In The Natural, published in 1952, a young pitcher named Roy Hobbs is shot in a Chicago hotel room by a strange young woman named Harriet Bird, and returns years later, past his prime, as a slugger with a homemade bat called Wonderboy. Malamud's novel ends in failure and disgrace. When it became a film in 1984, starring Robert Redford, Roy Hobbs got a happier ending, with a home run that shatters the stadium lights.
