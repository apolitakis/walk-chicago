=== STOP Holy Name Cathedral
=== DEEPER The Cardinal Who Built Big | history
For more than twenty years, the man who led this cathedral did everything on a grand scale. George Mundelein, a New Yorker of German descent, arrived as archbishop of Chicago in 1916, when he was only forty-three.

His welcome nearly killed him. That February, the city's leading men gave him a banquet at the University Club downtown, with the governor of Illinois among the guests. A cook in the club's kitchen, an anarchist named Jean Crones, had laced the soup with arsenic, and more than a hundred diners fell violently ill. The new archbishop came through it unharmed. The cook slipped away, and by most accounts he was never caught.

Mundelein went on to build as no Chicago churchman had before. He built a great seminary, Saint Mary of the Lake, on farmland north of the city, in a village that soon renamed itself Mundelein in his honor. In 1924 the pope made him a cardinal, and two years later he brought the International Eucharistic Congress to Chicago, the first time that great Catholic gathering had been held in the United States. For several days in June 1926, the city was full of pilgrims. Huge crowds filled Soldier Field for Mass, and on the last day, a crowd estimated in the hundreds of thousands, by some counts close to a million, traveled out to his new seminary.

He was outspoken, too. He was a strong supporter of Franklin Roosevelt and the New Deal, and in 1937, speaking to his priests, he called Adolf Hitler an Austrian paperhanger, and a poor one at that. The German government protested to the Vatican, and the Vatican stood by its cardinal.

He died in 1939. A great deal of the Catholic Chicago you can still see, from the seminary's lakeside campus to churches and schools across the city and suburbs, was built in his time.
=== DEEPER Cardinal Bernardin's Last Gift | people
If Mundelein was the builder, Joseph Bernardin was the peacemaker, and the way he spent his last years here moved people far beyond his own church.

He was born in Columbia, South Carolina, in 1928, the son of Italian immigrants. He became archbishop of Chicago in 1982 and a cardinal the following year. He was known as a patient consensus builder, and he argued for what he called a consistent ethic of life, linking the church's opposition to abortion with opposition to the death penalty, to nuclear war, and to poverty.

Then, in November 1993, a former seminarian named Steven Cook filed a lawsuit accusing Bernardin of abusing him years before. The cardinal denied it, calmly and in public, and within a few months Cook withdrew the claim, saying he could no longer trust memories that had surfaced under hypnosis.

What happened next is the part people remember. At the end of 1994, Bernardin went to see his accuser. Cook was dying of AIDS. They met at a seminary outside Philadelphia, and Bernardin brought a Bible and a chalice. He told Cook he forgave him, and the two men celebrated Mass together.

In 1995, Bernardin learned that he had pancreatic cancer. He spent much of the time he had left visiting other people with cancer, and he wrote a short book about facing death, The Gift of Peace. He finished it two weeks before he died, on November fourteenth, 1996.

His funeral was held here, in the cathedral he had led for fourteen years. People of many faiths, and of none, mourned him, and the story of the cardinal and his accuser at the same altar is still told as one of the most remarkable acts of forgiveness in modern American religious life.
=== DEEPER Look Up | look
If the doors are open, step inside and look up. The nave is covered by a vast wooden ceiling, made of thousands of carved and fitted pieces, some seventy feet above the floor. It has had a hard time lately.

On February twelfth, 2008, a piece of it, a chunk of wood weighing about ten pounds, broke loose and fell all the way to the floor. Engineers went up to inspect the ceiling, piece by piece, and found more than four thousand pieces that were loose or missing. The cathedral closed for emergency repairs. Crews restored the thirty-two columns that hold up the ceiling of the nave, and engineers added thirty-four steel tie rods, thirty of them hidden above the ceiling, to bind the old roof trusses and the ceiling together. Later that year, the cathedral reopened.

Then, early on the morning of February fourth, 2009, fire broke out in the attic, in the dark space between that wooden ceiling and the roof. Investigators traced it to a faulty system meant to melt ice along the roofline. Firefighters stopped the fire before it could bring the ceiling down, but the roof was badly damaged, and the water used to fight the fire soaked the sanctuary. Holy Name closed again, and it reopened that August.

So the ceiling above you has been taken apart, tied together, burned over, and soaked, and it's still there. Look for the long rows of columns that carry it, and then look high above the altar for the red hats of Chicago's cardinals, which you heard about outside.

It's a reminder that a church this old is never really finished. Every generation of Chicagoans who worship here inherits it, and has to keep it standing for the next.
=== STOP Newberry Library & Bughouse Square
=== DEEPER Mr. Newberry's Will | history
The library in front of you exists because of a will, two short lives, and a long wait.

Walter Loomis Newberry came west from Connecticut and settled in Chicago in 1833, when it was still a muddy frontier town of a few hundred people. He bought land on the North Side while it was cheap, and as the city grew over it, he became one of the richest men in Chicago, with interests in banking and railroads as well as real estate.

In 1868, on a voyage to France to join his wife and daughters, he died at sea. There's an old Chicago story, told for generations and impossible to prove, that his body came home preserved in a cask of rum, and that he was buried at Graceland Cemetery still inside it.

His will provided for his family, but it had an unusual condition. If his two daughters died without children of their own, his estate was to found a free public library on the North Side. Both daughters died young, in 1874 and 1876. After his widow died in 1885, the trustees set to work, and on July first, 1887, the Newberry Library was founded, with an estate worth more than two million dollars.

The trustees hired as librarian William Frederick Poole, who had built the Chicago Public Library from scratch after the Great Fire. Since the city already had a public library for borrowing books, the Newberry became something else: a free library where anyone with a serious question could come and read, but where the books never leave the building.

That's still how it works. You can't take anything home, but with a reader's card, the collections are yours to use, more than a century after a banker's daughters died without heirs and left their father's fortune to the city.
=== DEEPER Treasures in the Stacks | look
You can't see them from the park, but some extraordinary things are kept inside that granite building.

The Newberry's most requested treasure is a modest-looking handwritten book from around 1700. It holds the oldest known copy of the Popol Vuh, the sacred story of the K'iche' Maya people of Guatemala. A Dominican priest named Francisco Ximénez wrote it out in the K'iche' language, with a Spanish translation running beside it. Without his copy, the story might have been lost: how the gods tried to make people from mud, and then from wood, before they succeeded with maize, and how a pair of hero twins outwitted the lords of the underworld in a deadly ball game.

It came to Chicago with Edward Ayer, a businessman who made his fortune in lumber and spent much of it collecting books and documents about the Native peoples of the Americas. He began giving his collection to the Newberry in 1911.

The vaults also hold the only copy in Chicago of Shakespeare's First Folio, the collected plays published in 1623, seven years after his death. About half of his plays had never been printed before it, so without this book we might have lost Macbeth, The Tempest, and Twelfth Night. The Newberry's copy has notes scribbled in its margins by earlier owners.

And there are Chicago treasures here too: the drawings of Oswald Cooper, a Chicago lettering artist, for Cooper Black, the soft, chunky typeface of the 1920s that later turned up on countless record covers and storefront signs, and the archives of Mister Kelly's, the Rush Street nightclub you'll hear about at the next stop.

The library shows some of its treasures in free exhibitions, and it has put a great deal of its collection online, including every page of the Popol Vuh.
=== DEEPER Lucy Parsons | people
Of all the speakers who climbed onto soapboxes in this park, few had a life as dramatic as Lucy Parsons.

Even her beginnings are uncertain. She was born around 1851, in Texas or perhaps Virginia, and historians believe she was very likely born into slavery, though she herself spoke instead of Mexican and Native American ancestry. In Texas in the early 1870s, she married Albert Parsons, a former Confederate soldier who had become a Radical Republican, and the couple, facing hostility over their marriage across the color line, moved to Chicago.

Here they became leaders of the anarchist labor movement, organizing workers and campaigning for the eight-hour day. After the Haymarket bombing in 1886, Albert was among the men convicted and hanged. Lucy spent the rest of her life defending his memory and attacking the system that had killed him. She traveled the country speaking and writing, and the police broke up her meetings and arrested her again and again. A Chicago police official is said to have called her more dangerous than a thousand rioters.

In June 1905, she was one of the few women among the delegates who gathered in Chicago to found the Industrial Workers of the World, the radical union whose members later spoke in this very park. She kept speaking into old age, and for years she was one of the best-known voices at Bughouse Square.

She died on March seventh, 1942, in a fire at her home. Afterward, the authorities came and took away her library of books and her personal papers, and they were never returned.

Her life was, in a way, the whole story of this park: the conviction that ordinary people had the right to stand up in public and say what they believed, however unpopular, and the long effort of the powerful to make them stop.
=== STOP The Pump Room & the First Playboy Mansion
=== DEEPER Booth One | food
The Pump Room was the creation of Ernie Byfield, a Chicago hotel man with a gift for theater. It opened on October first, 1938, in the Ambassador East, and from the start it was as much a show as a restaurant.

Byfield loved fire. Waiters carried dishes to the tables in flames or set them alight right beside you, and the Pump Room is often credited with helping start the craze for flaming food that swept American restaurants after the Second World War. Byfield is said to have joked that the whole effect was like Halloween in hell.

The real show was in the corner: Booth One, a cream-colored leather booth where the most famous guest in the house was seated, in full view of everyone else. For years, a headwaiter named Arturo Petterino decided who got it, steering the stars into the corner and everyone else to tables where they could watch them.

Byfield died in 1950, but the Pump Room carried on for decades. What finally dimmed it wasn't the food. It was the jet plane. Once stars could fly from coast to coast in an afternoon, they no longer had to spend a night in Chicago between trains, and the parade of celebrities thinned out.

The Pump Room lived on through new owners and makeovers. After the hotel changed hands and was renamed, a restaurant called Booth One took its place for a couple of years, from 2017 until 2019.

Today you have to imagine it: the clink of glasses, the flash of a photographer's bulb, the waiter crossing the room with dinner on fire, and every head turning toward the corner to see who had been given Booth One.
=== DEEPER Mister Kelly's | culture
Mister Kelly's opened on Rush Street in November 1953, at first as a restaurant, and within a year it had a stage. It was run by two brothers, George and Oscar Marienthal, who also ran the London House, a jazz supper club downtown, and later a theater called the Happy Medium. The name came from the club's first manager, Pat Kelly, with the Mister added, the story goes, for a touch of class.

The room was small and intimate, and the brothers had a knack for booking performers just before the rest of the country discovered them. Barbra Streisand you've heard about. Sarah Vaughan recorded a live album there in 1957. Ella Fitzgerald sang there. Comedians from Lenny Bruce to Richard Pryor worked out their acts in front of its audiences, and a young Maya Angelou, long before she was famous as a writer, sang calypso there. In the last week of 1969, Dizzy Gillespie played the room.

In 1956, the brothers even put their clubs on television, in a music show broadcast in color on Chicago's NBC station, a rare thing at the time.

Oscar Marienthal died in 1963 and George in 1972, and Mister Kelly's closed in 1975, the same year as the London House. Tastes had changed, and the great age of the supper club was over.

But the club's memory is in good hands. Its records, photographs, and contracts are at the Newberry Library, which you just passed, and in 2024 the library mounted an exhibition about the club, its owners, and the performers who got their start on its little stage. For a few years in the 1950s and 1960s, if you wanted to see who was going to be famous next, a narrow room off Rush Street was the place to be.
=== DEEPER Playboy's Penthouse | history
The house at thirteen forty North State Parkway is older and more respectable than its reputation. It was built in 1899 for a surgeon, Dr. George Isham, and designed by James Gamble Rogers, the architect who later gave Northwestern its Gothic campus near the lake and gave Yale much of its look.

In 1959, the year he bought the house, Hugh Hefner also went on television. His show, Playboy's Penthouse, was taped in a Chicago studio and set in a make-believe bachelor apartment, where guests drifted around with drinks in their hands while musicians and comedians performed as if at a party. It went on the air in October 1959 and ran in syndication for two seasons.

The guest list on the first night included Nat King Cole, Ella Fitzgerald, the comedian Lenny Bruce, the songwriter Cy Coleman, and the novelist Rona Jaffe. What made the show unusual was that Black and white guests mingled as equals, sitting together, talking, and laughing, at a party broadcast on American television in 1959. Hefner said later that because of it, no stations in the Deep South would carry the show.

For the musicians, it was a rare chance to be seen at ease, talking with the host and the other guests, instead of performing one number and leaving the stage. Some of the episodes survive, and they're a remarkable record of jazz and comedy at the end of the 1950s.

A decade later, Hefner revived the idea in Los Angeles with a new show, Playboy After Dark. By then he was spending more and more of his time in California, and in the 1970s he moved there for good. But the formula, the magazine, the clubs, and the television party, had all been worked out here in Chicago, many of them behind the doors of this house.
=== STOP Astor Street & the Charnley-Persky House
=== DEEPER The Cottages by the Gulf | architecture
James Charnley and Louis Sullivan were friends as well as client and architect, and around 1890 they did something together that tells you a lot about the house in front of you.

They bought neighboring lots on the Mississippi Gulf Coast, at Ocean Springs, and built vacation cottages side by side among the pines and live oaks above the water. Sullivan's firm designed both, and like this house, they're usually seen as collaborations between Sullivan and his young draftsman, Frank Lloyd Wright. They were simple wooden houses with broad porches for the southern heat, a long way from the castles and chateaux the rich were building elsewhere.

Sullivan loved his place there, and for years he escaped to it in the winter. For a man who spent his working life in Chicago's noise and smoke, it was a kind of paradise, and he wrote about it fondly long afterward.

In 2005, Hurricane Katrina struck the coast. Sullivan's cottage was destroyed, and the Charnleys' was badly damaged. It was painstakingly restored, in work finished in 2013, and you can visit it today.

The Chicago house had its own rescue. In 1995, a philanthropist named Seymour Persky bought it and gave it to the Society of Architectural Historians for its headquarters. That's why it carries two names: Charnley for the family that built it, and Persky for the man who made sure the public could go inside.

When you look at the house, then, think of it as one of a family: a city house and two cottages by the sea, all designed in the same few years by the same two restless young minds, one of them about to become the most famous architect in America.
=== DEEPER The House That Ink Built | people
Walk a couple of blocks north to Burton Place and look at the big palazzo of brick and terra cotta on the corner. It was built in the early 1890s by Stanford White, the star New York architect, for Elinor Medill Patterson, a daughter of Joseph Medill, the great editor of the Chicago Tribune, and her husband, Robert Patterson, who worked at the Tribune and later ran it.

The Medill newspaper money produced two remarkable grandchildren, and they both grew up in the Pattersons' world. Their son, Joseph Medill Patterson, helped run the Tribune and then, in 1919, started a new kind of paper in New York, a tabloid full of photographs, crime, and scandal, called the Daily News. Within a few years it had the largest circulation of any newspaper in America.

Their daughter, Eleanor, known as Cissy, was even more colorful. After an unhappy marriage to a Polish count, she went to work for William Randolph Hearst's newspapers in Washington, bought them, and merged them into the Washington Times-Herald, making her one of the first women to run a major American daily. Their cousin, Robert McCormick, ran the Tribune itself, as you may have heard on the river tour.

After Robert Patterson died in 1910, the house was sold to Cyrus McCormick Junior, the head of International Harvester and son of the inventor of the reaper. It stayed in the family's wider circle, since Elinor's sister had married into the McCormick clan. In 1927, the society architect David Adler added a wing at the back that roughly doubled the size of the house, and in 1978 it was divided into condominiums.

For a few decades, though, this one house was home to the family behind three of the best-known newspapers in the country.
=== DEEPER The Madlener House | look
Across State Parkway from the Patterson-McCormick mansion, on the corner of Burton Place, is a house that makes a perfect companion to the Charnley House.

It was built in 1902 for Albert and Elsa Madlener, and designed by the architect Richard Schmidt, working with his gifted designer, Hugh Garden. Schmidt had worked with Louis Sullivan early in his career, and you can see the influence. Like the Charnley House, it's essentially a cube, of brick on a stone base, calm and almost severe beside the turrets and gables of its neighbors. And like the Charnley House, it saves its richness for a few chosen places. Look at the doorway, framed in intricate ornament, and at the way the windows are grouped and set into the wall.

It's a building on the hinge between two ideas. It still has the formal symmetry of an old-fashioned mansion, but its plain walls and flat horizontal lines point toward the Prairie School houses that Wright and his circle were beginning to build in Oak Park and on the South Side.

The house became a Chicago landmark in 1973. For decades it has been the home of the Graham Foundation, founded in 1956 with money left by Ernest Graham, who ran Daniel Burnham's firm after Burnham's death. His firm, Graham, Anderson, Probst and White, designed the Wrigley Building, Union Station, and the Merchandise Mart. The foundation gives grants to architects and writers on architecture, and it holds exhibitions in the house that you can usually visit for free.

So in a few blocks, you can see the whole argument of the Gold Coast: the mansions that dressed up as European palaces, and these two quiet boxes that asked what an American house should look like instead.
=== STOP Potter & Bertha Palmer's Castle
=== DEEPER The Castle's Architects | architecture
The Palmers' castle was designed by two young architects in their twenties, Henry Ives Cobb and Charles Sumner Frost, and both names turn up elsewhere on these walks. Cobb went on to design the Newberry Library and the first buildings of the University of Chicago. Frost went on to design Navy Pier.

The castle took from 1882 to 1885 to build and cost at least a million dollars, at a time when a comfortable family house cost a few thousand. Inside, it was a stage set for entertaining, with grand reception rooms where Bertha Palmer received Chicago society and a parade of famous visitors. Her family was well connected. Her sister Ida had married Frederick Dent Grant, a son of President Ulysses Grant.

During the World's Fair of 1893, the Palmers entertained royalty. The Infanta Eulalia of Spain, an aunt of the young Spanish king, came to see the fair, and the Palmers gave a grand party in her honor. The princess, the story goes, had been heard to sniff that she would not be received by an innkeeper's wife, a jab at the Palmer House hotel. Chicago took the insult personally, and the gossip outlived the visit by many years.

After the Palmers were gone, the castle became too big and too costly for anyone to want. It passed through other hands, sat empty for years, and came down, as you heard, in 1950.

What survives of it is mostly paper: photographs, floor plans, and descriptions in old society pages. But stand here and imagine it, a dark stone fortress on the lakefront, with lamps burning in its windows and carriages lining up along the drive, and you'll understand how one house could pull a whole neighborhood toward it.
=== DEEPER Modern Woman | culture
When the World's Fair opened in 1893, Bertha Palmer, as you heard, was at the head of the women who ran its Woman's Building, and she meant to make that building a statement.

The building itself was designed by a young architect named Sophia Hayden, the first woman to graduate in architecture from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. Inside were exhibits of women's work from around the world. At the dedication ceremonies for the fair, the autumn before it opened, Bertha Palmer gave a speech with a line that was widely quoted: even more important than the discovery of Columbus, she said, was the fact that the government had just discovered woman.

For the building's great hall, she wanted murals, and she persuaded Mary Cassatt, the American painter who lived in France and showed with the Impressionists, to paint one. Cassatt's mural, called Modern Woman, was enormous, nearly sixty feet long and more than twelve feet high, and it hung high on the wall. Its center showed young women in modern dress picking fruit from the tree of knowledge. The side panels showed girls chasing fame and women making music and dancing. Facing it, at the other end of the hall, was a companion mural, Primitive Woman, by another American painter, Mary MacMonnies.

The reviews were mixed. After the fair, the Woman's Building came down like most of the others, and the mural was taken away. It was last recorded in 1912, and then it simply vanished. No one knows whether it was destroyed or is rolled up in some forgotten storeroom. The only color image of it is a reproduction printed in a book about the fair.

In 2026, the Art Institute told its story in a major Cassatt exhibition. It remains one of the great lost artworks of Chicago, commissioned by the woman who lived on this spot.
=== DEEPER Mrs. Palmer of Sarasota | people
Potter Palmer died in 1902 and left his fortune to Bertha. There's a story that when his lawyer warned that she might marry again, Palmer replied that if she did, her second husband would need the money. Whether he really said it or not, she never remarried, and she proved perfectly able to manage what he left her.

In 1910, when she was about sixty, she went to look at a sleepy town on the west coast of Florida called Sarasota. She had seen it advertised, the story goes, in a Chicago newspaper. She liked it so much that she began buying land, and within a few years she owned more than eighty thousand acres in and around the town.

She ran it like a business. On one stretch she started a cattle ranch called Meadowsweet Pastures, and she brought new methods to Florida ranching, fencing her herds in and dipping the cattle in vats to kill the ticks that spread disease. She experimented with crops, built roads, and sold land to settlers, and her name drew other wealthy northerners to the area. For herself, she made a winter estate on the bay at Osprey Point, with gardens that are still preserved as part of a historic site.

She died in 1918. Years later, much of her ranch land became Myakka River State Park, one of the oldest and largest state parks in Florida, where alligators bask along the river and the old Florida landscape she knew is still protected.

Sarasota remembers her as one of the founders of the modern city. The woman who made a Chicago castle the center of society ended her life as one of the largest landowners on the Gulf Coast of Florida, and in many ways she was the more remarkable businessperson of the two Palmers.
=== STOP The Museum of Surgical Science
=== DEEPER Dr. Thorek's College | people
The museum in this mansion grew out of one Chicago surgeon's very big idea.

Max Thorek was born in Hungary in 1880 and came to Chicago with his family in 1897, as a teenager without much money. He was determined to become a doctor, and according to the museum's own telling, he learned to play the drums so that he could join the university band and help pay his way. He earned his medical degree in 1904, and a few years later, with another young doctor, Solomon Greenspahn, he founded the American Hospital on the North Side. It later took his name, and today it's Thorek Memorial Hospital.

He was a surgeon with ambitions far beyond his own operating room. He wrote textbooks and a lively autobiography, A Surgeon's World, and in 1935 he founded the International College of Surgeons, in Geneva, Switzerland, to bring surgeons from many countries together at a time when the world was drifting toward war. The college made its headquarters here in Chicago, in a mansion on this same stretch of Lake Shore Drive.

The college began collecting: old surgical instruments, paintings, books, and manuscripts, meant at first for a hall of fame honoring surgery's heroes. Members and friends sent objects from around the world, and on September ninth, 1954, the collection opened to the public as this museum. One of the first exhibits was the Hall of Immortals, a gallery of twelve large stone statues of great figures from the history of medicine.

Thorek died in 1960. He left behind a hospital, a worldwide society of surgeons, and this odd and wonderful museum, which still surprises nearly everyone who walks through its elegant front door expecting just another old mansion.
=== DEEPER The Architect of the North Shore | architecture
Howard Van Doren Shaw, who designed this mansion, was the architect Chicago's rich families called when they wanted a house that looked like old money.

He was born in Chicago in 1869, studied at Yale and then studied architecture at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and opened his own practice in the 1890s. Most of his work was houses: great country houses in Lake Forest and along the North Shore, and city houses like this one, in English, Italian, and French styles, always beautifully built.

His own house was more personal. In 1897 he built Ragdale, a rambling Arts and Crafts summer home in Lake Forest. It stayed in his family, and since the 1970s it has been a retreat where writers and artists come to work.

In Lake Forest he also designed Market Square, finished in 1916, a little town center of shops and offices arranged around a green, built with a developer named Arthur Aldis. It's often described as one of the first planned shopping centers in America, and it still looks like a village square from an English novel.

You may have met his work elsewhere on these walks: the old Lakeside Press building for the printer R. R. Donnelley, in the South Loop, and the parish buildings around the courtyard of Fourth Presbyterian Church on Michigan Avenue. He died in 1926, still in his fifties.

His daughter, Sylvia Shaw Judson, became a sculptor, and one of her pieces, a small bronze of a girl holding up two bowls, became famous decades later as the Bird Girl, the statue on the cover of the bestselling book Midnight in the Garden of Good and Evil. Talent, it seems, ran in the family, and it ran from grand houses to a little girl in a Savannah cemetery.
=== DEEPER The Oldest Operation | history
Among the strangest things in the museum are the skulls with holes cut in them. They're evidence of the oldest surgery we know of, and the story they tell is more hopeful than you might expect.

The operation is called trepanation: cutting, scraping, or drilling an opening through the skull of a living person. People were doing it thousands of years before anyone wrote anything down. Skulls with these openings have been found at Stone Age burial sites across Europe and in many other parts of the world, and some of the most remarkable come from ancient Peru, where surgeons of the Inca and earlier peoples performed the operation often.

How do we know the patients lived? Bone heals. When someone survived for months or years after the operation, the edges of the opening grew smooth and rounded with new bone. Studies of skulls from Peru suggest that by Inca times, a large majority of patients survived. Over the centuries, the surgeons seem to have learned which cuts were safest.

Why did they do it? Some patients had head wounds, perhaps from clubs or sling stones, and opening the skull may have relieved pressure or removed broken bone. Others may have been treated for headaches, seizures, or illnesses of the mind, or for reasons we can only guess at, such as letting out an evil spirit.

The idea never completely disappeared. Surgeons in Europe were still opening skulls to treat head injuries in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and modern neurosurgeons do it too, with far better tools, for some of the same reasons.

So as you look at those skulls, think of the patients who walked away from those operations, thousands of years before anesthesia or antiseptics, and lived long enough for their bones to heal.
=== STOP The Archbishop's Residence
=== DEEPER The First Archbishop | people
The man who built this house was Patrick Feehan, Chicago's first archbishop.

He was born in Ireland, in County Tipperary, in 1829, and came to America as a young priest in the early 1850s to serve in Saint Louis. He spent fifteen years as bishop of Nashville, and in 1880, when the pope raised Chicago to an archdiocese, Feehan was chosen to lead it.

His Chicago was a city of immigrants, and his great challenge was keeping them together in one church. Germans, Poles, Bohemians, Italians, Lithuanians, and others each wanted priests who spoke their language and churches built in the style of home. Feehan generally let them have them, allowing what were called national parishes, so that a Polish family and an Irish family living on the same street might belong to different parishes, a few blocks apart. Under him, the number of parishes and Catholic schools in the archdiocese grew enormously.

In 1885, he built this residence, on what was then the quiet northern edge of the settled city, beside the old cemetery that was becoming Lincoln Park. Before that, the bishops of Chicago had lived in a house a few blocks west, at LaSalle Street and North Avenue.

He died in 1902. Every archbishop of Chicago after him lived in this house, with its nineteen chimneys, for well over a century, until one of them decided not to.
=== DEEPER A House Too Big | history
In the fall of 2014, a new archbishop arrived in Chicago, Blase Cupich, and he made an announcement that surprised a lot of people: he would not live in this mansion.

He moved instead into the rectory at Holy Name Cathedral, where you started this tour, living alongside the parish priests. He was following the example of Pope Francis, who after his election the year before had chosen to live in a Vatican guesthouse instead of the grand papal apartments.

The residence stayed in use for official events and to house important guests, but in 2019 the archdiocese said it was considering selling it. Like many dioceses, the church in Chicago has been closing and merging parishes as congregations shrink, and the cost of keeping up a mansion of this size, with all those chimneys, is hard to justify.

Whatever becomes of it, the house tells a story about how the church once saw itself in this city: a powerful institution, with a palace for its leader on the most fashionable edge of town, grand enough for a pope to stay in. The choice to leave it tells a story too, about a church trying to look less like a power and more like a neighbor.

Stand on the sidewalk and look at it for a moment. Few buildings in Chicago carry as much history so quietly, and few have outlived the world they were built for so completely.
=== DEEPER Why So Many Chimneys | look
Those nineteen chimneys weren't only decoration. When this house was built, fireplaces were still part of how a big house was heated and how its rooms were judged. Even in homes with furnaces, every important room had a fireplace of its own, and every fireplace needed its own flue to carry the smoke up and out. A grand house meant many fires, and many fires meant many chimneys.

Keeping them going was hard work. Servants carried coal and wood up from the basement, tended the fires, and hauled the ashes back down, all winter long. As steam and hot water heating spread in the following decades, the forest of chimneys on houses like this one began to look old-fashioned.

The architects of the 1880s turned the necessity into ornament. In the Queen Anne style, chimneys were made tall and decorative, often grouped in clusters and built of patterned brick, and they helped give a house its lively, irregular skyline. Look at the roof as a whole: steep slopes meeting at different angles, gables and dormers popping out, very little quite symmetrical. That restless outline was the point. A Queen Anne house was meant to look as if it had grown over time, like an old English manor added to by generations.

Now think back to the Charnley House and the Madlener House, built only a few years later. Their outlines are calm and boxlike, with no parade of chimneys and gables. In less than twenty years, the most adventurous architects in Chicago had moved from a skyline of chimneys and gables to the plain, clean block. You can see both ideas on this one short walk.
