=== DEEPER Sankey's Songs | culture
You heard that Moody toured with the gospel singer Ira Sankey. The two met in 1870, at a convention of the Young Men's Christian Association in Indianapolis. Sankey was a big, genial man from western Pennsylvania who worked in the federal tax office back home in New Castle. As the story is usually told, the singing at an early morning prayer meeting was dragging, and someone asked Sankey to start a hymn. Afterward Moody came over, asked him a few questions, and told him he would have to give up his job and come to Chicago, because he had been looking for him for eight years. Sankey took about six months to make up his mind.

His gift was making a simple song carry. He sang solos at a small reed organ, slowly and clearly, so that every word reached the back of a hall holding thousands. Moody preached, and Sankey sang the message home.

The most famous story about him comes from Scotland, in 1874. On a train to Edinburgh, Sankey found a poem in a newspaper about a shepherd who leaves his ninety-nine sheep to search for the one that strayed, and he clipped it out. A day or two later, at a meeting in Edinburgh, Moody preached on the Good Shepherd and turned to Sankey for a closing song. Sankey had nothing ready, so he set the clipping on his organ, struck a chord, and made up the tune as he sang. That's the story Sankey told, and The Ninety and Nine became one of the best-loved gospel songs of the age. But an account published in 1876 says the tune was written beforehand. Students of hymns point out that the first printed version uses only three chords, which is just what you'd expect from a melody invented on the spot.

Their songbooks sold by the hundreds of thousands. Sankey gave his royalties away, in later years to the schools Moody founded in Massachusetts. He spent his last years blind, and he died at his home in Brooklyn in 1908.
=== DEEPER The Ballplayer Preacher | people
In the years after Moody's death, the title of America's most famous evangelist passed to a man Chicago had first cheered as a ballplayer.

Billy Sunday grew up poor in Iowa. His father, a Union soldier, died in the Civil War when Billy was a baby, and the boy spent part of his childhood in orphans' homes. He was fast, fast enough that in 1883 the Chicago White Stockings, the team that later became the Cubs, signed him as an outfielder. He was never much of a hitter, but crowds loved to watch him run.

One night in 1886, as he told it, he was sitting on a curb on Van Buren Street with some teammates after an evening of drinking, when a band from the Pacific Garden Mission came by, playing hymns his mother used to sing. He followed it inside, and soon he was converted. He played a few more seasons, but in 1891 he gave up baseball for a job with the Young Men's Christian Association in Chicago, at a fraction of the pay. He learned the revival business as an advance man for a traveling preacher, and then struck out on his own.

From the early 1900s through the First World War, Sunday preached to enormous crowds in temporary wooden tabernacles with sawdust spread on the floors. When he called sinners forward, they walked down the sawdust aisles, and hitting the sawdust trail became an American expression for getting saved. His preaching was pure theater. He leapt onto chairs and slid across the platform like a runner stealing second. He was one of the loudest voices for Prohibition, and his sermon against liquor, known simply as Booze, was famous.

Chicago never quite surrendered to him. In 1922, the songwriter Fred Fisher published Chicago, That Toddlin' Town, which calls it the town that Billy Sunday could not shut down.

Sunday died in Chicago in November 1935. He is buried in Forest Home Cemetery, in the western suburb of Forest Park, not far from the monument to the anarchists of the Haymarket affair.
=== DEEPER Moody's Schools | history
Moody had little formal schooling, and his grammar was famously rough, but he started schools wherever he went. In 1879, in his hometown of Northfield, Massachusetts, he opened a seminary for girls, and two years later, across the Connecticut River, a school for boys called Mount Hermon. They were meant for young people of modest means. In 1971 the two merged into one school, Northfield Mount Hermon, which is still there.

The Chicago school had a founder of its own, and it wasn't only Moody. Her name was Emma Dryer. She was a teacher who came to Chicago around the time of the Great Fire, and in 1873 Moody's church hired her to run its Bible work, training women to visit families in the poorest parts of the city. For years she urged Moody to open a real training school, and in 1883 she started a gathering for Bible study and prayer called the May Institute.

Moody kept putting it off. Then, early in 1886, he told a meeting in Chicago that the city needed what he called gap-men: ordinary men and women, trained in the Bible, to stand in the gap between the ministers and the masses who never went to church. The institute counts its founding from that year, and early in 1887 the Chicago Evangelization Society was formally organized to do the work. After Moody died, it took his name.

For a long time, Dryer was a footnote in the official story. Today the school's own archives credit her as the person who did the most to bring it about, and in 2019 the institute announced new tributes to her on its campus.

The schools shared one idea with the man himself: that faith was something you did, not just something you believed. Moody's students learned to preach on street corners, visit tenements and lead singing, and many of them went out as missionaries all over the world. The institute still trains them, about a mile south of here.
=== DEEPER The Men Who Sang for Lincoln | look
Before you walk west, look south down Clark Street, across North Avenue. On the right-hand side, a short block away, stands a grand clubhouse in the style of the Renaissance. It is the Germania Club building, and it has one of the most unusual origin stories in Chicago.

In the spring of 1865, Abraham Lincoln's body came through Chicago on its way home to Springfield, and it lay in state at the courthouse downtown while tens of thousands of people filed past. A group of German Americans, many of them veterans of the Union Army, formed a men's chorus to sing for the occasion. The chorus became a club, and the club became one of the centers of German life in the city. In the late 1880s it built this clubhouse, designed by the firm of Addison and Fiedler, where members gathered for concerts, dinners, dances and long evenings of song.

Chicago was one of the most German cities in America then. The North Side had German newspapers, German theaters, German gymnastic societies and German beer gardens, and on many blocks you heard more German than English.

Then came the First World War. When the United States declared war on Germany in 1917, anything German became suspect. Across the country, schools dropped German classes, sauerkraut was renamed liberty cabbage, and families changed their names to sound less foreign. The Germania Club changed its name too. It became the Lincoln Club, reaching back to its own beginnings for a name nobody could call unpatriotic. A few years after the war, it quietly took its old name back.

But the old German world never fully recovered. The war, and then Prohibition, which closed the beer gardens and the breweries, broke up much of the public life that had held it together, and the families slowly moved on to other neighborhoods and suburbs. The building was named a city landmark in 2011, a reminder of the men who sang for Lincoln.

=== DEEPER Viola Spolin's Games | people
You heard that the Second City's improvisation games came from Paul Sills's mother, Viola Spolin. Her own teacher was a remarkable woman named Neva Boyd, who ran a training school for recreation workers at Hull House, the settlement house on the Near West Side. Spolin began studying with her in 1924. Boyd believed that play was serious business. Games, folk dances and storytelling, she taught, could build confidence and cooperation, and help the children of immigrants from a dozen countries, who might not share a language, learn to get along.

In 1939, Spolin went to work for the Works Progress Administration, the New Deal jobs program, teaching drama in Chicago's neighborhoods. Many of her students were immigrants and their children, and she found that scripts got in the way. So she invented games instead. Each one gave the players a simple problem to solve. In one, two players face each other, and one follows the other's every move, like a reflection in a mirror. In another, the players speak only in made-up gibberish, so they have to get their meaning across with their bodies and voices. In another, they have to show the audience where they are, a kitchen or a bus stop, without ever saying it.

While the players worked, she called out instructions from the side, the way a coach shouts from the sidelines, and she called it side-coaching. The point was to get the actors out of their heads and into the moment.

After the war she moved to California, and her son carried her games to the University of Chicago. When the Compass Players started in Hyde Park in 1955, she came back to help train the first company. In 1963 she gathered more than two hundred of her games and exercises into a book, Improvisation for the Theater, dedicated in part to Neva Boyd. It is still in print, and it's used everywhere from acting schools to classrooms to business training.
=== DEEPER The Compass | history
In July 1955, Paul Sills and a producer named David Shepherd opened a new kind of theater in the back room of a bar near the University of Chicago, on 55th Street in Hyde Park. The building is gone. A fire station stands on the spot today, at 55th Street and University Avenue.

Shepherd, a New Yorker with family money and radical ideas, wanted a theater for ordinary people, as quick and cheap as a newspaper. So the Compass Players didn't perform written plays. They worked from scenarios, a page or so of plot, and made up the dialogue every night. When the scenarios ran short, they took suggestions from the audience and improvised short scenes on the spot, using Viola Spolin's games.

The actors were extraordinary. Elaine May, a brilliant, sharp-tongued young woman who sat in on university classes without enrolling, was in the first company. Mike Nichols, who had left the university, joined soon after. Shelley Berman, later famous for his one-sided telephone calls, was there, and so was Barbara Harris, who went on to star on Broadway and in the movies.

Nichols and May discovered that together they were magic. Their two-person scenes, a smothering mother on the phone with her scientist son, a pair of nervous teenagers parked on a date, became the core of an act that made them stars in New York within a couple of years. In 1960 they opened on Broadway in An Evening with Mike Nichols and Elaine May. Later, Nichols became one of the great directors of stage and screen, and May a writer and director herself.

The Compass in Chicago didn't last. It moved from one spot to another and folded after about two years. But its ideas didn't fold. The scenario, the audience suggestion, the games and the trust between players all went north to Wells Street, where Sills and his partners built the Second City on them. Nearly every improv theater in the world can trace its family tree back to that back room on 55th Street.
=== DEEPER Second City Television | culture
In 1973, the Second City opened a theater in Toronto, and a few years later its company there made a television show. Second City Television, known by its initials, S C T V, imagined the programming of a tiny, cheap TV station in a made-up town called Melonville. Each episode flipped through the channel's whole schedule: the news, soap operas, talk shows, commercials and late-night horror movies. The actors played both the station's staff and the stars of its shows, and they wrote most of the material themselves.

The cast was a dream team: John Candy, Joe Flaherty, Eugene Levy, Andrea Martin, Catherine O'Hara, Harold Ramis and Dave Thomas, later joined by Rick Moranis and Martin Short. Several of them had worked the stage here on Wells Street. Ramis was the show's first head writer, before he left for Hollywood, where he co-wrote Animal House, Caddyshack and Ghostbusters.

Some of its characters became cult favorites. Flaherty played Count Floyd, the host of a horror-movie show who tried hard to be scary and never once was. Andrea Martin played Edith Prickley, the station manager, in loud leopard print. Levy and Candy played the Schmenge brothers, a pair of mournful polka musicians.

And then there were Bob and Doug McKenzie, two dim brothers in wool caps who sat in front of a map of Canada, drinking beer and talking about nothing at all. They were born when the show moved to the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation, which wanted two extra minutes of distinctly Canadian content in each episode. Moranis and Thomas, half in protest, improvised the most Canadian thing they could imagine. The bit became a hit record and a movie.

Starting in 1981, an American network ran the show in a longer, late-night version, and it won Emmy Awards for its writing. Its alumni are still everywhere. Decades later, Eugene Levy and Catherine O'Hara were still playing off each other, as the parents on the comedy Schitt's Creek.

John Prine was born in 1946 and grew up in Maywood, just west of the city. His parents came from Muhlenberg County, in the coal country of western Kentucky, and years later he wrote a song, Paradise, about the little town there that his family knew, and the coal company that strip-mined it away.

He served in the Army, much of it in West Germany, and then went back to delivering mail. Around 1970, at an open-mic night at a folk club called the Fifth Peg, on Armitage Avenue, he grumbled that the singers weren't very good, and somebody dared him to do better. He got up and played three of his own songs, and the room went quiet. That's how he told it, anyway.

On another night in 1970, Roger Ebert, the film critic of the Sun-Times, wandered into the Fifth Peg. As the story goes, he had walked out of a bad movie and stopped in for a beer. He heard Prine and wrote a rave, published on October ninth, 1970, under the headline Singing Mailman Who Delivers a Powerful Message in a Few Words. It was the first review Prine ever got.

His friend Steve Goodman did the rest. In 1971, Goodman dragged Kris Kristofferson, then one of the hottest songwriters in the country, to hear Prine sing. Kristofferson joked that Prine was so good they'd have to break his thumbs, and he invited both young men to open for him at the Bitter End, a folk club in New York. The next morning, Jerry Wexler of Atlantic Records offered Prine a contract.

His first album came out that year, with Sam Stone, Angel from Montgomery, Hello in There and Paradise. For the next half century he was the songwriter other songwriters admired most, from Bob Dylan to Bonnie Raitt. He died on April seventh, 2020, of complications from Covid, in the first weeks of the pandemic.
=== DEEPER City of New Orleans | culture
Steve Goodman learned he had leukemia when he was barely into his twenties, and he lived the rest of his life at full speed, as if he knew his time was short.

He wrote his most famous song after a train ride. He and his wife took the Illinois Central Railroad's daytime train, the City of New Orleans, downstate to Mattoon, Illinois, to visit her family, and Goodman sat by the window and wrote down what went by: freight yards and farm towns, junkyards of rusting cars, old men playing cards in the club car. The song became a farewell to the passenger train itself, which was disappearing all over America in those years.

The famous story is how Arlo Guthrie came to record it. Goodman spotted Guthrie at a Chicago bar, the Quiet Knight, and asked to play him a song. Guthrie grudgingly agreed, so the story goes, on one condition: if Goodman bought him a beer, Guthrie would listen for as long as it took to drink it. Goodman played City of New Orleans, and Guthrie loved it. His recording became a top-twenty hit in 1972.

In 1984, Willie Nelson made it the title song of an album, and the next year it won the Grammy for best country song. Goodman had died a few months before, so the award came too late for him to accept it.

His friendship with John Prine produced another classic. Goodman wrote You Never Even Call Me by My Name, the song that calls itself the perfect country and western song, with Prine's help, and David Allan Coe made it a hit. Prine, the story goes, refused to take a songwriting credit, and Goodman thanked him with a jukebox.

The train still runs. Amtrak's City of New Orleans now leaves Chicago in the evening and rolls into New Orleans the next afternoon.
=== DEEPER Win and Frank's School | history
The Old Town School began in a living room in Oak Park. In the mid-1950s, a young guitarist from Los Angeles named Frank Hamilton was giving guitar lessons in the home of Dawn Greening, a folk music lover there. One of his students was Win Stracke, and Stracke saw that Hamilton's way of teaching could be the basis of a whole school.

Stracke was a big-voiced singer and actor who had worked in Chicago radio and early television, until the blacklist of the McCarthy years caught up with him. He was dropped from a television soap opera for his politics, and Studs' Place, the show he did with his friend Studs Terkel, was canceled. The two liked to call themselves the Chicago Two.

The school opened on December first, 1957, and the great bluesman Big Bill Broonzy played on opening night. He was already ill, and he died the next summer.

Stracke wanted every class to end with what he called the Second Half, a jam session where students of every level played and sang together, so that beginners learned by joining in with players better than they were. It made the place feel less like a school than a club, and people came back for years. Among the teenagers who took lessons in the early days was Jim McGuinn, who later, as Roger McGuinn, led the Byrds.

Hamilton moved on after a few years and for a time played with the Weavers, the folk group whose hits had helped launch the revival. Stracke stayed on as the school's first director, as the folk boom filled its classrooms.

In 1968 the school moved to Armitage Avenue, and since 1998 its main home has been a former public library on Lincoln Avenue, an Art Deco building that it renovated, with a concert hall where you can still hear folk, blues and music from all over the world. === DEEPER Brick or Wood | politics
After the fire of 1871, Chicago's business leaders and newspapers were determined that the city would never burn like that again, and they pushed for fire limits: a line inside which no new wooden buildings would be allowed. Many of them wanted the line drawn at the city limits, which would have banned wood everywhere.

On the North Side, that sounded like a sentence of exile. Many of the people here were German and Irish working families who had owned small frame houses and lost them in the fire. They could afford to rebuild in wood. Brick was out of reach. A ban would turn owners into renters, or push them out beyond the city line.

So in January 1872, when the city council took up the question, the North Siders marched on it. Their homemade banners, misspelled in a newcomer's English, said No Fire Limits on the North Side, and Leave a House for the Laborer. Accounts differ on how rough things got when they reached the council chamber.

The council gave way. The fire limits it passed that year left much of the North Side outside the line, and families here rebuilt in wood, quickly, often on the same lots. Many of the wooden cottages around the Triangle date from the years right after the fire.

The victory didn't last. On July fourteenth, 1874, a second big fire burned more than eight hundred buildings just south of downtown. This time the insurance companies took charge. Their national board demanded bigger water mains, a reorganized fire department, and a ban on wooden buildings everywhere in the city. When the council dragged its feet, the board told its members to cancel their Chicago policies. A city nobody would insure couldn't do business, and the council extended the fire limits to the city line.

The builders of cheap frame houses simply moved outside the city, to the towns that Chicago swallowed in the great annexation of 1889. That's one reason so many wooden cottages stand in neighborhoods that were once beyond the city limits.
=== DEEPER Edgar Miller's Studios | architecture
About a ten-minute walk south of here, across North Avenue, on a short street called Burton Place, is one of the most enchanting buildings in Chicago. It's known as the Carl Street Studios, because Burton Place was called Carl Street when the building was made over. The name changed in the 1930s.

In 1927, an artist named Sol Kogen bought an old mansion there and asked his friend Edgar Miller to help turn it into studios and apartments for artists. Miller was a young artist who had come to Chicago from Idaho to study at the Art Institute. The two had very little money, so they built with whatever they could salvage from buildings being torn down around the city: common bricks, oak doors and beams, sheets of marble, wrought iron, copper washtubs, even old plumbing pipes. During the Depression, Kogen bought tiles by the truckload.

Miller did almost everything by hand. He carved doors and beams, made stained glass, laid mosaics, painted murals and modeled sculpture, and he filled the place with animals, figures and patterns. The courtyard and the front are a riot of tile, carved wood and brick laid in odd designs. Inside, the apartments climb through rooms on different levels, with built-in benches, little balconies, winding stairs and windows of jewel-colored glass. It looks like a fairy tale crossed with Art Deco.

The partners went their separate ways around 1935, but Kogen and others remade more houses on the street in the same spirit, and today Burton Place is listed as a historic district.

Miller lived until 1993 and kept working for decades, but for years he was nearly forgotten. A book, Edgar Miller and the Handmade Home, and a group devoted to his legacy have brought his work back into view. If you take the Little Italy tour, you'll meet more of it: a parade of stone animals he made for a public housing project.

The studios are private homes, so admire them from the sidewalk.
=== DEEPER How to Read a Cottage | look
Here's how to read the old houses as you wander the Triangle.

Start with the lot. Chicago's builders laid out most house lots twenty-five feet wide and about a hundred and twenty-five feet deep, so the classic worker's cottage is narrow and long, with its gable end facing the street. That steep triangle of roof over the front is the signature of the type.

Next, count the stories. Most cottages are one story or one and a half, the half being an attic room tucked under the roof, often lit by a small window in the gable. Many sit on a raised basement, with a few steps up to the front door. Owners often rented out the lower level, or put a second little house at the back of the lot, and the rent helped pay the mortgage.

Look at the door. On many cottages it's set off to one side, opening into a narrow hall, with two windows beside it. Then look up at the eaves. Many have brackets, scrolls and a bit of molding, Italianate trim that came ready-made from planing mills. In the decades after the Civil War, Chicago was the biggest lumber market in the world, and doors, windows and trim arrived by the carload.

How did they go up so fast? With the balloon frame: a skeleton of light, machine-sawn studs nailed together, instead of heavy timbers joined by skilled carpenters. Chicago likes to claim it invented the method in the 1830s. Whether or not that's true, it was perfect for a city in a hurry.

Finally, look for the signs of later lives. A brick front added to a wooden house. A porch that came and went. A dormer squeezed into the roof for another bedroom. And the careful restorations of the last sixty years or so, since the artists and young families who saved this neighborhood started fixing up houses nobody else wanted. Some of those modest cottages now sell for well over a million dollars.
=== STOP Saint Michael's Church
=== DEEPER The Brewer's Gift | history
The parish began with a gift from a brewer. Michael Diversey was a German immigrant who, with his partner William Lill, ran one of the leading breweries in early Chicago.

By the early 1850s, German families were pouring onto the cheap land around North Avenue, then the edge of the city, and the Catholics among them wanted a church of their own, where the sermons and the hymns would be in German. In 1852, Diversey gave them a plot of land near North Avenue, and they put up a small wooden church. It was dedicated on October seventeenth, 1852, with forty-three families, and it cost about seven hundred and fifty dollars, bell included. It was named for Saint Michael the Archangel, and, as the story is told, as a quiet thank you to the man who gave the land.

The parish grew with astonishing speed. Within a few years the frame church was far too small, and in 1860 the parish was handed to the Redemptorists, an order of priests founded in Italy in the 1700s and known for preaching missions, revivals that could last a week or more. The first Redemptorist pastor said his first Mass here in February 1860. The great brick church followed within the decade.

By the end of the century, Saint Michael's was one of the biggest German parishes in the city, with its own schools, choirs, mutual aid societies and clubs for every age. A child could be baptized, schooled, married and buried without ever leaving the parish, and many were.

Diversey's name lives on, though most people who say it have no idea who he was. Diversey Parkway, a mile and a half north of here, is named for him. So the next time you hear a Chicagoan say Diversey, you'll know there's a brewer behind it, and behind him, the church in front of you.
=== DEEPER The Lager Beer Riot | politics
In March 1855, Chicago elected a mayor from the Know-Nothing movement, a party that wanted to limit the power of immigrants and Catholics. The new mayor, Levi Boone, a doctor and a relative of the frontiersman Daniel Boone, barred immigrants from city jobs. Then he went after their drinking.

His council raised the price of a liquor license sixfold, from fifty dollars to three hundred, and cut its term from a year to three months, and the city began enforcing an old law that closed taverns on Sundays. For the Germans of the North Side, a Sunday afternoon in a beer garden with the family was a cherished custom, and they saw the new rules as aimed squarely at them and at the Irish.

Many saloonkeepers defied the law, and the city arrested them. On April twenty-first, 1855, a test case came to trial at the courthouse downtown, and a crowd of supporters marched south from the North Side to fill the square outside. The mayor had the Clark Street bridge swung open to cut off the rest of the marchers on the north bank of the river. When the bridge swung back and the crowd surged across, the police were waiting, and shooting broke out. Boone called out the militia, and the fight was over quickly. At least one man was dead, and some sixty people had been arrested.

The Know-Nothings won the street fight and lost the war. The next March, German and Irish voters turned out in force and threw them out of office, and the fifty-dollar license came back. Historians date the start of hard-fought party politics in Chicago's city elections to that riot.

It was the first of many battles in Chicago over beer, from Sunday closing laws to Prohibition, and the North Side Germans won most of them. So the next time you order a beer in Chicago on a Sunday afternoon, you might lift it to the North Siders who marched to the river in 1855.
=== DEEPER The Policeman's Cottage | legends
The fire that gutted Saint Michael's kept moving north, and almost nothing in its path survived. One famous exception stands about half a mile north of here, at twenty-one twenty-one North Hudson Avenue: a small wooden cottage with Italianate trim, built in 1869 for a police officer named Richard Bellinger. It was designed by William W. Boyington, the architect of the Water Tower on Michigan Avenue, one of the other famous survivors of the fire.

On the second day of the fire, as the flames came up from the south, Bellinger decided to fight. He tore up the wooden sidewalk in front of the house and pulled down the wooden fence, so the fire would have nothing to creep along. He raked away the dry leaves. Then he soaked the roof and walls. The pumps at the waterworks had stopped hours before, and the water soon ran out.

And here's where the legend begins. When the water was gone, the story goes, Bellinger went down to his cellar, brought up his barrels of cider, and used them to soak the roof and put out every spark that landed on it. The cottage that cider saved has been a Chicago favorite ever since.

Did it happen? It's a wonderful story, and it has been told for more than a century and a half. But in 1915, according to old newspaper reports, Mrs. Bellinger herself dismissed the cider story. Whatever saved the house, whether water, cider, hard work or a lucky shift in the wind, it's one of the very few buildings in the burned district that came through the fire.

Neighbors have been arguing about the cider for generations, a little like the arguments over Mrs. O'Leary's cow. If you walk up Hudson Avenue, you can stand on the sidewalk and decide for yourself. It's a private home, so admire it from there.

=== DEEPER Reverend Kono | people
The temple's founder was a minister named Gyodo Kono. Like the families he served, he was sent to an incarceration camp during the war, at Rohwer, in the swampy Delta country of southeastern Arkansas. After his release he came to Chicago, and on July tenth, 1944, he founded what was then called the Midwest Buddhist Church. Inside, as in most temples of this tradition, the altar holds a golden image of Amida Buddha, standing and leaning slightly forward, as if already stepping toward anyone who calls his name. Around it are candles, flowers and incense, and on special occasions the altar holds offerings of fruit and rice cakes.

=== DEEPER The Resettlers | history
You heard that Chicago became the biggest destination for Japanese Americans leaving the camps. That was no accident. The War Relocation Authority, the federal agency that ran the camps, opened its very first field office here, in the Loop, on January fourth, 1943. It was run by Elmer Shirrell, who had been the first director of the Tule Lake camp in California.

To leave a camp, a prisoner had to pass a security review and usually needed a job or a sponsor waiting on the outside. The first to come were mostly young, single and American-born, the children of immigrants, and many arrived with little more than a suitcase and an address. Churches and volunteer groups opened hostels to tide them over, among them the Church of the Brethren and the American Friends Service Committee, the relief agency of the Quakers.

Wartime Chicago had plenty of work, in factories, offices, hotels and private homes. Housing was harder. Many landlords wouldn't rent to them, and many newcomers found rooms in old boardinghouses and cheap flats near the corner of Clark and Division. Officials urged them to spread out and blend in, but people also needed one another, and the community built its own institutions.

In 1946, resettlers and the government set up the Chicago Resettlers Committee, to help newcomers find homes, jobs and their footing. In 1954 it became the Japanese American Service Committee, which still serves the community today and keeps an archive of its history.

The old neighborhood around Clark and Division didn't survive. In the 1960s, much of it was cleared for the Sandburg Village high-rises, and the families and shops moved north along Clark Street.

Many of the young people who arrived with a suitcase in 1943 lived the rest of their lives here. Some of their grandchildren still come to this temple.

If you come to a Sunday service here, parts of it may feel surprisingly familiar. In many temples of this tradition there are rows of seats facing the altar, a service book, hymns called gathas sung to piano or organ, and a Sunday school for the children, called Dharma school.

The tradition is Jodo Shinshu, or True Pure Land Buddhism, founded in Japan in the thirteenth century by a monk named Shinran. Its teaching is simple at its heart. You can't reach enlightenment by your own efforts, however hard you try. Instead you entrust yourself to the boundless compassion of Amida Buddha. The central practice is the Nembutsu, saying Namu Amida Butsu, not to earn anything, but in gratitude. It was the faith of most of the Japanese immigrants who came to Hawaii and the West Coast.

In America, the temples took on some of the forms of Protestant churches, partly to fit in and partly to hold on to their American-born children. The war turned that into a matter of survival. In 1944, at a meeting held inside the Topaz camp in Utah, the national body of Jodo Shinshu temples renamed itself the Buddhist Churches of America. The word churches was chosen to sound less foreign. The organization also made English its official language and loosened its formal ties to the head temple in Japan. That same year, this congregation was founded as the Midwest Buddhist Church. Many of these congregations, this one included, have since gone back to calling themselves temples.

Each summer, the temple also holds Obon, a festival for remembering those who have died, with Bon Odori, circle dances that anyone can join. The steps are simple, the drums are loud, and nobody minds if you get them wrong.
