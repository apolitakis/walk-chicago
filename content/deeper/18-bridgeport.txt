=== DEEPER The Hitless Wonders | history
Charles Comiskey, whose name was on the old ballpark across the street, was a Chicago boy. He was born here in 1859, the son of an Irish immigrant known as Honest John Comiskey, who sat on the city council. Charles chose baseball over politics, and by twenty-three he was managing the Saint Louis Browns while playing first base. His Browns won four pennants in a row, from 1885 to 1888, and in 1886 they beat Chicago's National League champions, the White Stockings, in a series for the championship of the world.

In 1894, he bought a minor league team in Sioux City, Iowa, in a league run by his friend Ban Johnson, and soon moved it to Saint Paul, Minnesota. Then he set his sights on his hometown. In October 1899, James Hart, who ran Chicago's National League club, agreed to let him in on conditions: Comiskey's team had to play south of 35th Street, far from Hart's ballpark on the West Side, and it couldn't use the city's name. Hart is said to have assumed that few fans would put up with the smell of the stockyards. Comiskey borrowed the old name of Hart's own team, the White Stockings, which it no longer used, and the newspapers soon shortened it to White Sox.

Johnson's league renamed itself the American League, declared itself a major league in 1901, and went to war with the National League for players and fans. Comiskey's team won the new league's first pennant.

His greatest triumph came in 1906. That season, the Cubs won a hundred and sixteen games, still the best winning percentage in modern baseball history. The White Sox had a team batting average of two thirty, the worst in their league, and they were nicknamed the Hitless Wonders. They won with pitching and defense, and when the two Chicago teams met in the World Series, the Hitless Wonders beat the Cubs four games to two. It's still the only World Series the two teams have ever played against each other.

Sportswriters called Comiskey the Old Roman. He died in 1931, at his retreat in Eagle River, Wisconsin, and his family kept the team until 1959.
=== DEEPER Veeck as in Wreck | people
The most inventive owner the White Sox ever had grew up in the game. Bill Veeck was born in Chicago in 1914, and his father, a sportswriter, became president of the Cubs, so young Bill spent his boyhood around the ballpark on the North Side. He told the world how to say his name in the title of his autobiography: Veeck, as in wreck.

In the Second World War, he served with the Marines in the South Pacific, and on the island of Bougainville, the recoil of an anti-aircraft gun smashed his leg. It was eventually amputated, and for the rest of his life he wore wooden legs, with holes cut in them to use as ashtrays.

As owner of the Cleveland Indians, he signed Larry Doby in July 1947, making him the first Black player in the American League, eleven weeks after Jackie Robinson's debut with the Brooklyn Dodgers. The next year, he signed the great Negro Leagues pitcher Satchel Paige, then in his forties, and Cleveland won the World Series. Later, with the lowly Saint Louis Browns, he sent a man three feet seven inches tall up to bat. Eddie Gaedel walked on four pitches, and the league quickly voided his contract.

Veeck bought the White Sox in 1959, and that fall they won their first pennant in forty years. The next season, he unveiled the first exploding scoreboard in the major leagues, which set off fireworks and spun pinwheels after every White Sox home run. Illness forced him to sell in 1961.

He came back in 1975, gathering investors to buy the team when it looked likely to move to Seattle. In 1976, he put his players in shorts for a game. In 1978, he hired Larry Doby again, this time as manager, only the second Black manager in major league history. And the anti-disco promotion that ended in a riot in 1979 was cooked up by his son Mike, the team's promotions director, together with a local disc jockey.

Veeck sold the team for good in 1981, and he died in Chicago in 1986. Five years later, he was elected to the Hall of Fame, the owner who never stopped believing that a ball game should be fun.
=== DEEPER The East-West Classic | culture
The East-West game began as a promoter's idea in the depths of the Depression. In 1933, Gus Greenlee, a Pittsburgh nightclub owner who had grown rich in the numbers racket, was reviving the Negro National League, and he wanted a showcase. Some historians give the credit for the idea to two Pittsburgh sportswriters, Roy Sparrow and Bill Nunn. Greenlee joined forces with Robert Cole, the owner of the Chicago American Giants, and they booked Comiskey Park.

The first game, on September tenth, 1933, drew a crowd of perhaps twenty thousand. What set it apart from the major league All-Star Game was who picked the players. The fans did. Black newspapers across the country, above all the Chicago Defender and the Pittsburgh Courier, printed ballots, and readers voted for the starting lineups. For Black America, it was a national election, held every summer.

The game grew into the biggest event in Black sports. Fans came from all over the country, many dressed in their best clothes, and the hotels, clubs, and theaters of Bronzeville filled up for a weekend of parties. In 1936, the East-West game outdrew the major leagues' own All-Star Game. On August first, 1943, a crowd of fifty-one thousand seven hundred and twenty-three packed Comiskey Park, the largest in the game's history.

The players included many of the greatest in baseball history: Satchel Paige, the slugging catcher Josh Gibson, and the outfielder Cool Papa Bell, who was said to be so fast that he could switch off the light and be in bed before the room got dark. Anyone who doubted that Black players belonged in the major leagues only had to come and watch.

After 1947, when the major leagues began signing the best Black players, the Negro Leagues slowly faded, and the East-West game faded with them. But for a generation, the biggest yearly gathering of Black baseball fans in America happened right here, on the South Side.

=== DEEPER Nobody Nobody Sent | politics
In 1948, a University of Chicago law student named Abner Mikva walked into his local Democratic ward office, eager to volunteer for two reform candidates, Adlai Stevenson for governor and Paul Douglas for the Senate. The ward committeeman, Timothy O'Sullivan, looked him over and asked who had sent him. Mikva said nobody had. As Mikva told the story for the rest of his life, the answer came back: We don't want nobody nobody sent. We ain't got no jobs.

Those two sentences sum up how the machine worked. It ran on jobs. The party controlled tens of thousands of positions in city and county government, from street sweepers to building inspectors, and each one went to someone sent by a ward boss. In return, the job holder worked his precinct at every election, or lost the job.

When Daley first won the mayor's office, in April 1955, a North Side alderman and saloonkeeper named Paddy Bauler is said to have danced a jig and declared that Chicago ain't ready for reform. For decades, it seemed he was right.

Mikva found his own way in. He won a seat in the state legislature as an independent Democrat, and later a seat in Congress, and he went on to be a federal appeals judge and White House counsel to President Clinton.

What finally broke the jobs system was a lawsuit. In 1969, a young lawyer named Michael Shakman ran as an independent for a convention to rewrite the state constitution, and he sued, arguing that armies of patronage workers gave the machine's candidates an unfair advantage. The case produced a series of federal court orders known as the Shakman decrees. The first, in 1972, banned firing government workers for political reasons. A second, in 1983, banned political hiring. Enforcing them took decades. In 2006, a top aide to Mayor Richard M. Daley was convicted of rigging city hiring to reward political workers, and the city wasn't released from federal oversight until 2014.
=== DEEPER The Snow That Beat a Mayor | history
When Richard J. Daley died in December 1976, the city council chose a quiet lawyer from his own neighborhood to take his place. Michael Bilandic, the son of Croatian immigrants, had been the eleventh ward's alderman, and he was the machine's man. He won a special election the next year, and in 1979 he seemed certain to win a full term.

His challenger was Jane Byrne, who had served under Daley as the head of the city's consumer sales department. Bilandic had fired her in 1977, after she publicly attacked a taxi fare increase his administration had approved. Few people gave her a chance.

Then came the snow. On January thirteenth and fourteenth, 1979, about twenty inches fell, on top of snow left by earlier storms. Side streets stayed buried for weeks. Cars disappeared under drifts, garbage piled up in the alleys, and the trains of the Chicago Transit Authority broke down or ran far behind. To speed up service, the transit authority had some trains skip stations in Black neighborhoods on the South and West Sides, leaving riders standing on frozen platforms as the trains rolled past.

Byrne made the snow her campaign. Her message, in effect, was that the city that works had stopped working. Jesse Jackson endorsed her, and on February twenty-seventh, 1979, she won the Democratic primary by about seventeen thousand votes, with strong support in the Black wards that had felt abandoned on those platforms. In April, she won the general election with eighty-two percent of the vote, and she became the first woman to serve as mayor of Chicago.

Politicians took note, in Chicago and far beyond. For years afterward, the mayors of snowy cities were said to think of Michael Bilandic whenever a big storm rolled in, and to make very sure the plows reached the side streets.
=== DEEPER The Bungalow Belt | architecture
The Daley house is a Chicago bungalow, and once you learn to recognize the type, you'll see it all over the city. Between about 1910 and 1940, Chicagoans built some eighty thousand of them, and they still make up about a third of the city's single-family houses. Most stand in a great crescent, four to seven miles from downtown, that sweeps around the city's outer neighborhoods. Chicagoans call it the Bungalow Belt.

The Chicago bungalow was shaped by the Chicago lot, which is typically twenty-five feet wide and a hundred and twenty-five feet deep. So the house turns its narrow end to the street. It's usually a story and a half tall, built of brick, with a low roof and deep, overhanging eaves. In front, look for a bay of windows, often with panes of colored art glass. Inside, you'd typically find oak trim, built-in cabinets, and a basement raised a few feet above the ground, so that its windows catch some light.

What made the bungalow remarkable was who could buy one. It was modern, with central heat, indoor plumbing, and electric light, but it was small enough to be affordable, and developers put them up by the dozen, block after block, for working families. Many buyers borrowed from neighborhood savings and loan associations, often run by and for a single ethnic group. For the children of immigrants who had grown up in crowded cottages and flats near the stockyards, a bungalow a few miles farther out was proof that the family had made it.

The Bungalow Belt became a political term, too. Its homeowners, many of them city workers, were long seen as the backbone of the machine's vote, and later as the voters every candidate for mayor had to win. Around 2000, the city began a program to help owners restore their bungalows' original details, and a nonprofit, the Historic Chicago Bungalow Association, still runs it.

=== DEEPER A Peaceful Place | names
The Ramova's name is Lithuanian, and it's a clue to who lived on these streets when the theater opened. The story goes that in the summer of 1929, a naming contest was held through the Lithuanian newspaper Draugas, whose name means friend. The winner was Ramova, which was said to mean a peaceful place. Before that, the new theater had been advertised under a far less poetic name, the Roxee.

Lithuanians began arriving in Chicago in large numbers in the 1880s and 1890s. Their homeland was then part of the Russian Empire, and many were escaping poverty and service in the Russian army. Many found work in the stockyards, and Bridgeport became the first center of Lithuanian Chicago. In 1892, they founded the city's first Lithuanian parish, Saint George, and its big church rose a few blocks northwest of here. Halsted Street filled with Lithuanian taverns, bakeries, shops, and halls, and Chicago came to be called the largest Lithuanian city outside Lithuania.

There's a reason so much Lithuanian was printed here. From 1864 to 1904, the Russian authorities banned books and newspapers in the Lithuanian language printed in its own alphabet, so Lithuanian publishing flourished abroad, and Chicago became one of its capitals.

Walk one block west of Halsted, and you'll find Lituanica Avenue. It used to be Auburn Avenue, and in the 1930s it was renamed for the Lituanica, the plane that two Chicago Lithuanian pilots flew across the Atlantic in 1933. Saint George's church stood on that street. It closed in 1990, and it has since been torn down.

Over the twentieth century, Lithuanian Chicago moved south and west, to the neighborhood around Marquette Park, where a stretch of 69th Street is called Lithuanian Plaza Court, and later to the suburbs. But the name on the marquee here still speaks the old language.
=== DEEPER The Lituanica | history
On July fifteenth, 1933, two Chicago Lithuanians took off from Floyd Bennett Field in Brooklyn, bound for Kaunas, then the capital of Lithuania, without a stop.

Steponas Darius was born in Lithuania in 1896 and came to America with his family as a boy. In the First World War, he fought in France with the American army, and he was gassed and wounded. Afterward, he went back to Lithuania, where he became a military pilot and helped introduce basketball, publishing the country's first rules for the game in 1926. Stasys Girėnas, three years older, had come to America as a young man and served in the American army at home. In Chicago, he started a taxi company. The two men met in 1927, and they soon began to dream of flying nonstop from America to Lithuania.

In 1932, they bought a Bellanca, a sturdy six-seat airplane, and rebuilt it for the long crossing. Lithuanian American groups helped raise the money, and in the spring of 1933, the plane was christened the Lituanica. Before they left, the two men wrote a short testament addressed to Young Lithuania, dedicating the flight to the nation's young.

They crossed the ocean. But in the early hours of July seventeenth, after more than thirty-seven hours in the air and nearly four thousand miles, the Lituanica crashed in a forest near the town of Soldin, then in Germany and now in Poland, about four hundred miles short of Kaunas. Both men were killed.

Investigators concluded that it was an accident, most likely a failed emergency landing in bad weather and fog. But many Lithuanians suspected the new Nazi government. Rumors said searchlights at a camp near Soldin had caught the plane in their beams, and the Germans had moved the bodies before Lithuanian officials arrived. Autopsies found no bullets, and the doubts have never entirely gone away.

In Lithuania, Darius and Girėnas became national heroes. Their faces appeared on the ten litas banknote, Kaunas named its stadium for them, and the wreck of their plane is kept in a museum there. In Chicago, a monument to the two fliers stands in Marquette Park, on the Southwest Side.
=== DEEPER The Ghost of Archer Avenue | legends
About a mile north of here, Archer Avenue slants along the northern edge of Bridgeport. It follows the route of the old Illinois and Michigan Canal, and it's named for William Beatty Archer, a canal commissioner who handed out jobs to the Irish diggers. Follow it southwest for about a dozen miles, and you reach Resurrection Cemetery, in the suburb of Justice. That stretch of road belongs to Chicago's most famous ghost.

As the story is usually told, she was a young woman with light hair and a white dress who went dancing one night at a ballroom out on Archer Avenue. She quarreled with her date, set off walking home, and was struck and killed by a driver who never stopped. She was buried at Resurrection in her white dress.

Since the 1930s, drivers on Archer Avenue have told of picking up a quiet young woman who asks for a ride. She says little, and as the car passes the cemetery gates, she's gone. In one early account, from 1939, a young man named Jerry Palus said a girl he'd met at a dance directed him down Archer Avenue, asked to get out at the cemetery, and vanished. Later drivers reported hitting a young woman in white outside the cemetery, only to find no one there.

The most famous night came in August 1976, when a man called the police in Justice to report a girl locked inside the cemetery after dark. An officer found no one, but two bars of the gate were bent apart, with what looked like the marks of small hands seared into the metal. Crowds came to see them. The cemetery said a truck had damaged the bars.

Who was she? Researchers have proposed several real young women. One was Mary Bregovy, who died in a car crash downtown in 1934. Another was Anna Norkus, a twelve-year-old from a Lithuanian family, killed in 1927 on her way home from the Oh Henry Ballroom on Archer Avenue, though she's buried at Saint Casimir, the Lithuanian cemetery, not Resurrection. None of them fits perfectly. Resurrection Mary belongs to a very old kind of tale, the vanishing hitchhiker, told around the world. But on Archer Avenue, people still keep an eye out for her.
=== STOP The Union Stock Yard Gate
=== DEEPER Christmas Day, 1865 | history
Before 1865, Chicago's livestock trade was scattered among several competing yards along different railroad lines, and moving animals between them meant driving herds through the city streets. The Civil War changed the scale of everything. The Union army needed meat, and during the war, Chicago passed Cincinnati as the nation's biggest pork packer.

So a group of meatpackers and railroad men organized one central market. The state legislature granted a charter, the new company raised a million dollars, and it bought three hundred and twenty acres of swampy prairie south of the city. To lay it out, it hired a railroad engineer named Octave Chanute. He designed a grid of streets and alleys lined with pens, with room for about twenty-one thousand cattle, seventy-five thousand hogs, and twenty-two thousand sheep, and with railroad tracks running right up to the chutes. The Union Stock Yards opened on Christmas Day, 1865.

Chanute went on to build the first bridge across the Missouri River, at Kansas City. In his sixties, he became a pioneer of flight. In 1896, he tested gliders on the sand dunes of Indiana, and he later advised and encouraged the Wright brothers.

The yards grew into a city of their own, with hotels, a bank, and a daily newspaper for the livestock trade. Every morning, buyers rode through the alleys on horseback, sizing up the animals in the pens and bidding for them.

Fire was a constant danger. On May nineteenth, 1934, during a drought and a fierce heat wave, a fire broke out in the hay of a cattle pen, perhaps from a cigarette tossed by a passing driver. It swept across several blocks, destroying pens, buildings, and the great show pavilion, and it did about eight million dollars in damage, the most destructive fire in the city's history after the fire of 1871. The company rebuilt quickly. By the end of that year, a new arena, the International Amphitheatre, stood where the pavilion had burned.
=== DEEPER The Ice Car | companies
In the 1870s, beef traveled east on the hoof. Cattle were crowded into stock cars in Chicago and shipped alive to slaughterhouses in the East. It was cruel and wasteful. Animals lost weight on the way, some died, and the railroads charged as much to haul the parts of a steer nobody would eat as the parts that became steak.

Gustavus Swift saw a better way. He was a Yankee from Cape Cod, born in 1839, who had gone to work for a butcher as a boy, and he came to Chicago in 1875 as a cattle buyer. His idea was to slaughter the cattle here and ship only the dressed meat, kept cold so that it would arrive fresh.

He's said to have started by shipping meat in winter, in ordinary boxcars with the doors left open. In 1878, he hired an engineer named Andrew Chase to design a true refrigerator car. Chase's car was insulated and ventilated, with ice in a compartment near the roof, so that cold air sank naturally over the meat.

The big railroads wanted nothing to do with it. They had invested heavily in stock cars, pens, and feedlots for live cattle, and they feared losing that business. So Swift paid for the cars himself, and he found one line willing to haul them, the Grand Trunk, a Canadian railroad that made little money from live cattle. His cars rolled east through Michigan and Canada. He contracted for ice cut in Wisconsin and built icing stations along the way to refill the cars.

Next, he had to win over the customers. Eastern butchers, whose living depended on slaughtering animals locally, fought the Chicago meat, and many shoppers distrusted beef killed a thousand miles away. Swift teamed up with local dealers, opened his own branch houses, and undersold the competition.

It worked. Within a few years, the other big packers had refrigerator cars of their own, and Chicago beef was on tables across the country. When Swift died in 1903, his company was one of the largest businesses in America, and the refrigerator car was changing the way the whole country ate.
=== DEEPER Armour's Gamble | people
Philip Danforth Armour was born in 1832, on a farm in Stockbridge, in upstate New York. At nineteen, he left home for the California gold rush. He didn't strike it rich in the diggings. Instead, he started a business hiring miners who'd run out of luck to build sluices, the wooden channels that carried water through the gold fields, and by the age of twenty-four, he had saved about eight thousand dollars, a small fortune at the time.

He settled in Milwaukee, went into the grain trade, and then into pork packing, as a partner of John Plankinton. Near the end of the Civil War, he made the bet that made his name. The army's demand had driven pork prices sky high, and Armour was convinced they would collapse once the Union won. So he sold pork short, promising to deliver it months later at the high wartime price. When the war ended and prices fell, as he'd predicted, he made a fortune, while many of his competitors in Cincinnati were ruined.

In 1875, he moved to Chicago, where the family firm already had a packing plant, and he built Armour and Company into one of the biggest businesses in the world. He added beef and lamb to pork, built his own refrigerator cars, and found a use for nearly everything his plants produced. He was famous for his hours. He's said to have been at his desk by seven in the morning, and he expected his managers to keep up.

He was hard on rivals, and he fought hard against unions. The park beside the White Sox ballpark, Armour Square, was named in his honor in 1904.

Armour died of pneumonia at his Chicago home in January 1901. His son, J. Ogden Armour, expanded the company further, but he's said to have lost most of the family fortune in the 1920s. Today, the Armour name lives on mostly as a brand of canned meats on supermarket shelves.

=== DEEPER The Organizer | politics
Saul Alinsky was born in Chicago in 1909, to Russian Jewish immigrant parents. He studied at the University of Chicago, and as a graduate student in criminology, he got to know members of the Capone gang, an unusual education for a future organizer. He went on to work with young offenders and in a state prison.

In the late 1930s, his work brought him here, and he came to believe that what the neighborhood needed most was power. His partners were a local man, Joseph Meegan, who ran Davis Square Park, and Bernard Sheil, an auxiliary bishop of Chicago who had founded the Catholic Youth Organization.

The neighborhood was divided by old hatreds. Polish, Lithuanian, Slovak, Irish, Mexican, and other families had their own parishes, and many barely spoke to one another. Alinsky's idea was a council made not of individuals but of organizations, like parishes, unions, clubs, and businesses. On July fourteenth, 1939, about three hundred and fifty people from seventy-six groups came to the first meeting of the new Back of the Yards Neighborhood Council.

Two days later, the packinghouse workers' union held a mass rally at the Chicago Coliseum, and Bishop Sheil spoke from the same platform as John L. Lewis, the fiery leader of the new industrial unions. For Catholic workers, the sight of a bishop beside the union's leader sent a powerful message. Alinsky later wrote a biography of Lewis.

Alinsky took the council's methods across the country. In 1940, with money from the department store heir Marshall Field the Third, he founded the Industrial Areas Foundation to train organizers. His books, Reveille for Radicals, in 1946, and Rules for Radicals, in 1971, became handbooks for activists. One of his rules was that ridicule is the most potent weapon there is.

He died in 1972, in Carmel, California. People on the left and the right still argue about his ideas, which may be the truest measure of his influence.
=== DEEPER The Small Parks | history
Around 1900, the neighborhoods around the stockyards were among the most crowded in America, and they had almost no open space. Children played in the streets, in the alleys, and beside the railroad tracks. Chicago's great parks, like Washington Park and Jackson Park, were miles away, and few working families had the time or the carfare to get there.

Reformers, including Jane Addams of Hull House, had long argued for playgrounds in the poorest neighborhoods. In 1903, the South Park Commission set out to build a whole system of small parks. It hired the Olmsted Brothers, the firm run by the sons of Frederick Law Olmsted, to design the grounds, and Daniel Burnham's firm to design the buildings.

In 1905, ten of the new parks opened on the South Side. Among them were Davis Square, a mile or so west of here, Sherman Park, named for John B. Sherman, the longtime chief of the stockyards, and Armour Square, beside the White Sox ballpark. They had playgrounds, ball fields, and swimming pools, and something no American park had had before, a field house. Inside were gymnasiums, branch libraries, meeting halls, lunchrooms, and locker rooms with showers, a luxury at a time when many homes nearby had no bathtub. They were the first park field houses in the country.

Theodore Roosevelt called the new parks the most notable civic achievement in any American city, and cities across the country copied them.

Davis Square was named for Nathan Smith Davis, a Chicago doctor who helped found the American Medical Association, and it became the heart of Back of the Yards. In the 1930s, its director, Joseph Meegan, was one of the founders of the neighborhood council that made this corner of Chicago famous among organizers.

Many of the original field houses still stand, and they're still busy. They set the pattern for the park buildings that anchor neighborhoods all over Chicago today.
=== DEEPER The Sirloin Room | food
At 42nd and Halsted, a few blocks northwest of here, stood a building that once fed the whole livestock world, the Stock Yard Inn. It went up in 1912, right after fire destroyed the hotel that had stood on the site, and it was built to look like an old English inn, with half-timbered walls and steep gables.

The inn had rooms for the cattlemen, buyers, and salesmen who came to the yards, and several dining rooms. The most famous was the Sirloin Room. There, as diners remembered it, you walked up to a case of raw steaks, chose the one you wanted, and watched as it was branded with a red-hot iron, so you'd know the steak on your plate was the one you picked. The beef came from the yards across the street, and for decades a steak dinner here was part of any business trip to the stockyards.

The building was also home to the Saddle and Sirloin Club, the private club of the livestock business, whose name is said to come from the sirloin steaks and saddles of lamb its members dined on. Starting in 1903, the club collected oil portraits of the leading figures of the livestock world, breeders, packers, and stockmen. Most of the early portraits were lost in the great stockyards fire of 1934, but the club kept collecting, and after the yards closed, the collection moved to Louisville, Kentucky, home of a big national livestock show.

As the packers left Chicago, so did the inn's customers. The Stock Yard Inn closed in 1976 and was torn down the next year.

But Chicago's reputation for steak outlived it. The city's great steakhouses, with their aged beef and dark wood, still trade on the idea that Chicago is the place to eat a steak, an idea that grew up right here, beside the pens.
