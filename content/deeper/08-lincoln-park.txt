=== DEEPER The Proclamation That Burned | history
In the fall of 1863, in the middle of the Civil War, two Chicago women, Mary Livermore and Jane Hoge, organized a great fair to raise money for the Sanitary Commission, the volunteer organization that sent food, medicine and nurses to Union soldiers. It was the first of the big sanitary fairs that spread across the North, and people donated all kinds of things to be sold.

President Lincoln sent the most valuable gift of all: his own handwritten final draft of the Emancipation Proclamation. Thomas Bryan, a Chicago lawyer and the president of the fair, bought it for three thousand dollars and gave it to the Soldiers' Home, a refuge in Chicago for disabled veterans. Printed facsimiles of the document were sold to raise still more money.

The draft ended up in the care of the Chicago Historical Society, which displayed it in a big frame on the wall of its reception room. The Society's building, near Dearborn and Ontario Streets, was supposed to be safe from fire. It wasn't. When the Great Fire swept across the North Side in October 1871, the building burned, and almost the whole collection was lost: books, letters, maps, the records of Chicago's early years, and Lincoln's proclamation with them.

The Society started over. In the 1890s it built a fortress of rough granite on the same corner, and in 1932 it moved here, to the edge of the park, into the building that's now the museum.

The official copy in Washington, written out by a clerk, signed and sealed, survives. Lincoln's draft of the earlier, preliminary proclamation, which he gave to a similar fair in Albany, survives in the New York State Library. But the final draft in his own hand, the one he gave to Chicago, is gone.
=== DEEPER The Sculptor | people
Augustus Saint-Gaudens was born in Dublin in 1848, to a French shoemaker and an Irish mother, and he came to New York as a baby. At thirteen he was apprenticed to a cameo cutter, carving tiny portraits in shell and stone, and at night he studied drawing. In his twenties he trained in Paris and worked in Rome.

His big break was a statue of Admiral David Farragut, unveiled in Madison Square in New York in 1881, on a base designed by his friend Stanford White. It was hailed as a new kind of American monument, a hero who looked like a real man, braced as if on a rolling deck.

The Standing Lincoln, six years later, made him the leading sculptor in the country. He went on to create the Shaw Memorial in Boston, honoring the Black soldiers of the Fifty-fourth Massachusetts Regiment and their colonel, Robert Gould Shaw, a relief he labored over for some fourteen years. Then came the gilded General Sherman, riding behind a winged Victory at the corner of Central Park. And for his friend Theodore Roosevelt, he designed a twenty-dollar gold coin, first struck in 1907, which many collectors consider the most beautiful American coin ever made.

Other artists gathered around him at his summer home in Cornish, New Hampshire, where an art colony grew up. The house and studios are now a national park.

He died in 1907, and Chicago has a second Lincoln by him that he never saw in place. It's a seated figure in Grant Park, officially called Abraham Lincoln, the Head of State. It was cast in 1908, shown in New York and at the San Francisco world's fair of 1915, and finally unveiled in Grant Park in 1926, after years of arguments over where it should go. Stanford White designed its setting too, though he didn't live to see it either. If you're in Grant Park, look for the family resemblance between the two Lincolns.
=== DEEPER The Last Survivor of the Tea Party | legends
A short walk north of the museum, near Clark Street, a big granite boulder sits in the grass with a bronze plaque. It marks the grave, somewhere nearby, of a man named David Kennison. The plaque says he was the last survivor of the Boston Tea Party, and that he died in Chicago in 1852, at the age of a hundred and fifteen.

Kennison was a celebrity in his last years. He told how, as a young man, he had helped dump British tea into Boston Harbor in 1773, and then fought in the Revolution, and he spoke of service in later wars too. Chicagoans loved him. In a raw young city with almost no history of its own, here was a living link to the founding of the country, and when he died, by most accounts, the city turned out to honor him. He was buried in the City Cemetery, which then covered this ground.

In 1903, half a century later, patriotic groups placed this boulder in his memory, and for most of the next century his story was repeated in guidebooks and newspapers.

The trouble is that it doesn't hold up. As early as 1914, a speaker in Oak Park was publicly doubting it. Historians who have since gone through the records believe Kennison was born around 1764. That would have made him about nine years old at the Tea Party, and about eighty-five, not a hundred and fifteen, when he died. The Encyclopedia of Chicago now says plainly that he falsely claimed to be the Tea Party's last survivor.

So what is the boulder really? It's a monument to a story that Chicago wanted to believe, and to the cemetery that still lies under the park. Nobody knows exactly where in these lawns Kennison's body is. The plaque only says that he lies near this spot, and that part, at least, is probably true.
=== DEEPER Couch's Folly | companies
The man in the tomb, Ira Couch, made his fortune in hotels. In the 1830s he and his brother James took over a hotel at the corner of Lake and Dearborn Streets, named the Tremont House, and they rebuilt it more than once as the town grew. The grandest version, designed by John Van Osdel, Chicago's first professional architect, was a five-story brick hotel with two hundred and sixty rooms, so big for a muddy prairie town that skeptics called it Couch's Folly.

It turned out to be no folly at all. The Tremont became the place to stay in Chicago, and the place to be seen. Politicians made it their headquarters, and Abraham Lincoln stayed there on his visits to the city.

Its most famous moment came in July 1858. Senator Stephen Douglas opened his campaign for reelection with a speech from the hotel's balcony to a huge crowd in the street, with his Republican challenger, Lincoln, in the audience. The next evening, Lincoln answered him from the same balcony. Those two speeches were the curtain-raiser for the Lincoln-Douglas debates that summer and fall, which made Lincoln a national figure and set him on the road to the White House.

Ira Couch didn't live to see any of it. He died in 1857, and his family built this tomb the next year. The hotel he built burned in the fire of 1871. His estate put up yet another Tremont House on the same corner, also designed by Van Osdel, and it stood until 1937.

So the heavy stone box in the grass is the last trace of two lost Chicago landmarks: the cemetery that became this park, and the hotel where Lincoln began the campaign that made him president.

=== DEEPER Dr. Fisher's Apes | history
For three decades, one man shaped this zoo more than anyone else. Lester Fisher came here in 1947 as its veterinarian, became director in 1962, and ran the zoo until he retired in 1992.

Fisher's passion was the great apes, and under him Lincoln Park became famous for them. The zoo built up the largest gorilla population in North America, and in 1976 it opened a great ape house, later named for him, where visitors could watch whole families of gorillas and chimpanzees. By 2004 the zoo had celebrated forty-five gorilla births since 1970, at a time when many zoos had trouble getting apes to breed at all.

That success depended on a change in thinking. The old zoos had kept animals alone, as specimens to be looked at. Fisher and his staff learned that gorillas, like people, need family life: a silverback leader, several females, young ones to learn from their elders. Animals raised that way grew up knowing how to be parents themselves.

In 2004 the zoo replaced the Fisher building with the Regenstein Center for African Apes, which cost some twenty-six million dollars, the most expensive project in the zoo's history at the time. It gives western lowland gorillas and chimpanzees large indoor and outdoor spaces, with climbing structures, grass and room to get away from the crowds and from each other.

The zoo's scientists study how apes think and behave, and they help plan breeding for endangered species at zoos across the continent, so that small populations scattered in many cities can stay healthy over generations.

So when you look into the eyes of a gorilla here, you're seeing the latest in a long line of Lincoln Park apes, stretching back through Dr. Fisher's families to the most famous one of all, Bushman.
=== DEEPER Bushman's Afternoon Out | legends
Bushman was the zoo's biggest star for twenty years, and in the fall of 1950, near the end of his life, he gave Chicago one more story.

One day that autumn, Bushman found his way out of his cage and roamed the zoo's primate house. A full-grown gorilla of more than five hundred pounds was loose in a public building, and the news made the front page of the Tribune. Keepers cleared the area and tried to coax him back. One of them, a keeper named Eddie Robinson, tried to lead him toward his cage and was bitten, badly enough to need four stitches at Columbus Hospital.

Then came the part of the story that everybody remembers. The zoo's director at the time, Marlin Perkins, knew something about Bushman: the great gorilla was terrified of snakes. So, the story goes, a keeper brought out a small, harmless garter snake. Bushman took one look, turned around, and hurried back into his cage.

Accounts of the afternoon differ. A newspaper on the other side of the world reported that keepers used other animals from the zoo's exhibits to drive him back. But the version that stuck, the one Chicago kids heard for decades, is the one with the garter snake: a five-hundred-pound giant sent home by a creature you could hold in one hand.

Bushman was already old for a captive gorilla, and his health was failing. Crowds came to see him in his final months, and when he died, the city mourned him like a celebrity. The snake became part of his legend, a reminder that even a king has something he's afraid of.
=== DEEPER The Nature Boardwalk | nature
At the south end of the zoo, look for a pond ringed with tall grasses, a boardwalk around its edge, and, on one bank, a curving pavilion of pale wood. This is the Nature Boardwalk, which opened in 2010.

It used to be the zoo's South Pond, a shallow, hard-edged artificial pool filled with city tap water, and murky for much of the summer. The zoo and the Chicago architect Jeanne Gang's firm, Studio Gang, rebuilt it as a natural wetland. They deepened the pond so the water would hold more oxygen, softened the shoreline, and planted native prairie and marsh plants, so the water could clean itself and support life. Today it holds fish, turtles and frogs, along with dragonflies and the birds that hunt them.

The pond matters more than its size suggests. Chicago sits on one of the great migration routes of North America, and every spring and fall, millions of songbirds follow the lakeshore. To a tired warbler flying over miles of concrete, the green strip of Lincoln Park is a lifesaver, and a pond full of insects and cover is a place to rest and refuel. Bird watchers come here with binoculars at dawn in May.

The pavilion is made of prefabricated wooden planks, bent into arcs and joined end to end. Its ribbed, rounded shape was inspired by the shell of a tortoise, and there are no columns inside. It's officially the Peoples Gas Education Pavilion, and it has become one of the most photographed spots in the park, especially at sunset, with the skyline behind it.

Walk the boardwalk slowly, and look down. On a warm day you'll often see turtles lined up on a log, sunning themselves within sight of the Gold Coast.
=== STOP Conservatory & the Lily Pool
=== DEEPER Alfred Caldwell | people
The man behind the Lily Pool lived a long and stubborn life. Alfred Caldwell was born in St. Louis in 1903 and came to Chicago young, where he went to work for Jens Jensen and absorbed his belief that a landscape should feel like the land itself: prairie, woods, stone and water.

When the Depression hit, Caldwell became superintendent of parks in Dubuque, Iowa, and there he built Eagle Point Park, with low stone shelters stepping along the bluffs high above the Mississippi. It was a New Deal project, and in 1936 it won a national award for design. During the presidential campaign that year, Franklin and Eleanor Roosevelt came to see it.

Back in Chicago he made the Lily Pool, and then Promontory Point on the South Side lakefront, which you'll hear about on the Hyde Park tour.

Then came a surprising turn. Caldwell joined Mies van der Rohe at the Illinois Institute of Technology, where he taught for many years and shaped the landscape of Mies's campus. The prairie romantic and the master of steel and glass made an unlikely pair, but they understood each other: both believed in order, simplicity and nature. When the school dismissed Mies as its campus architect in 1960, Caldwell resigned in protest.

He also designed the landscape for Lafayette Park in Detroit, a housing development that Mies designed with the Chicago developer Herbert Greenwald. Its towers and townhouses stand in a gentle park of trees and lawns, and in 2015 it was named a National Historic Landmark.

Caldwell was a poet, too, and a fierce teacher who could talk for hours about architecture, democracy and nature. He died in 1998, at ninety-five. Sit for a moment by the waterfall in the Lily Pool, and you're sitting inside the idea he spent his life defending.
=== DEEPER Kennicott's Alaska | history
The Chicago Academy of Sciences, which runs the nature museum just north of here, had a remarkable young founder named Robert Kennicott. He grew up on his family's farm, called The Grove, in what's now Glenview, a sickly boy who was taught mostly at home and spent his days studying snakes, birds and small animals. In his early twenties he helped found the Academy.

In 1859 he headed north for the Smithsonian Institution, and for about three years he traveled by canoe and dog sled through the wilderness of the Hudson's Bay Company and along the Yukon, collecting specimens and making friends with fur traders and native peoples.

In 1865 the Western Union company hired him to lead the scientists on one of the most ambitious projects of the age: a telegraph line from America to Europe the long way around, through British Columbia, Russian America and Siberia. Kennicott took command of the expedition's scientific corps. But in May 1866, at about thirty, he died near the trading post of Nulato on the Yukon River. His friend William Healey Dall took over his work.

The telegraph line was never finished. A cable was laid across the Atlantic in 1866, and the company gave up the following year. But the timing of the expedition was extraordinary. In 1867 the United States bought Russian America from Russia, and what Kennicott and his colleagues had learned about its rivers, wildlife and people helped Americans understand what they had bought. Today we call it Alaska.

His name is on the map there: the Kennicott Glacier and the Kennicott River. A copper town nearby took the name, misspelled as Kennecott, and so did the giant copper company that grew from its mines. Back in Chicago, the Academy's collections, including much of his work, burned in the fire of 1871.
=== DEEPER The Palm House | look
Step inside the conservatory. It's free, and on a winter day, coming in from the cold into the warm, wet air is one of the great small pleasures of Chicago.

The glass house was built in stages between 1890 and 1895, replacing a small greenhouse from the 1870s. Start in the Palm House, which opened in 1892, and look for the giant fiddle-leaf rubber tree, with its big, leathery, violin-shaped leaves. It was planted in 1891, while the room was still being built, and it now rises about fifty feet, nearly to the top of the glass.

The Fern Room, which opened in 1895, is the most magical. A still pool sits among ferns, mosses and low, spreading plants, and the whole room feels like a forest from the age of the dinosaurs. Look for the sago palm, which isn't a palm at all. It's a cycad, from a family of plants far older than flowers, which grew alongside the dinosaurs.

In the Orchid House, look for the hundreds of orchids, along with bromeliads, the relatives of the pineapple that store water in the cups of their leaves, and a few carnivorous plants that trap insects for food. And the show house changes with the seasons, from a spring flower show to a holiday display in winter.

As you walk through, look up at the structure too: the slender iron ribs and thousands of panes of glass, curving overhead. Victorian conservatories like this one were a kind of time machine and travel agency in one, a way for city people to see the plants of the tropics without leaving home.

When you step back outside, notice the formal garden, laid out with bright beds of flowers, a deliberate contrast to the wild, natural look of the Lily Pool next door.

=== DEEPER The Gun Detectives | history
The massacre left investigators with an awkward question. Two of the killers had worn police uniforms. Were they real police? In 1929, with Chicago's police so entangled with the gangs, nobody could rule it out.

To find out, investigators turned to Calvin Goddard, a doctor and former army officer who had made himself an expert in a new science, forensic ballistics: identifying which gun fired a bullet from the tiny scratches its barrel leaves behind. Goddard examined the bullets and shell casings from the garage and showed that they had come from two Thompson submachine guns, and that they didn't match the guns carried by Chicago police.

Then the case broke open in another state. On December fourteenth, 1929, a police officer named Charles Skelly was shot dead on a street in St. Joseph, Michigan. Deputies searched a house near town rented by the gunman, who turned out to be Fred Burke, a killer for hire with ties to Capone's outfit. They found an arsenal, including two Thompson guns. Goddard test-fired them and matched them to the bullets from the garage on Clark Street.

Burke was eventually caught, but he was never tried for the massacre. Michigan convicted him of killing Skelly, and he died in a Michigan prison. The two guns are still kept by the sheriff's office in Berrien County, Michigan.

The case had a lasting result. In its wake, Northwestern University opened the Scientific Crime Detection Laboratory, with Goddard as its director, often described as the first independent crime laboratory in the United States. Its work in ballistics, fingerprints and lie detection helped turn detective work into a science. Every television crime show with a lab in it owes something to the morning of February fourteenth, 1929.
=== DEEPER Bugs Moran | people
George Moran wasn't really named George Moran. He was born Adelard Cunin in St. Paul, Minnesota, in 1893, ran away from home as a teenager, and took a new name. In Chicago he joined the North Side gang of Dean O'Banion and Hymie Weiss, and after both of them were killed, he ran what was left of it. His nickname, Bugs, was slang for crazy, and people said it fit his temper.

After the massacre, reporters asked him who had done it. Only Capone kills like that, he's said to have answered. Capone, in Florida, supposedly shot back that the only man who kills like that is Bugs Moran.

Moran survived the massacre, but his gang didn't. He had lost his best gunmen in a single morning, and over the next few years Capone's organization took over most of the North Side rackets. Moran stayed in Chicago for a while, then drifted out of the big time into smaller schemes.

He never went straight. In 1946 he was arrested for his part in a bank robbery in the small town of Ansonia, Ohio, and he spent the next ten years in the Ohio Penitentiary. When he was released in 1956, he was arrested again, for an earlier bank robbery, and sentenced to ten more years.

He didn't serve them. He died of lung cancer at the federal penitentiary in Leavenworth, Kansas, on February twenty-fifth, 1957, and he was buried in the paupers' section of the prison cemetery.

So the man who missed his own massacre because he was running late outlived nearly all of his enemies, including Capone, who died in 1947. But the last boss of the North Side gang ended up broke, forgotten and buried in a prison yard, a long way from Clark Street.
=== DEEPER Seven Men in a Garage | legends
Who were the seven men? Frank Gusenberg and his brother Peter were among the gang's toughest gunmen. Albert Kachellek, who went by the name James Clark, was another. Adam Heyer looked after the gang's business affairs. Albert Weinshank managed some of the businesses it used as fronts. John May was an auto mechanic who did work for the gang, and by many accounts his German shepherd was tied up in the garage that morning and was found howling among the bodies. And Reinhardt Schwimmer was a former optician and gambler who liked to hang around gangsters for the excitement. That morning, it cost him his life.

Why did the killers move before Moran arrived? One theory points to Weinshank. He was about Moran's size and build, and he dressed much like him. If lookouts across the street were watching for the boss, they may have seen Weinshank go in and given the signal.

And who were the killers? Nobody was ever convicted, and the theories have multiplied for almost a century. Most historians blame Capone's organization, with outside gunmen brought in so that nobody in Chicago would recognize them, and with Fred Burke, whose guns matched the bullets, among them. Capone's own gunman Jack McGurn was charged, but he had an alibi: he said he had spent the morning in a hotel with his girlfriend, Louise Rolfe, whom the newspapers promptly named the Blonde Alibi. The charges against him went nowhere. Others have pointed at Detroit's Purple Gang, or at real policemen settling a score.

Capone himself was at his estate on Palm Island, near Miami, with a perfect alibi, which some people thought was the most suspicious thing of all.

=== DEEPER The Window Dresser | companies
Before Oz made him famous, Baum's big idea was the store window. In the 1890s, the great department stores were learning to sell with spectacle, and the window on the street was their stage. Baum saw that the men who dressed those windows had no magazine of their own, so he started one.

The first issue of The Show Window appeared on November first, 1897. It was a monthly of about sixty pages, full of photographs and advice, and Baum thought of it as an art journal. He wrote about how to use electric lights, mirrors, revolving stands and moving figures to stop people in their tracks. In 1898 he founded the National Association of Window Trimmers, which quickly grew to a couple of hundred members, and in 1900 he gathered his advice into a handbook, The Art of Decorating Dry Goods Windows and Interiors. That October, with Oz on its way, he gave up the editorship.

Now think about the book he was writing at the same time. In the original story, the Emerald City isn't really green at all. Everyone who enters has to wear green-tinted spectacles, locked on by the Guardian of the Gates, and the spectacles make everything look green. And the great Wizard turns out to be an ordinary man from Omaha, working his illusions from behind a screen.

A city whose dazzle comes from the way you look at it, run by a showman who hides the machinery: you don't have to be a literary critic to see a window dresser's imagination at work. Baum never spelled out the connection, and no one can say for sure how much his day job shaped Oz. But the next time you stop in front of a holiday window downtown, you might think of him.
=== DEEPER Denslow | people
The pictures that made Oz came from William Wallace Denslow, a newspaper artist and poster designer who had worked his way through print shops and newsrooms around the country. He and Baum first teamed up on a book of nonsense verse, Father Goose, His Book, which was a best seller in 1899.

For The Wonderful Wizard of Oz, Denslow did far more than illustrate. He shared the book's copyright with Baum, and his drawings set the look of Oz for a generation: the Scarecrow's floppy, painted face, the Tin Woodman's funnel hat, the Lion's shaggy mane and Dorothy herself, a sturdy little girl in a checked dress. The book was printed with color on nearly every page, an expensive gamble at the time. Look closely at his work and you'll often find his signature, a little seahorse.

Success broke up the partnership. After the stage version of Oz became a hit in 1902, the two men quarreled, partly over money, and they never worked together again. Baum went on writing Oz books with a new illustrator, John R. Neill.

Denslow took his share and bought a small island in Bermuda in 1904. He then proclaimed himself its king, under the title King Denslow the First, Monarch of Denslow Islands and Protector of Coral Reefs. It's a wonderful title, but the reign didn't last. He lost money on later stage ventures, drank heavily, sold the island, and died in obscurity in 1915.

His Oz is still the one many readers picture first, more than a century after he drew it. Not bad for a newspaper artist who once crowned himself king of a rock in the Atlantic.
=== DEEPER Oz on Stage | culture
Before Hollywood, there was a Chicago musical. The Wizard of Oz opened at the Grand Opera House on Clark Street in June 1902, with a script by Baum himself, and it was turned into a lavish spectacle by the director Julian Mitchell, with chorus girls, comic songs and elaborate stage effects. It had more vaudeville than Kansas.

A lot of the story changed. Dorothy's little dog Toto was replaced by a cow named Imogene. New characters and songs were added, and the plot wandered wherever the jokes took it. Baum's young readers may have been puzzled, but audiences loved it.

The stars were a vaudeville team, David Montgomery as the Tin Woodman and Fred Stone as the Scarecrow. Stone's loose-limbed, rubbery dancing, as if his legs really were stuffed with straw, stole the show and made him a star. The production moved to Broadway in January 1903, toured the country for years, and made a great deal of money, which is part of why Baum and Denslow fell out.

Almost four decades later came the movie. Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer's Wizard of Oz of 1939 kept Dorothy's dog and dropped the cow. It added the song Over the Rainbow, cast Judy Garland as Dorothy, and turned Kansas into sepia and Oz into blazing color. It made one change that every fan knows: in the book, Dorothy's magic shoes are silver. The studio made them ruby red, to show off its new color film.

One pair of those ruby slippers was stolen from a museum in Minnesota in 2005 and recovered by federal agents in 2018, which shows that people still want to go to Oz, whatever it takes. Baum didn't live to see the film. But the playground here, with its Emerald Garden, is a fair tribute to the show he first put on in Chicago.

=== DEEPER Dillinger's Last Year | history
John Dillinger spent almost nine years in prison in Indiana for a botched holdup of a grocer in the farm town of Mooresville. When he was paroled in 1933, he formed a gang and began robbing banks across the Midwest with a speed and style that made him a folk hero to some Depression-era Americans, who had little love for banks.

In January 1934 he was captured in Tucson, Arizona, and sent to the jail in Crown Point, Indiana, which was said to be escape-proof. On March third, 1934, he walked out, using a wooden pistol that he claimed to have whittled himself, and drove off in the sheriff's own car. He drove it across the state line into Illinois, and that was a mistake. Taking a stolen car across a state line was a federal crime, and it brought the federal Bureau of Investigation into the hunt.

In April, federal agents surrounded a lodge in the north woods of Wisconsin called Little Bohemia, where the gang was hiding. The raid was a fiasco. Agents opened fire on a car of innocent men leaving the lodge, killing one of them, while Dillinger and his men slipped out the back. Baby Face Nelson, one of the gang, killed an agent nearby.

Back in Chicago, Dillinger had a plastic surgeon work on his face and tried to burn off his fingerprints with acid. He grew a mustache, dyed his hair, called himself Jimmy Lawrence, and started dating a waitress named Polly Hamilton. On June twenty-second, his thirty-first birthday, the government declared him Public Enemy Number One.

He seemed to think he could hide in plain sight. He went out on the town, and on a hot Sunday night in July, he went to the movies here, with Polly Hamilton on one arm and Anna Sage on the other. Exactly a month after his birthday, he was dead in the alley next door.
=== DEEPER Melvin Purvis | people
The agent who lit the cigar was Melvin Purvis, a small, dapper lawyer from South Carolina who had joined the Bureau in 1927 and was running its Chicago office by his late twenties. He was one of J. Edgar Hoover's favorites, until he became too famous.

The Dillinger hunt nearly ruined him. After the disaster at Little Bohemia, there were calls for his firing, and Hoover sent another agent, Samuel Cowley, to take charge of the case. But when the tip came from Anna Sage, it was Purvis who led the stakeout here, standing near the box office in the heat for nearly two hours while the movie played.

After the shooting, Purvis became a national hero. Three months later, in October 1934, agents under his command killed another public enemy, Pretty Boy Floyd, on a farm in Ohio. His face was in every newspaper, and that was the problem. Hoover wanted the credit to go to the Bureau, and to himself. Purvis found himself assigned to dull cases and watched closely, and in July 1935 he resigned.

He didn't fade away. He made a deal with the company that made Post Toasties cereal, and kids could send in box tops to join the Junior G-Men, with badges and secret codes, under his name. He served in the army in the Second World War and later went home to South Carolina.

In 1960, Purvis died of a gunshot wound from his own pistol, at home. It was ruled a suicide, though some in his family believed it was an accident. Hoover, who had long since written him out of the Bureau's official version of the Dillinger story, is said to have sent no condolences. He was fifty-six, and the night of the cigar outside the Biograph had followed him all his life.
=== DEEPER Was It Really Dillinger? | legends
Almost as soon as the shots were fired, people began to say the dead man wasn't Dillinger.

The doubters had their reasons, or thought they did. The face on the morgue slab had been altered by surgery. Some said the dead man looked shorter and heavier than Dillinger, and that details in the autopsy didn't match. In 1970, a writer published a book arguing that the Bureau had shot a small-time crook who resembled Dillinger, and covered up its mistake. The idea has never quite gone away.

The government says, and most historians agree, that the man in the alley was Dillinger. His fingerprints, damaged by the acid but not destroyed, were matched at the morgue, and his family identified him. He was buried in Crown Hill Cemetery in Indianapolis, and his father had the grave reinforced with concrete and iron, to keep souvenir hunters from digging him up.

In 2019, relatives of his got a state permit to exhume the body for a television documentary, saying they wanted to settle the question. The cemetery objected, and so did other members of the family. The television network dropped out of the project, and in December 2019 a judge ruled that under Indiana law the cemetery's consent was required. The grave stayed closed, and the cemetery's lawyers called the whole thing a decades-old conspiracy theory.

The alley has its own legends. Ghost-tour guides tell of people who have felt a sudden chill there on summer nights, or glimpsed a figure running toward the street and falling. Those are stories, of course. But stand in the alley for a moment, and you can see why a city that loves its gangsters likes to believe John Dillinger never quite left.
=== DEEPER DePaul's Big Man | culture
DePaul University, just south of here, has a famous basketball story. In 1942, a tall, awkward young man from Joliet named George Mikan enrolled. He stood about six feet ten, wore thick glasses, and was so clumsy that another school had reportedly told him he'd never make a basketball player.

DePaul had a new coach that year, Ray Meyer, who was still in his twenties, and he saw something in Mikan. He put him through drills of every kind: jumping rope, shadowboxing and footwork, and endless hook shots close to the basket, first with one hand, then the other, over and over. Coaches all over the world still use that exercise, and they still call it the Mikan drill.

It worked. Mikan became the most dominant player in the country. He was so good at swatting shots away just above the rim that college basketball banned goaltending, a rule that's often credited, at least in part, to him. In 1945 he led DePaul to the championship of the National Invitation Tournament in New York, then one of the most important tournaments in the sport, and in one game there he scored fifty-three points, as many as the whole other team.

As a professional, Mikan led the Minneapolis Lakers to five championships, and in 1950 the Associated Press named him the greatest basketball player of the first half of the century. The big man, as a position, began with him.

Ray Meyer stayed at DePaul for forty-two seasons and won more than seven hundred games. Today the university's teams play in a big arena near McCormick Place, but the old gym culture of Lincoln Park, and the drill, are where it started. If you see kids practicing hook shots on a neighborhood court, you're watching George Mikan's lessons, eighty years on.
