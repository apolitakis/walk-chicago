=== STOP The River Mouth & the Chicago Lock
=== DEEPER Raising Chicago | history
You heard that the sewage problem never fully went away. It started with the ground itself. Chicago was built on a flat, soggy plain only a few feet above the level of the lake, and in its early years its streets turned to deep mud every spring. People liked to tell of signs planted in the worst spots, reading No Bottom Here, or The Shortest Road to China, though the stories have surely grown. Worse, there was nowhere for sewers to drain. A pipe buried in that ground would have sat below the river it was supposed to empty into.

In 1855, the city hired an engineer from Boston, Ellis Chesbrough, to design one of the first comprehensive sewer systems in the country. His answer was audacious. If the sewers couldn't go down, the city would come up. Pipes were laid along the middle of the old streets and buried under fill, raising the streets by several feet, and in places by much more.

That left every building in town with its front door below the new street. Some owners simply let their ground floors become basements. Others raised their buildings, with crews of men turning thousands of jackscrews in unison, a fraction of an inch at a time. In 1860, a team that included a young George Pullman lifted half a block of brick stores on Lake Street while business went on inside. The next year, the Tremont House, the city's grandest hotel, was jacked up with its guests still in it. One of them is said to have noticed only that the front steps seemed to get steeper every day.

The new sewers worked, but they emptied into the river, and the river emptied into the lake, where the city got its drinking water. In 1871, Chicago made its first attempt to turn the river around, by deepening the old Illinois and Michigan Canal so that it would draw water out of the river and send it southwest. For a while it worked. In wet seasons it didn't, and the search for a lasting answer led to the great canal of 1900.
=== DEEPER The Storm That Wasn't | legends
Ask how Chicago came to reverse its river, and someone may tell you a terrible story. In 1885, it goes, a great storm washed the city's sewage far out into the lake, past the water intakes, and cholera and typhoid killed ninety thousand Chicagoans, about one person in eight. And so the city turned its river around. You'll find the story in books, on websites, and on tours. It never happened.

The historian Libby Hill, while writing her history of the river, went looking for that epidemic and couldn't find it. There was a heavy storm in August 1885, and it did foul the water supply. But there's no mass death in the city's mortality records, and no report of one in the newspapers of the day. Typhoid deaths in Chicago never passed a thousand in any year of the 1880s, and the city hadn't had a cholera outbreak since the 1860s. In 2005, the Chicago Tribune, which had repeated the number, printed a correction.

So where did ninety thousand come from? The trail seems to lead back to the Sanitary District itself. In the 1950s and 1960s, while it campaigned for flood control and then for the giant project called the Deep Tunnel, its publicity told the tale of the deadly storm of 1885, and the numbers grew with each retelling.

The real history was grim enough without it. Cholera swept through Chicago in 1849 and again in 1854, when it killed some fourteen hundred people in a city of a few tens of thousands. Typhoid stayed a steady killer for decades. In 1891, by most counts, it killed close to two thousand Chicagoans, and it didn't retreat until the river was turned around and the water was finally treated. The river was reversed because of all of that, decades of sickness in a city growing faster than its pipes, not because of one storm. But a good story is hard to kill, and this one is still going.
=== DEEPER The Carp at the Door | nature
You heard that turning the river around made enemies downstream. The deeper consequence took a century to show. The canal of 1900 joined two of the great watersheds of North America, the Great Lakes and the Mississippi, which had been separated since the end of the last ice age, apart from the spring floods across the old portage. And anything that can swim or float can use that connection.

Zebra mussels, which reached the Great Lakes in the ballast water of ships in the 1980s, slipped through the canal into the Illinois River by the early 1990s and spread down the Mississippi system, clogging water pipes and smothering native mussels. But the traffic people fear most now runs the other way.

In the 1970s, fish farmers in the South imported several kinds of Asian carp to keep their ponds clean. Floods let them escape, and they spread up the Mississippi and the Illinois, eating the plankton that native fish depend on and crowding them out. Silver carp leap high out of the water when a boat motor startles them, and they've injured plenty of boaters. If they establish themselves in Lake Michigan, scientists fear they could upend fisheries worth billions of dollars.

So since 2002, the Army Corps of Engineers has run electric barriers in the Sanitary and Ship Canal near Romeoville, about thirty miles southwest of here, pulsing electricity through the water to turn fish back. They're the largest electric barriers across a navigable waterway in the world, and they aren't foolproof: in 2017, a live silver carp was caught above them, just miles from the lake. Michigan and other states have gone to court to try to force Chicago's locks closed, and lost. A new line of defense, with underwater noise, bubble curtains and lights, is planned at the Brandon Road Lock near Joliet.

And in 2022, Illinois gave the invaders a new name, copi, from the word copious, hoping that people who would never eat a carp might order a plate of copi, and help fish them out of the rivers.
=== DEEPER The Deep Tunnel | underfoot
You heard that in the heaviest storms, the lock gates may be thrown open so that floodwater can escape into the lake. Here's why. Like many old cities, Chicago carries rainwater and sewage in the same pipes. On an ordinary day, it all flows to the treatment plants. But in a big storm, the pipes and plants are overwhelmed, and the mixture overflows into the rivers, and in the worst storms, into the lake itself. For generations, it also backed up into thousands of basements.

The answer, begun in 1975, is one of the largest public works projects in the world: the Tunnel and Reservoir Plan, better known as the Deep Tunnel. Under the city and suburbs run about a hundred and nine miles of tunnels, bored through solid rock two hundred to three hundred and fifty feet down, some of them more than thirty feet across. When a storm hits, the overflow drops down shafts into the tunnels and is held there until the treatment plants can clean it. The tunnels alone can hold about two point three billion gallons.

That still wasn't enough, so the plan's second stage turns old stone quarries into reservoirs. The Thornton reservoir in the southern suburbs opened in 2015. The McCook reservoir, west of the city, opened its first stage a few years later, and when it's finished, it will hold about ten billion gallons. Together, the system is meant to catch about eighty-five percent of the pollution that used to overflow into the region's waterways.

It has been under construction for half a century, and it still isn't finished. In the very worst storms, the tunnels and reservoirs still fill up, and the gates here at the lock still have to open. But every time a summer cloudburst passes without fish dying in the river, that hidden system is doing its work, hundreds of feet below the streets.
=== STOP DuSable Bridge & Fort Dearborn
=== DEEPER The Mystery of DuSable | people
You heard the outline of DuSable's life. Much of it is still a mystery. Nobody knows for certain where he was born. Many historians think it was Saint-Domingue, the French Caribbean colony that became Haiti, around 1745, though others have suggested French Canada, and stories that he was educated in France have never been proven.

What is documented begins with the American Revolution. In 1779, British troops arrested him at Trail Creek, near what's now Michigan City, Indiana, on suspicion of sympathizing with the Americans. A British commander described him in a footnote as handsome and well educated. He was soon released, and the British sent him to manage an estate called the Pinery, on the Saint Clair River in Michigan. By the mid-1780s, he had settled at the mouth of the Chicago River, with Kitihawa and their children.

The best evidence of what he built here turned up more than a century later. In 1913, a researcher found the bill of sale for his farm in an archive in Detroit. In 1800, he sold the whole place to a trader named Jean La Lime for six thousand livres, and the document lists every building, animal and tool in such detail that it's clear this was the most substantial homestead for miles around.

He spent his last years in Missouri, in Saint Charles, and he died there in 1818, poor by most accounts. He was buried in the town's Catholic cemetery, where a marker now honors him.

Chicago was slow to remember him. For a century, its histories began with John Kinzie, the white trader who took over the farm. It was Black Chicagoans who kept DuSable's name alive, campaigning in the 1920s and 1930s for public recognition, and getting a replica of his cabin into the World's Fair of 1933. Today there's a museum on the South Side named for him, a bust by the bridge, and the drive along the lake. The first Chicagoan, it turns out, was a man of African descent from somewhere in the French Atlantic world, with a Potawatomi wife and a farm on the north bank of the river.
=== DEEPER The Fort | history
The first Fort Dearborn went up in 1803, built by soldiers under Captain John Whistler. He had an unusual past: he had come to America as a British soldier during the Revolution, been captured, and later joined the American army. His grandson was the painter James McNeill Whistler, whose portrait of his mother is one of the most famous paintings in the world.

The fort stood on the south bank, at the bend where the river turned toward the lake. It was a simple frontier post: a log stockade with blockhouses at its corners, barracks, officers' quarters, and a flagpole. For nine years it guarded a few dozen soldiers, their families, and the traders who did business in its shadow. After the battle of 1812, it was burned.

The army came back in 1816 and built a second fort on the same spot. It was garrisoned on and off for the next twenty years, and its worst days came in 1832, during the Black Hawk War. Settlers from all over northern Illinois crowded inside for protection, and soldiers arriving by steamboat from the East brought cholera with them. Within days, the fort had become a hospital, and dozens of men died. By the late 1830s, the last troops were gone, and the growing city closed in around the old post. Part of it was torn down in the 1850s so that the river could be widened, a fire destroyed most of the rest, and the last blockhouse burned in the Great Fire of 1871.

The fort lived on in memory. A replica was built for the World's Fair of 1933, and in 1939, the city added a fourth star to its flag in its honor. Look down at the pavement near the southeast corner of the bridge, along Wacker Drive and Michigan Avenue. Bronze markers set into the sidewalk and the street trace the outline of the walls, so that thousands of people walk right through the old fort every day without knowing it.
=== DEEPER The Kinzies | people
John Kinzie, the trader who ended up with DuSable's farm, is often called the father of Chicago. His story is darker and stranger than that title suggests.

Kinzie was a silversmith by training, and he made his living in the fur trade, selling silver ornaments and goods to Native people across the Great Lakes. He took over the house on the north bank around 1804, a few years after DuSable left. In 1812, a few months before the battle at the fort, he got into a fight with Jean La Lime, the same man who had bought DuSable's farm and was then the fort's interpreter, and he stabbed La Lime to death. Kinzie said it was self-defense, and he was never tried.

When the fort was evacuated that August, the Kinzie family survived the attack because they had friends among the Potawatomi, who protected them. John Kinzie died in 1828, and his son, John H. Kinzie, became a government agent to the Ho-Chunk people in Wisconsin, and later a leader in the young city. Kinzie Street is named for the family.

His wife, Juliette Kinzie, became Chicago's first important historian. In 1856, she published Wau-Bun, a memoir of frontier life that included the family's account of the battle of 1812. For a century, it was the version most Chicagoans knew, and it helped fix the name massacre on the event.

The family's most famous descendant had nothing to do with Chicago at all. Juliette Kinzie's granddaughter and namesake, Juliette Gordon Low, grew up in Savannah, Georgia, and in 1912 she founded the Girl Scouts. So the line that runs from a trader's house on this riverbank leads, in the end, to the cookies sold every spring in front of grocery stores all over the country.
=== DEEPER The Four Stars | culture
You may have seen the city flag flying from a bridge house: two blue stripes for the branches of the river, on a white field. Look at the four red stars in the middle. Each one stands for something, and so does every point on every star.

The flag was designed by Wallace Rice, a Chicago writer, who won a city competition in 1917. His design had only two stars: one for the Great Fire of 1871, and one for the World's Fair of 1893. A third was added in 1933, for the city's second World's Fair, the Century of Progress, and the fourth in 1939, for Fort Dearborn. Because the fort came first in the city's history, its star went first on the flag, nearest the pole.

Why six points? Rice explained that in the language of flags, five-pointed stars stand for sovereign states, like the stars on the American flag, and a city isn't one. So he gave his stars six points, and gave each point a meaning. The points of the Fort Dearborn star stand for the governments that have ruled this place: France, Great Britain, Virginia, the Northwest Territory, the Indiana Territory, and Illinois. The points of the other stars stand for civic virtues and boasts, from transportation and commerce to education and justice, and even, on one star, the city's motto, I Will.

Every few years, someone proposes a fifth star, for a championship, a mayor, or a great event, and so far none has stuck.

Somewhere along the way, Chicagoans fell in love with their flag. In 2004, a survey of flag experts ranked it the second-best city flag in the United States, after Washington's. You'll see it on T-shirts, caps, coffee mugs, bike jerseys and tattoos, on bar signs and storefronts, and on the shoulders of people who have moved far away. Few cities have a symbol that so many people choose to wear.
=== STOP Wrigley Building & Tribune Tower
=== DEEPER The Colonel | people
Robert Rutherford McCormick ran the Tribune for more than forty years, and he made it as much a part of the city as the river. He was a grandson of Joseph Medill, the paper's great editor, and he was raised partly in England, where his father was a diplomat. He went to Groton and Yale, and at twenty-five, he became president of the Sanitary District, the agency that ran the canal that reversed this river.

In the First World War, he served with the artillery of the First Division in France, and saw action at the battle of Cantigny. For the rest of his life he called himself the Colonel, and he named his country estate in the western suburbs Cantigny. It's now a park and museum.

From 1925, he ran the paper alone, and he ran it as an extension of himself: fiercely conservative and isolationist, suspicious of the British Empire, of eastern bankers, and above all of Franklin Roosevelt and the New Deal. He also had odd enthusiasms. From 1934, the Tribune used his own simplified spellings: thru for through, tho for though, and frate for freight. Some of them lasted until 1975.

His paper made news as well as reporting it. In June 1942, just after the Battle of Midway, the Tribune ran a story revealing that the Navy had known the Japanese fleet's plans in advance, which hinted strongly that American codebreakers were reading Japanese messages. The government convened a grand jury, which declined to indict. The Japanese, it seems, never noticed.

And in November 1948, an early edition, printed on deadline before the votes were counted, carried the most famous wrong headline in American history: Dewey Defeats Truman. A couple of days later, at the railroad station in Saint Louis, a grinning Harry Truman held it up for the photographers. McCormick died in 1955, and the tower he built is the monument he left behind.
=== DEEPER The Competition | architecture
In 1922, the Tribune turned seventy-five, and to celebrate, it announced an international competition, with a hundred thousand dollars in prizes, for what it called the most beautiful and distinctive office building in the world. About two hundred and sixty architects from twenty-three countries sent in designs, and the entries became a snapshot of an art at a crossroads.

Some looked backward and some looked far ahead. The Viennese architect Adolf Loos submitted a skyscraper in the shape of a giant black Doric column, perhaps as a pun on a newspaper column, and perhaps as a joke at the expense of the whole contest. Walter Gropius, the founder of the Bauhaus, sent a bare, modern grid of concrete and glass that looks like it belongs in the 1950s. You heard about the second-place design, by Eliel Saarinen. Its prize of twenty thousand dollars helped bring him to America, where he became one of the most influential teachers of architecture in the country.

The winners were John Mead Howells, a well-established New York architect, and his partner Raymond Hood, thirteen years younger and barely known. Hood went on to design some of the greatest skyscrapers of the next decade, including the Daily News Building in New York and the tall slab at the center of Rockefeller Center.

Now walk up to the main entrance on Michigan Avenue and look above the doors. The carved stone screen there is full of characters from Aesop's fables, chosen by Colonel McCormick himself. The sculptor, René Paul Chambellan, slipped in a couple of jokes of his own: look for Robin Hood, for Raymond Hood, and a howling dog, for Howells.

Then look up at the crown, where the flying buttresses ring the top of the tower like the top of a cathedral. They don't hold anything up. They're there because a newspaper that called itself the world's greatest wanted a building that looked like a cathedral of the press, and it got one.
=== DEEPER Gum, War and Women's Baseball | companies
You heard how William Wrigley switched from soap to baking powder to gum. What made him rich was his faith in advertising. In 1915, he mailed free sticks of gum to every listing in the telephone books of the United States, about a million and a half of them, and a few years later he did it again, to millions more. He's often credited with the motto tell them quick and tell them often, and he put the Wrigley name on billboards, streetcar cards and electric signs across the country.

During the Second World War, gum became standard equipment for American soldiers, who chewed it to stay alert and to calm their nerves. When wartime shortages made good ingredients hard to get, Wrigley took its famous brands off the market at home altogether and sent all of them to the armed forces. Its ads at home showed nothing but an empty wrapper, with the words Remember this wrapper. Civilians had to make do with a wartime brand called Orbit.

The founder's son, Philip Wrigley, ran the company from 1932, and he owned the Cubs too. In 1943, worried that the war would empty the major leagues of players, he started the All-American Girls Professional Baseball League. Its teams played in Midwestern factory towns, like the Rockford Peaches and the South Bend Blue Sox. The players wore skirts on the field, were sent to charm school, and played hard, fast baseball. In their best season, they drew nearly a million fans. The league lasted until 1954, and it was largely forgotten until the 1992 film A League of Their Own.

In 2008, the candy company Mars bought Wrigley for about twenty-three billion dollars, and a few years later the company sold its headquarters building. But the name over the door hasn't changed, and the white tower is still lit up every night, the way the gum king wanted it.
=== DEEPER Cheezborger, Cheezborger | food
Head down the stairs from Michigan Avenue to the lower level of the street, just north of the Wrigley Building, and you'll find a dim, low-ceilinged bar and grill that has become one of the most famous in America: the Billy Goat Tavern.

It was opened in 1934 by William Sianis, a Greek immigrant, near the old Chicago Stadium on the West Side. The story goes that a goat fell off a passing truck, wandered in, and Sianis adopted it, grew a goatee, and took the goat's name for the bar. Whether that's true or not, the goat became his trademark, and you may have heard about the curse he put on the Cubs. In 1964, the tavern moved here, under Michigan Avenue, close to the Tribune and to the Sun-Times, a few blocks west along the river.

For decades, it was the newspaper reporters' bar. Printers, copy editors and columnists drank here after their shifts, and the columnist Mike Royko made it famous, writing about it and holding court at the counter. The walls are still covered with yellowed newspaper pages and photographs of the writers who drank here.

Then television made it a legend. In 1978, Saturday Night Live ran a sketch, written by Don Novello, who had spent time at the Billy Goat, in which John Belushi played a Greek grill man shouting at his customers: Cheezborger, cheezborger, no Coke, Pepsi, no fries, chips. The real grill men do say something like that, and they still do, to tourists who come in hoping to hear it.

The burgers are thin and griddled, served on a kaiser roll, and the standard order is a double cheeseburger. It's not fancy, and it's not supposed to be. It's a reminder that this stretch of Michigan Avenue, now all hotels and flagship stores, was once a newspaper town, full of people working all night to put out the morning paper.
=== STOP Riverwalk at State Street
=== DEEPER South Water Street | food
You heard that Wacker Drive replaced a market street called South Water Street. For the better part of a century, that street was the city's belly. It ran along the south bank of the river, right where you're walking, and it was Chicago's main wholesale market for fruit, vegetables, butter, eggs and poultry.

Produce came in by schooner and steamboat, by railroad car and by wagon from the farms around the city. Along the street, commission merchants sold it in the dark hours before dawn to grocers, restaurant cooks, hotel buyers and the peddlers who pushed carts through the neighborhoods. Crates were stacked on the sidewalks, wagons were jammed hub to hub in the street, and the whole district smelled of onions, oranges and horses. It was famous as the most congested street in the city.

That was exactly why the city's planners wanted it gone. The Plan of Chicago called for a grand double-decked drive along the river, and the merchants fought the move for years. They lost. In 1925, the market moved to a new South Water Market on the Near West Side, south of Roosevelt Road, between Morgan Street and Racine Avenue: rows of brick buildings with loading docks on both sides, built along a railroad embankment so that boxcars could unload right at the doors.

That market lasted until 2001. Then the produce dealers moved again, to a modern wholesale market near the Stevenson Expressway, and the old market buildings were turned into lofts and condominiums. If you visit the University Village neighborhood, near the University of Illinois at Chicago, you can still see them, with their old loading docks along the fronts.

The work itself never moved far from the river's old rhythm. Every night, while the city sleeps, trucks still roll into the wholesale market, and by dawn the fruit and vegetables are on their way to restaurants and stores all over the region, just as they once left South Water Street.
=== DEEPER The River Comes Back | nature
You heard that a few decades ago the river was considered too polluted for anything. In the early 1970s, biologists counted only about ten kinds of fish in the Chicago area's waterways, mostly tough species that could survive almost anything. Today the count is more than seventy. There are largemouth bass, bluegill, catfish, perch and pike, and fish are spawning again in places where none could live for generations.

The comeback took decades and a long list of changes. The Clean Water Act of 1972 forced factories and sewage plants to clean up what they released. The Deep Tunnel began catching storm overflow. In the middle of the 2010s, after years of pressure from environmental groups, the district's two biggest treatment plants began disinfecting the water they send into the river, one of them with ultraviolet light. And as the riverbanks turned from loading docks into parks, people started caring about what was in the water.

Wildlife followed the fish. Herons stalk the shallows along the Riverwalk, cormorants dry their wings on the pilings, beavers have built lodges along the branches, and in 2022, a photograph of a huge snapping turtle, sunning itself on a chain at the river's edge, became a sensation. Chicagoans named it Chonkosaurus.

The biggest test came in September 2025, when hundreds of people swam in the river downtown, in the first organized swim in the river in about a century, and the event has since become an annual one. The water met the organizers' safety standards, and the swimmers came out smiling.

It isn't a clean mountain stream, and nobody pretends it is. After heavy rain, overflow still carries sewage into the river, and swimming outside organized events isn't allowed. But the change in a single lifetime is astonishing. The river that the city once used as a sewer has become a place where Chicagoans go to sit, eat, paddle and look for birds, and they've started calling it the city's second shoreline.
=== DEEPER Building the Riverwalk | history
The idea of a public walk along the river is more than a century old. Burnham's plan of 1909 imagined the riverbanks downtown lined with quays and promenades, the way the Seine runs through Paris. But for most of the twentieth century, the river was a working waterway, and then a smelly one, and the lower level along it was a place for trucks and deliveries, not for people.

The modern Riverwalk began almost by accident. When the city rebuilt the crumbling Wacker Drive around 2002, it added a stretch of walkway at the water's edge. People liked it, and the city kept going, block by block. The hard part was money and space. There was barely any room between the old dock walls and the drive above, so the city got permission from the Army Corps of Engineers, which controls the river, to build out into the water, by roughly twenty-five feet.

To pay for the last six blocks, the city took out a federal transportation loan of about a hundred million dollars in 2013, to be repaid from the Riverwalk's own income, from the restaurants, bars, boat rentals and events along it. Critics doubted it would ever pay its way. The loan was paid off in 2021.

The design, by the landscape architects at Sasaki and the Chicago firm Ross Barney Architects, divided the walk into blocks between the bridges, each designed like a room with a character of its own. And it was built to get wet. The river can rise several feet in a big storm, and the lowest parts of the walk are designed to go under and come back, with tough materials and plantings that can survive a flood.

The whole length from the lake to Lake Street was finished in 2016. It won national design awards, and other cities have sent planners to study it. On a summer night, when the cafés are crowded and the boats slide by, it's hard to believe this was once the city's back door.
=== STOP Marina City & the IBM Building
=== DEEPER Bertrand Goldberg | people
Bertrand Goldberg was a Chicagoan, born in 1913. He studied at Harvard, then went to Germany in the early 1930s to study at the Bauhaus, until the Nazis shut it down. He came home with the modernist faith in industry and new materials, and spent the next twenty years trying to apply it to how people actually live.

He was an inventor as much as an architect. During the Second World War, he designed a portable delousing unit to fight typhus among troops in North Africa, and a mobile laboratory for making penicillin. After the war, he designed a railroad boxcar made of molded plywood, and then a prefabricated house adapted from it, called the Unishelter. Most of these never went into mass production, but they taught him to think about buildings as engineered objects, shaped by how they're made and used.

Marina City came from an unlikely client: the janitors' union. Its leader, William McFetridge, wanted to put his members' money into downtown Chicago, to create jobs and to show that the city's center could be a place to live, not just to work. The project grew into the towers you see, financed largely by the union and its allies, and built at a time when almost everyone else was building in the suburbs.

Goldberg spent the rest of his career designing curving concrete buildings, especially hospitals, which he saw as small cities organized around nurses and patients. You may hear about one of them, a cloverleaf-shaped women's hospital in Streeterville, on the Michigan Avenue tour. In the 1980s, he built River City, a long, snaking apartment complex on the South Branch of the river, about a mile and a half south of here.

He died in 1997. For years his work was treated as a curiosity. Now Marina City is a Chicago Landmark, and architects around the world study his buildings again, as proof that modern architecture didn't have to be a box.
=== DEEPER Newspaper Row | companies
Look east along the river from Marina City, past the dark IBM Building, to the very tall, silvery tower beside it. For half a century, that site held the low, modern building of the Chicago Sun-Times, which was written, edited and printed right here on the river.

The paper was the product of a rivalry. In 1941, Marshall Field the Third, grandson of the merchant, founded the Chicago Sun as a liberal answer to Colonel McCormick's Tribune, and in 1948 it merged with the Daily Times to become the Sun-Times. It was scrappier than the Tribune, a tabloid in size, and it had some of the best writers in the city.

One of them was Roger Ebert, who became the paper's film critic in 1967 and, in 1975, the first film critic ever to win the Pulitzer Prize. With Gene Siskel of the Tribune, he went on to television, and the two of them made thumbs up and thumbs down a national verdict on the movies. Another was the columnist Mike Royko, who came over from the Daily News. When the Australian publisher Rupert Murdoch bought the Sun-Times in 1984, Royko announced that no self-respecting fish would be wrapped in a Murdoch paper, and he quit and moved to the Tribune.

The paper's most famous investigation was a bar. In 1977, the Sun-Times and a civic watchdog group bought a run-down tavern on North Wells Street, named it the Mirage, and staffed it with reporters and investigators. For months, they recorded city inspectors and others taking small bribes to overlook violations. The series ran in 1978 and caused a sensation, but the Pulitzer board passed it over, because some of its members thought the deception was unethical.

In 2004, the Sun-Times building was torn down to make room for the tower that stands there now, completed in 2009. Its giant letters, added in 2014, have drawn arguments ever since.
=== DEEPER Mies's Last Tower | architecture
You heard that this was Mies's last American office tower. In fact, he died before construction began, and the building was finished in 1973 by the office he had built, working from his ideas. It's fifty-two stories and almost seven hundred feet tall, which makes it the second-tallest building ever designed by Mies, after a bank tower in Toronto.

It was built for IBM, which was then the giant of the computer age, and it shows Mies's late style at its most refined. Look at the skin: dark aluminum and bronze-tinted glass, with thin vertical mullions running up the face of the building from the plaza to the roof. Those mullions do little structural work. They're there to express the frame inside and to give the flat facade rhythm and depth as the sun moves across it. Look at the plaza, too, opening onto the river.

If the lobby is open, step inside. You'll find a bronze bust of Mies, by the Italian sculptor Marino Marini, looking out at the river through the glass.

The building has changed with the city around it. IBM moved out, and in 2008 the tower was made a Chicago Landmark. A few years later, the American Medical Association moved its headquarters here, and the building was renamed AMA Plaza. Several of its lower floors were turned into a luxury hotel, the Langham, which opened in 2013, with rooms where IBM's offices used to be.

Standing between this tower and Goldberg's round ones, it's worth remembering that the two architects had a history. Goldberg had worked for Mies in Berlin as a young man. The student built curves, the teacher built grids, and Chicago got to keep both, side by side on the same riverbank.
=== STOP The Eastland Disaster
=== DEEPER Why She Rolled | history
The Eastland was launched in 1903 at Port Huron, Michigan, as a fast excursion steamer for the lake trade, and she had a reputation almost from the start. She was narrow for her length and carried a lot of weight high up, and within a year or two of her launch, she had listed alarmingly with a crowd on board. Over the next decade her owners changed her again and again, adding and removing cabins and equipment, and sailors on the lakes were said to call her a cranky ship, their word for an unstable one.

Then came the Titanic. After that disaster in 1912, the public demanded lifeboats for everyone, and in March 1915, Congress passed the Seamen's Act, which required them. The Eastland had been designed to carry six lifeboats. By July 1915, she carried eleven lifeboats, thirty-seven life rafts and life jackets for everyone on board, most of it on her upper decks. Nobody ran a test to see what that extra weight would do to her stability.

On the morning of the disaster, the ship's chief engineer, Joseph Erickson, was managing her ballast tanks, letting water in and pumping it out to keep her level as the crowd surged aboard. Thousands of passengers packed onto the upper decks, and many of them moved to the river side to watch the other boats. The ship began to tip. It's still debated exactly what went wrong with the ballast that morning, but most historians now believe that a ship already top-heavy from the new lifeboats was doomed the moment so many people crowded so high up.

Afterward, the blame fell everywhere and nowhere. A federal case was brought against the ship's officers and owners, and Erickson was defended by Clarence Darrow, the most famous trial lawyer in the country. In 1916, a federal judge in Michigan ruled that there wasn't enough evidence against any of them. Lawsuits by the victims' families dragged on for more than twenty years, and in the end they won almost nothing.
=== DEEPER Hawthorne Works | companies
The people on the Eastland that morning worked for one of the most important factories in America. Western Electric built its Hawthorne Works in Cicero, just west of the city, in the first years of the twentieth century. By 1917, it employed about twenty-five thousand people, and at its peak, about forty-five thousand. Besides telephones, it turned out the switchboards, cables and switching equipment that connected them.

Hawthorne was a world of its own, with its own sports teams, clubs, evening classes and a magazine for employees, and the annual picnic was the high point of its summer.

The factory also became famous in a way nobody expected. Starting in 1924, researchers ran a series of experiments there to find out how working conditions, like lighting and rest breaks, affected productivity. They found that output seemed to rise almost no matter what they changed, which suggested that the workers were responding mostly to being watched and paid attention to. That became known as the Hawthorne effect, and it's still taught in business schools and psychology classes, although later scholars have argued about what the experiments really showed.

Two pioneers of modern quality control learned their trade at Hawthorne too. Joseph Juran worked there as an engineer, and W. Edwards Deming worked there briefly as a young man. After the Second World War, both men taught their methods to Japanese manufacturers, helping to make Japanese products famous for their quality.

The Hawthorne Works closed in 1983, as the Bell telephone system was being broken up, and most of the buildings came down. A shopping center stands on much of the site today. Few of the shoppers know that, for most of a century, the telephones of America were made there.
=== DEEPER Missed Boats and Second Lives | people
One of the Western Electric employees who was supposed to be on the Eastland that morning was a twenty-year-old from the West Side named George Halas, working at the Hawthorne Works for the summer. He didn't board. In later years he said he'd decided to take a later boat, though accounts of why differ. Either way, a newspaper printed his name among the employees missing and presumed dead. When his fraternity brothers came by his house to offer their condolences, they found him alive. A few years later, he founded the team that became the Chicago Bears.

Others weren't so lucky. Whole families were wiped out together. In the days after the disaster, Chicagoans lined up outside the temporary morgue to identify their relatives, and the funerals went on for days across the West Side and the suburbs, especially in the Czech neighborhoods.

The ship herself had a long second life. After she was raised, she was sold to the Navy, rebuilt as a gunboat, and renamed the Wilmette. In 1921, as part of a gunnery exercise in Lake Michigan off Highland Park, her guns sank a German submarine that had been surrendered after the First World War and brought to the Great Lakes for display. In the Second World War, she trained sailors on the lake. She was finally scrapped after the war.

For most of the twentieth century, the disaster was strangely forgotten. It happened as Europe was at war, and it was soon overshadowed by bigger news, and most of its victims were working people without powerful friends. A historical marker went up here only in 1989. In recent decades, descendants of the victims and survivors have worked to gather every name and every story, and to make sure that the deadliest day in Chicago's history is remembered on the riverbank where it happened.
=== STOP Merchandise Mart
=== DEEPER The Kennedys' Mart | people
You heard that Joseph P. Kennedy bought the Mart in 1945. He had already been a Wall Street speculator, a Hollywood producer, the first chairman of the Securities and Exchange Commission, and the American ambassador to Britain, and he paid Marshall Field and Company about twelve and a half million dollars for the building. Field's had built it at the start of the Depression, and had struggled for years to fill it.

Kennedy filled it. Its rents became a steady stream of money for the family, and some of that money helped pay for the campaigns that sent his son John to the House, the Senate, and in 1960, the White House.

To help run the Mart, Kennedy hired a young man named Sargent Shriver, who came to Chicago in 1948. Shriver married Kennedy's daughter Eunice in 1953 and stayed in Chicago for most of the 1950s, running the Mart's business and serving as president of the city's Board of Education. Then he went to Washington, where he became the first director of the Peace Corps and led President Lyndon Johnson's War on Poverty. Eunice Kennedy Shriver, meanwhile, started the camp for children with intellectual disabilities that grew into the Special Olympics, whose first games were held at Soldier Field in Chicago in 1968.

In 1953, Joseph Kennedy also gave the Mart its odd row of bronze heads facing the river, a kind of hall of fame for American retailers. He launched it at a black-tie dinner on the roof.

The family owned the Mart for more than fifty years. In 1998, they sold it to the real estate company Vornado, in a deal worth about six hundred and twenty-five million dollars. The Kennedys held on to one more piece of riverfront, though: the land at Wolf Point, just west of here, which for decades was a parking lot, and which the family finally developed into glass towers in the 2010s and 2020s.
=== DEEPER The Chicago School of Television | culture
You heard that NBC had studios in the Mart. In the late 1940s and early 1950s, when television was brand new, those studios were among the most creative in the country. Chicago didn't have New York's Broadway stars or Hollywood's money, so its producers made a virtue of being small, cheap and intimate. Critics called the results the Chicago School of Television.

Kukla, Fran and Ollie was the most famous of them. The puppeteer Burr Tillstrom and the actress Fran Allison improvised every episode, with no script, and adults loved it as much as children did. The show's announcer was a young Hugh Downs, who later became one of the best-known faces on network television.

Garroway at Large, starring Dave Garroway, was a relaxed variety show that let the cameras wander around the studio, showing the cables and the crew, and talking to the viewers as if they were in the room. Garroway went on to become the first host of the Today show in 1952. Studs' Place starred the broadcaster Studs Terkel as the owner of a neighborhood diner, with his regulars making up their lines as they went. And Ding Dong School, starring a former teacher named Frances Horwich, known to millions of children as Miss Frances, was one of the first television shows made for preschoolers. She spoke directly to the children watching at home and asked them to help with the day's activities.

It didn't last. As the networks connected the country by cable and moved production to New York and Hollywood, Chicago lost most of its shows by the mid-1950s. NBC stayed in the Mart until 1989, when it moved to its own tower on the river, a few blocks east.

But the Chicago School left a mark. Its easygoing, unscripted style, talking to the camera as if to a friend, shaped morning television and talk shows for decades. And a later Chicago talk show, taped a couple of miles west of here, would turn out to be one of the most successful in television history.
=== DEEPER Merchants on Columns | look
Walk down to the river side of the Mart and look at the row of tall columns topped with bronze heads. These are the busts of the Merchants of America Hall of Fame, which Joseph Kennedy created in 1953 to celebrate the men who had built American retailing.

You heard about Marshall Field and Frank Woolworth. The others tell the story of how Americans learned to shop. John Wanamaker of Philadelphia built one of the first great department stores and helped popularize the fixed price tag, so that customers no longer had to haggle. He's often credited with a line about advertising: half the money I spend on it is wasted, and the trouble is I don't know which half. Nobody has ever found proof that he said it. Edward Filene of Boston became famous for his automatic bargain basement, where prices dropped every week that an item didn't sell, and he spent much of his fortune promoting credit unions for working people.

George Huntington Hartford built the Great Atlantic and Pacific Tea Company, the A and P, into the first great national chain of grocery stores. Julius Rosenwald turned Sears into a giant, and gave away much of his fortune, as you can hear on the Hyde Park tour. Aaron Montgomery Ward invented the general mail-order catalog in Chicago in 1872. And General Robert E. Wood, an army quartermaster who had helped supply the building of the Panama Canal, took Sears out of the catalog business alone and into retail stores, betting correctly that Americans were moving to the suburbs and buying cars.

It's an odd monument, a little like a row of saints outside a cathedral of commerce, and an old-fashioned one. Nobody has been added to it in decades, and there are no women among the merchants. The marker that once named the promenade is gone, and today the busts stand watch over an outdoor restaurant, while diners eat lunch beneath the founders of the department store, the price tag and the bargain basement.
=== STOP Wolf Point
=== DEEPER The First Chicago | history
Before there was a city here, there was Wolf Point, and almost every first in Chicago's history happened within sight of where you're standing.

Nobody is sure how it got its name. Juliette Kinzie, who came here in the early 1830s and later wrote a memoir of those days, said it was named for a Native man called Moaway, or the Wolf, who lived here. Others said it was named for the wolves that howled at night, or for a sign with a painted wolf that hung outside a tavern.

The taverns came first. In 1828, James Kinzie, a son of the trader John Kinzie, built one on the west bank of the river, and soon there were three, serving traders, travelers and the soldiers from the fort. In 1831, a French-Canadian innkeeper named Mark Beaubien opened Chicago's first hotel here, the Sauganash, a rambling frame building where he's remembered for playing the fiddle for his guests. He named it for his friend Billy Caldwell, whose Potawatomi name was Sauganash. Caldwell was the son of an Irish officer in the British army and a Mohawk woman, and he became a leader among the Potawatomi, helping to negotiate the treaty of 1833 by which they gave up their last lands around Chicago.

Beaubien also ran the first ferry across the river. Around Wolf Point grew the town's first store, its first church, its first bridges, and its first theater company, which performed in the hotel's dining room.

It was all wooden, and all temporary. Within a few years, the center of town moved east, toward the lake and the new streets of the Loop, and Wolf Point became an industrial corner, crowded with warehouses and rail yards. For decades in the twentieth century, much of it was a parking lot. Today, glass towers stand on the point, and a small park at the water's edge lets you stand where Chicago began, at the place where the river divides.
=== DEEPER Lincoln at the Wigwam | politics
Look south across the river toward the Lake Street bridge. On the far bank, at the corner of Lake Street and what's now Wacker Drive, close to where Beaubien's old hotel had stood, the Republican Party built a huge wooden hall in the spring of 1860 for its national convention. They called it the Wigwam, and it held about ten thousand people.

Almost everyone expected the nomination to go to William Seward, the senator from New York, who was the party's best-known leader. Abraham Lincoln was a former one-term congressman from Illinois, famous mainly for his debates with Stephen Douglas two years before. He stayed home in Springfield, as candidates did in those days, and left the work to his managers, led by a burly judge named David Davis.

They worked hard. Lincoln telegraphed them not to make any deals that would bind him, and they are said to have ignored him, trading promises of cabinet posts for delegates. The story goes that Lincoln's friends also printed extra tickets to the hall and handed them out to their own supporters, who got there early and filled the seats, so that when Seward's marching bands and followers arrived, many couldn't get in. It's a famous story, repeated in countless histories, though the evidence for it is thinner than you'd think.

What is certain is that the Wigwam was loud. When Lincoln's name was put in nomination, the hall erupted, and the noise was described as a roar like nothing anyone had ever heard. Seward led on the first ballot, Lincoln gained on the second, and on the third ballot, on May 18, 1860, Lincoln won. A cannon on the roof signaled the news to the crowds outside, and the city celebrated through the night.

Within a year, Lincoln was president and the country was at war. The Wigwam itself didn't survive long, but Chicago has hosted more national political conventions than any other city in America.
=== DEEPER Goose Island | food
Look north up the North Branch toward Goose Island, which you heard about. The island was born in the 1850s, when a channel was dug across the neck of a river bend. Irish immigrants settled along this stretch of the river in those years, in a shantytown known as Kilgubbin, a name said to come from County Cork. Later came tanneries, soap works and coal yards, and for most of the twentieth century, the island was all industry.

The beer that made its name famous began in 1988, when John Hall opened a brewpub on Clybourn Avenue, near the island. At the time, most Americans drank the same few mass-market lagers, and a brewpub was a novelty. In 1992, Goose Island's brewers aged a heavy stout in used bourbon barrels from Kentucky, and Bourbon County Stout became one of the most imitated beers in the world. Today, bourbon barrel stouts are everywhere, and every November, when the new batch is released, people line up for it before dawn.

In 2011, Goose Island was sold to Anheuser-Busch InBev, the world's largest brewer, for close to forty million dollars. Many beer lovers were outraged, and the sale became a symbol of the craft beer business growing up, or selling out, depending on whom you ask.

The island itself has changed too. Many of the factories are gone, replaced by technology offices and labs. Along the old canal on the island's east side, a project called the Wild Mile has been building floating gardens and walkways, to bring wildlife and people back to a channel that was once lined with factories. And just upriver, on the west bank at Elston Avenue, the old Morton Salt warehouse, with its famous sign reading When It Rains It Pours, reopened in 2022 as a concert hall called the Salt Shed.

So the North Branch, once Chicago's industrial back alley, now has beer, music and floating gardens, which is a long way from tanneries and coal.
