=== DEEPER The Battle of the Viaduct | history
The strike reached Chicago on July twenty-third, 1877. That evening, a young typesetter named Albert Parsons climbed onto a platform downtown and spoke to an enormous crowd of workers, by some estimates tens of thousands, about wages, hours, and the power of the railroads. The next day, he was fired from his job at the Chicago Times, and two plainclothes police officers came for him. By his own account, he was brought before the chief of police and a roomful of angry businessmen, who told him to get out of town. For about two years afterward, he later wrote, no print shop in Chicago would hire him.

Across the city, workers walked out of the rail yards, the lumber yards, the packinghouses, and the factories, and crowds moved from shop to shop, calling on others to join them. The city answered with its police, with businessmen and veterans sworn in as special patrols, with the state militia, and finally with regular army troops sent by the federal government.

The worst fighting came here, on July twenty-sixth, when the police estimated the crowd around the Halsted Street viaduct at about ten thousand people. The strikers had stones and bricks; the police and soldiers had clubs and guns. The battle surged back and forth across the viaduct and along Halsted Street for hours. That same day, a few blocks to the north, police forced their way into a hall on 12th Street, where the furniture workers' union had met with employers to talk about wages and the eight-hour day, and drove the men out with their clubs.

Both sides drew lessons from that week. Chicago's business leaders helped pay for new armories for the militia, and after the Haymarket bombing nine years later, a group of them bought land on the lakeshore north of the city and gave it to the army for a post, Fort Sheridan, so that troops would be close at hand. And many of the workers who struck that week went on to join unions and the eight-hour movement, while some, like Parsons, turned to far more radical ideas.
=== DEEPER The Reaper Works | companies
The story of the reaper begins on a farm in the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia. Cyrus McCormick's father, Robert, had spent years trying to build a machine that could cut grain, and he never got it to work. In 1831, the son tried again, with the help of Jo Anderson, a man enslaved by the McCormick family, and that summer, their horse-drawn machine cut grain in a field near the farm.

McCormick wasn't the only one at work. Obed Hussey patented a reaper in 1833, a year before McCormick did, and the two men fought for decades, in demonstration fields and in the courts.

What made McCormick rich was not only the machine, but where and how he sold it. He saw that the future of grain farming lay on the flat prairies of the Midwest, and in 1847 he moved to Chicago and built a factory on the north bank of the river, with backing from the city's first mayor, William Ogden. He's often credited with selling his reapers in ways that were new at the time: at a fixed price, with a written guarantee, often on credit, to be paid off after the harvest, and through traveling agents who showed farmers the machine at work in their own fields.

The Great Fire destroyed the factory in 1871, and two years later, the company opened a much larger works, out on the South Branch of the river. By the 1880s, it was one of the biggest factories in Chicago.

Cyrus died in 1884, and his son, Cyrus Junior, took over. In 1902, with the help of the banking house of J. P. Morgan, the company merged with its fiercest rival, the Deering company, and several others, to form International Harvester. The new company built the Farmall tractor in the 1920s, one of the machines that ended the age of the horse on American farms. In 1985, it sold its farm equipment business, and the next year, what was left took a new name: Navistar.
=== DEEPER The White Pine | nature
Many of the old houses you're walking past were built from one tree: the eastern white pine. It once grew in vast forests across northern Michigan, Wisconsin, and Minnesota, in stands of trees that could be centuries old and well over a hundred feet tall. Its wood was light, soft, and straight-grained, easy to saw and to nail, and it kept its shape as it dried. It was ideal for the cheap, quick balloon-frame houses and barns that went up all over the prairie.

The logging was done in winter. Crews of lumberjacks felled the trees and hauled the logs on sleds over iced roads to the riverbanks, and when the ice broke up in spring, the logs were floated downstream to the sawmills at the river mouths, in towns like Muskegon and Menominee. There the logs were sawn into boards, stacked onto schooners, and sailed across Lake Michigan to Chicago.

Along the South Branch, the lumbermen dug slips into the land, narrow channels where the schooners could tie up and unload. Gangs of lumber shovers, many of them Czech and German immigrants from this neighborhood, carried the boards off the boats by hand and stacked them in towering piles to dry. In 1880, Chicago handled more than a billion board feet of lumber, graded, dried, and shipped by rail to the treeless farm towns of the prairies and the plains.

By the end of the century, the great pine forests were nearly gone. The loggers left behind a wasteland of stumps and dry branches, called the cutover, which burned again and again. Chicago's lumber trade moved on, to the pines of the South and the firs of the Pacific Northwest. The northern forests have grown back, but only scraps of the old white pine woods survive, and today the white pine is Michigan's state tree.

So when you look at an old Pilsen cottage, with its narrow front and its steep roof, you may be looking at a piece of a forest that vanished more than a century ago.

=== DEEPER The Freethinkers | culture
Many of the Czechs who came to Chicago had little love for the Catholic Church. At home, it was bound up with the Habsburg emperors who ruled the Czech lands from Vienna, and with the memory of the 1600s, when the Habsburgs had stamped out Czech Protestantism by force. In America, for the first time, people could say so out loud.

In 1870, they founded the Congregation of Bohemian Freethinkers of Chicago. It wasn't a church, but it did much of what a church did. It set up building and benevolent societies, kept a school and a library, ran programs for children and lectures for adults, and sponsored concerts and plays. In 1875, a publisher named August Geringer began printing Svornost, which means Concord, the first Czech daily newspaper in the United States, and for many years its editor was the freethinker František Zdrůbek. Two years later, the freethinkers opened a Czech school that met on Sundays, and Geringer himself taught there on Sunday afternoons.

The freethinkers' greatest monument is on the city's Northwest Side. Early in 1877, after a priest, as the story goes, had refused to bury a Czech woman in a Catholic cemetery, more than twenty Czech societies met and agreed to build a cemetery of their own, where any Czech could be buried, whatever he or she believed. The Bohemian National Cemetery was chartered that April, and its first burial took place on July first. Today it holds well over a hundred thousand graves, and it's listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

For decades, Czech Chicago was really two communities, side by side. Catholics sent their children to parish schools like the one at Saint Procopius, read their own daily paper, Národ, and were buried in Catholic ground. Freethinkers sent theirs to the free schools, read Svornost, and, when the time came, were buried among their own, in the Bohemian National Cemetery.
=== DEEPER Masaryk's American Family | people
Tomáš Masaryk was born in 1850 in Moravia, the son of a coachman, and he became a professor of philosophy in Prague. But the person who shaped his life most was an American. Charlotte Garrigue came from a wealthy family in Brooklyn, and she was studying music in Leipzig, in Germany, when she met the young Czech scholar. They married in New York in 1878, and Masaryk did something almost unheard of for a man of his time: he took her family name as his own middle name, and he signed himself Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk for the rest of his life. Charlotte, a passionate believer in women's rights, helped translate John Stuart Mill's The Subjection of Women into Czech.

Masaryk's ties to Chicago began long before 1918. In 1902, at the invitation of Charles Crane, the heir to a Chicago manufacturing fortune and a man fascinated by the Slavic world, he gave a series of lectures at the University of Chicago and met the Czech community here. It was the beginning of a long friendship, and Crane became one of the most important American friends of the Czech cause.

After Czechoslovakia won its independence, the two families grew even closer. Crane's son, Richard, became the first American minister to the new country, in 1919, and in 1924, Masaryk's son, Jan, married Crane's daughter Frances. Tomáš Masaryk served as president until 1935, and he died two years later.

Jan Masaryk became a diplomat himself. During the Second World War, he spoke to his occupied country by radio from London, and after the war, he returned home as foreign minister. In March 1948, two weeks after the Communists seized power in Prague, he was found dead in the courtyard beneath a window of the foreign ministry. The new government called it suicide. Many Czechs never believed it, and later investigations have suggested that he may have been murdered, but the question has never been settled.
=== DEEPER From Pilsen to Berwyn | history
The Czechs didn't stay in Pilsen forever, and the path they took out of it is one of the clearest in Chicago's history. Many of the first Czech settlers, in the 1850s and 1860s, lived closer to downtown, on the Near West Side. After the Great Fire, as the lumber yards and factories along the South Branch grew, more and more of them settled here, and by the end of the nineteenth century, Chicago had become the third-largest Czech city in the world, after Prague and Vienna.

Then, as families saved money, they moved west along 22nd Street, the street that's now Cermak Road. Around 26th Street and California Avenue, they built a new neighborhood, known as Czech California. By 1910, Czechs owned about four out of five of the houses there.

After the First World War, they kept going, across the city line into the suburbs of Cicero and Berwyn. Many found work at Western Electric's giant Hawthorne Works in Cicero. In Cicero and Berwyn, Cermak Road was lined with so many savings and loan associations that people called it the Bohemian Wall Street. With their help, thousands of Czech families bought brick bungalows on quiet, tree-lined streets, the dream that had started in a frame cottage in Pilsen. And for decades, Cicero has held a fall festival called Houby Day, after the Czech word for mushrooms, which Czech families have long gathered in the woods.

Each time the Czechs moved on, new arrivals took their places. In Pilsen, and in Czech California, which is now Little Village, the newcomers were Mexican, and the old Czech halls, churches, and storefronts gradually became theirs. If you look closely at the cornerstones and the names carved over the doorways, you can still read the story of one community in buildings now used by another.
=== STOP The Fisk Power Plant & Samuel Insull
=== DEEPER The Turbine Gamble | companies
When Samuel Insull went looking for a steam turbine big enough for Fisk Street, the company best able to build it was reluctant to try. The turbines General Electric had made until then were much smaller, and the company's head, Charles Coffin, balked at the size Insull wanted: five thousand kilowatts. Insull pressed, and the two men struck a deal. General Electric would take the risk of building the machine, and Insull's company, Chicago Edison, would pay to install it.

The turbine stood upright, unlike the turbines of later years, and when it was first tested, in March 1903, Insull was there to watch. It worked, and on October second, 1903, the station went into service.

The plant was designed by a young Chicago engineering firm, Sargent and Lundy, founded a dozen years earlier by Frederick Sargent and a partner with a memorable first name, Illinois Lundy. The firm is still based in Chicago, and it still designs power plants around the world.

Progress came so fast that by 1909, the first Fisk turbine had been replaced by bigger machines, and General Electric took it back to its works in Schenectady, New York. It's still on display there, and the American Society of Mechanical Engineers has named it a landmark of engineering history.

In 1907, Chicago Edison merged with its sister company, Commonwealth Electric, to form Commonwealth Edison, and the name survives today, on the electric bills of northern Illinois.

Insull's other great idea was about prices. On a trip home to England in the 1890s, he saw a new kind of meter in the seaside town of Brighton, which recorded not only how much power a customer used, but the most that customer drew at any one moment. With meters like it, Chicago Edison could charge each customer a share of what it cost to build the plants, and then very little for each hour of use beyond that. The more steadily a customer used power, the less each unit cost, and the more steadily the turbines ran. That was the secret of cheap electricity, and versions of it are still built into electric rates today.
=== DEEPER The Flight of Samuel Insull | people
When Insull's empire collapsed in 1932, he resigned from his companies and sailed for Europe. At home, he became a symbol of everything that had gone wrong in the 1920s. Franklin Roosevelt, campaigning for president that fall, attacked him by name, and prosecutors in Chicago charged him with embezzlement, and later with mail fraud.

Insull went to Greece, and the Greek courts refused American requests to send him back. Then, early in 1934, under heavy pressure from Washington, the Greek government ordered him to leave the country.

What followed made headlines around the world. Insull, then seventy-four years old, boarded a small Greek freighter, the Maiotis, and put to sea, bound, it was said, for Egypt. For about two weeks, the papers followed the old man's voyage across the eastern Mediterranean, guessing where he might try to land. When the ship anchored at Istanbul, Turkish police came aboard and took him off, and he was handed over to the Americans and brought home to face trial.

The federal trial opened in Chicago in October 1934. The government's case was led by the United States attorney in Chicago, Dwight Green, who later became governor of Illinois. Insull took the stand and told the story of his life, from office boy in London to master of a utility empire, and the jury found him not guilty. He was acquitted in the state courts too.

Insull went back to Europe. In July 1938, he collapsed and died of a heart attack in a station of the Paris subway, at the age of seventy-eight.

Historians still argue about him. To some, he was a swindler who built a house of cards and left small investors holding the losses. To others, he was a visionary who made electricity cheap and plentiful, whose holding companies were no worse than many others, and who was made a scapegoat for the Depression. The laws that followed his fall, which broke up utility holding companies and put them under federal regulation, shaped the power industry for the rest of the century.
=== DEEPER The Fight for Clean Air | politics
By the early 2000s, Fisk and its sister plant, the Crawford station in Little Village, stood in the middle of crowded neighborhoods, surrounded by homes, schools, and playgrounds. In 2002, a study from the Harvard School of Public Health estimated that pollution from the two plants was causing forty-one premature deaths and twenty-eight hundred asthma attacks every year.

Two neighborhood groups were at the heart of the fight to close them. One was the Pilsen Environmental Rights and Reform Organization, whose initials spell perro, Spanish for dog. The other was the Little Village Environmental Justice Organization. They knocked on doors, held marches and vigils, and packed hearings at City Hall. In 2010, aldermen introduced a Clean Power Ordinance that would have forced the plants to cut their soot drastically, or shut down. It never made it out of committee, but it kept up the pressure.

On February twenty-ninth, 2012, the plants' owner, Midwest Generation, announced that it would close them both, in a deal brokered with help from Mayor Rahm Emanuel. By that fall, both had stopped burning coal. The next year, Kimberly Wasserman, a leader of the Little Village group, won the Goldman Environmental Prize, one of the world's top honors for grassroots activists, for her part in the campaign.

The story didn't end there. In April 2020, during the first weeks of the pandemic, a developer demolished the Crawford plant's smokestack, and a huge cloud of dust rolled over the homes of Little Village, at a time when people were being told to stay inside to protect their lungs. The anger that followed pushed the city to tighten its rules for demolitions and to look harder at where it allows polluting industries to operate.

For Pilsen, the closing of Fisk was a turning point, and it raised a new question that the neighborhood is still answering: when the smokestacks go dark, and the air gets cleaner, who gets to stay and breathe it?

In the early 1970s, Pilsen's teenagers started high school in a small building in the neighborhood, the Froebel branch of Harrison High School, where ninth graders took classes before moving on to the main school. The main school was a long way to the west, and getting there, many families said, meant crossing the turf of rival gangs.

Parents petitioned for a full high school in Pilsen, and the Board of Education turned them down. Then the board moved to close the Froebel branch itself. That was the last straw. On June fifth, 1973, students and parents set off on a long march from Froebel to the offices of the Board of Education downtown. There were more demonstrations, a boycott, and a takeover of the Froebel building, and at last the board agreed to spend almost nine million dollars on a new school.

Much of the organizing came from Pilsen Neighbors, the community council, and from three women who are remembered as the school's founding mothers: Mary Gonzales, Teresa Fraga, and Raquel Guerrero. They and the students who marched with them had learned that in Chicago, a neighborhood got what it was organized enough to demand.

The school was designed by one of the most celebrated architects in Mexico, Pedro Ramírez Vázquez, who had designed the National Museum of Anthropology in Mexico City and Aztec Stadium, the great soccer stadium there.

It was dedicated on September sixteenth, 1977, Mexican Independence Day. For the families who had fought for it, the building was a declaration of its own: that the Mexican children of Pilsen deserved a school as good as any in the city, in their own neighborhood, named for a hero of their own history.

Over the decades since, its graduates have included teachers, artists, union organizers, doctors, and elected officials, many of them the first in their families to finish high school.
=== DEEPER Rudy Lozano | people
Rudy Lozano was born in Texas in 1951 and came to Chicago as a boy. By his twenties, he was an organizer for the International Ladies' Garment Workers' Union, signing up workers in the garment shops and factories of the city, many of them immigrants. He rose to become the union's chief organizer in the Midwest.

He believed that Mexican and Black Chicagoans, working side by side, could change the city, and he worked hard to bring them together. His work went on without him. His campaign manager, Jesús García, known as Chuy, won the 22nd Ward seat in 1986, and decades later, he was elected to Congress. Others who had worked beside Lozano went on to lead unions, community groups, and city departments.

The neighborhood remembers him. In 1989, the Chicago Public Library opened its Rudy Lozano branch in Pilsen, a school bears his name, and July twenty-ninth is honored as Rudy Lozano Day.
=== DEEPER The Shepherd Boy From Oaxaca | history
Benito Juárez was born in 1806 in a small village in the mountains of Oaxaca, and by the age of three, he had lost both his parents. He grew up herding sheep for an uncle, speaking only his people's language, Zapotec. At twelve, he walked to the city of Oaxaca, where his sister worked as a cook for a merchant family, the Mazas, whose daughter Margarita he would one day marry. A bookbinder who was a devout Franciscan lay brother took him in and saw to his schooling. He learned Spanish, studied law, and rose to become governor of his state.

In 1853, the dictator Antonio López de Santa Anna sent him into exile, and he spent a hard year and a half in New Orleans, where, it's said, he rolled cigars to make a living. He came back to help overthrow Santa Anna, and then to help write the Reform laws, which separated church and state, ended the special courts of the clergy and the army, and put much of the Church's land up for sale.

When the French army invaded, the Mexicans won a famous victory at Puebla on May fifth, 1862, the Cinco de Mayo, which is still celebrated far more in the United States than in Mexico. But the French took the capital the next year and made an Austrian archduke, Maximilian, emperor of Mexico. Juárez never gave up. He carried his government north in a black carriage, all the way to the town of Paso del Norte, on the Rio Grande, which is now called Ciudad Juárez in his honor.

When the French withdrew, Maximilian was captured, tried, and executed by a firing squad in 1867, despite pleas for mercy from around the world, among them one from the French novelist Victor Hugo. Juárez died in office five years later.

His birthday, March twenty-first, is a national holiday in Mexico, and his face is on Mexican money. For the students who walk through these doors, he's a reminder that a shepherd boy who didn't speak Spanish until he was twelve could become the president who saved his country.

=== DEEPER The Great Muralists | culture
The great Mexican muralists were children of the Mexican Revolution. When the fighting ended, around 1920, the new government's minister of education, José Vasconcelos, set out to teach a nation where most people couldn't read, and he handed artists the walls of public buildings in Mexico City, to paint the country's history where everyone could see it.

Three of them, Rivera, Orozco, and Siqueiros, became known as the big three, and in the 1930s, all three painted in the United States. Orozco painted a burning Prometheus in a college dining hall in California, and an epic of the Americas in the library at Dartmouth College. Rivera covered the walls of the Detroit Institute of Arts with the assembly lines of the Ford plant, and in New York, he painted a mural at Rockefeller Center that was destroyed because it included a portrait of Lenin. In Los Angeles, Siqueiros painted a crucified Indian beneath an American eagle, and it was soon painted over.

Of the three, Siqueiros was the great experimenter. He painted with industrial lacquers and spray guns, and in 1936, one of the young artists in his workshop in New York was Jackson Pollock, who, it's often said, first saw paint poured and dripped there, the technique he later made famous.

Decades later, young Mexican American artists in Chicago took up the tradition. In 1970, a young painter named Ray Patlán became the first artist in residence at Casa Aztlán, the community center on Racine Avenue, and began painting with neighborhood teenagers, on walls they passed every day. When the first murals faded, other artists, among them Marcos Raya, painted new ones over them, and the walls became a conversation between generations.

Patlán later moved to California, where he went on painting murals for decades. He died in 2024, at seventy-seven.

Outdoor murals don't last forever. Sun, rain, and Chicago winters fade and crack them, and so the walls of Pilsen keep changing, as old murals are restored, painted over, or replaced. The wall in front of you may look quite different twenty years from now.
=== DEEPER A Czech Saint for a Polish Church | names
The church whose towers rise over Paulina Street was built by Poles, but it's named for a Czech. He clashed with the powerful men of his diocese, left Prague more than once, and finally set out as a missionary to the pagan Prussians on the shores of the Baltic Sea. In 997, they killed him. === DEEPER Altars for the Dead | culture
The Day of the Dead isn't a Mexican Halloween. It falls on the first two days of November, the Catholic feasts of All Saints and All Souls, but its roots reach back to the ancient peoples of Mexico, who honored their dead with festivals of their own. On the first of November, families remember the children they've lost, and on the second, the adults.

At the heart of the holiday is the ofrenda, the altar of offerings. A family builds it at home, or at the grave, often in tiers, and covers it with the things the dead loved in life: photographs, their favorite foods and drinks, cigarettes or a bottle of tequila, toys for a child, candles to light the way, and copal incense. Bright orange marigolds, called cempasúchil, are scattered over the altar and sometimes laid in a path to the door, because their color and scent are said to guide the spirits home. There's pan de muerto, a sweet bread decorated with strips of dough shaped like bones, and skulls made of sugar, with a name written across the forehead.

The grinning skeletons of so much Day of the Dead art owe a great deal to one artist. José Guadalupe Posada was a printmaker in Mexico City around 1900, who drew calaveras, skeletons acting out the follies of the living, for cheap broadsheets sold on the street. His most famous figure is a skeleton lady in a grand hat piled with feathers and flowers, mocking Mexicans who put on European airs. Decades later, Diego Rivera painted her into a great mural in Mexico City, strolling through a park beside Posada himself, and he gave her the name she's known by today: La Catrina.

In 2008, the United Nations cultural agency added the Day of the Dead to its list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity. But in Pilsen, it was never a museum piece. In the weeks before November, look in the windows of homes and shops along 18th Street, and you may see altars of all sizes, built by families who are simply keeping their dead close.
=== DEEPER The Mayor in the Park's Name | people
The park around the museum was created in 1912 and named for Carter Harrison, the elder, five times mayor of Chicago. Harrison was a Kentuckian, born in 1825, who came to Chicago in the 1850s and made a fortune in real estate. He was a showman. He rode around the city on a white Kentucky mare, in a broad slouch hat, and he courted the city's immigrant voters, German, Irish, Czech, and Polish alike.

He was mayor from 1879 to 1887, four terms in a row, and he ran what his enemies called a wide-open town, easygoing about saloons and gambling, and beloved in the working-class immigrant neighborhoods like this one. In 1893, he won a fifth term, to be the mayor of the World's Columbian Exposition.

On October twenty-eighth, 1893, two days before the fair was to close, a young man named Patrick Prendergast rang the bell at Harrison's house on Ashland Avenue. Prendergast had backed Harrison's campaign, and he had convinced himself that the mayor owed him a job as the city's chief lawyer, the corporation counsel. When Harrison came into the hall, Prendergast shot him, and the mayor died within the hour. The fair's grand closing ceremony was canceled, and a memorial service took its place.

Prendergast was convicted of murder. Clarence Darrow, then a young lawyer, tried to save him from the gallows by arguing that he was insane, but he failed, and Prendergast was hanged in 1894. He's often said to be the only one of Darrow's clients ever to be executed.

The mayor's son, Carter Harrison Junior, was later elected mayor five times too, so between them, father and son ran the city for about twenty years. The park keeps the family name, and its old park building has held the museum since it opened, with a new wing added in 2001.
=== DEEPER Conchas and Carnitas | food
If you walk back along 18th Street, follow your nose.

In a Mexican bakery, you serve yourself. Pick up a metal tray and a pair of tongs near the door, walk along the cases, choose what you like, and bring it to the counter. The best known of the sweet breads is the concha, a soft, round roll with a crackly sugar topping scored to look like a seashell. There are orejas, ears of flaky pastry; cuernos, horns; and marranitos, little pigs of soft gingerbread, darkened with piloncillo, an unrefined brown sugar. In early January, for the feast of the Three Kings, bakeries sell the rosca de reyes, a ring of sweet bread with a tiny figure of the baby Jesus baked inside. Then there are carnitas. In 1975, Inocencio Carbajal, from the city of Uruapan, in the Mexican state of Michoacán, opened a shop on 18th Street, making carnitas the way he'd learned from his father and uncle: pork simmered slowly in big pots of lard, until it's tender inside and crisp at the edges. Carnitas Uruapan, at 1725 West 18th Street, still sells it by the pound, with tortillas and salsa, and when the day's batch is gone, it's gone. The family, now in its third generation, has opened more places, in Gage Park and in Little Village.

And on a hot day, look for a paletería, selling paletas, Mexican ice pops made with real fruit, in flavors like mango with chile, tamarind, cucumber, and rice pudding. Order one, find a bench in the park, and you'll be doing exactly what Pilsen families have done on summer afternoons for half a century.
