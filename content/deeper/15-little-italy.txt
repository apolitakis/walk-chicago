=== STOP Maxwell Street
=== DEEPER Sunday on Maxwell Street | history
Why was Sunday the big day here? Because the first merchants on Maxwell Street were observant Jews. From sundown on Friday to sundown on Saturday, the Jewish Sabbath, they kept their stalls and shops closed. So the market came roaring back to life on Sunday morning, the one day when most working people in the city had time to shop, and when most of the big stores downtown were closed.

The shopkeepers didn't wait for customers to find them. Many hired pullers, men who stood out on the sidewalk, sized up everyone who passed, and talked them into the store, or tugged them in by the sleeve. Inside, nothing had a fixed price. The merchant named a figure, the customer acted shocked, and the bargaining began. Regulars liked to say that only a stranger paid the first price.

By the 1930s, the crowds on a busy Sunday were estimated at fifty thousand people or more. A young sociologist at the University of Chicago, Louis Wirth, who had come to America as a Jewish boy from a village in Germany, walked these streets while he was writing his 1928 book, The Ghetto. In the market, he saw something very old: a bazaar that the immigrants had carried with them from the towns of Eastern Europe and set down, almost unchanged, in the middle of an American city.

After 1994, the market kept its name but lost its street. It moved first to Canal Street, near the river, and then, in 2008, to Des Plaines Street, a little farther north. By then, many of the vendors and their customers were Mexican and Mexican American, and the smells drifting over the stalls were of grilled meat, tacos, churros, and champurrado, a thick, hot chocolate drink made with corn. The bargaining, though, never stopped.
=== DEEPER And This Is Free | culture
In 1964, a photographer named Mike Shea, who had worked for Life magazine, brought a movie camera down to Maxwell Street, and he kept coming back, Sunday after Sunday, sixteen times in all. What he made from that footage is a short documentary, in black and white, called And This Is Free. It has no narrator. The camera simply drifts through the crowd, among the shoppers and the hustlers, the preachers and the families out for the day, and lets you watch and listen.

Nearly everywhere the camera goes, there's music. Robert Nighthawk, one of the great slide guitarists to come up from the Delta, plays on a corner with a small band behind him. Johnny Young plays the mandolin, a rare sound in the blues. A young harmonica player named Carey Bell is there, and so is Big John Wrencher, a fixture of the market, who had lost his left arm in a car crash a few years before and played the harmonica with the hand he had left.

Shea had help from a young guitarist from the North Side, Mike Bloomfield, who knew many of the musicians and came along on those Sundays to win their trust. A year later, Bloomfield was standing beside Bob Dylan on the night Dylan went electric at the Newport Folk Festival. The sound was recorded by Gordon Quinn, who went on to help found Kartemquin Films, the Chicago documentary studio behind Hoop Dreams, and the editing was done with help from Howard Alk, one of the founders of The Second City.

Nighthawk died in 1967, and the film holds some of the only footage of him playing. For years it was hard to see, but it has since been released on disc, along with more music from the street.

If you want to know what this corner looked and sounded like on a Sunday morning in its prime, the film is the closest you can come.
=== DEEPER The Depression Sandwich | food
The Maxwell Street Polish has a rival for the title of this corner's greatest gift to Chicago. In 1929, a man named Abe Drexler, whom everyone called Fluky, opened a hot dog stand at Maxwell and Halsted. That same year, the stock market crashed, and as hard times settled over the city, Drexler came up with a bargain. For a nickel, he'd sell you a hot frankfurter on a bun, loaded with mustard, relish, onion, pickle, peppers, lettuce, and tomato. People called it the Depression sandwich, because it was practically a meal, and it cost next to nothing.

Fluky's, which later left Maxwell Street for other parts of the city and the suburbs, has long claimed that this was the birth of the Chicago-style hot dog, with everything on it. Food historians are more careful. Bruce Kraig, who has written widely about the hot dog, has pointed out that there's no record from the time to prove the story. By one account, a newspaper used the same name, Depression sandwich, in 1931, for something else entirely: ham and cheese.

What nobody disputes is where the frankfurter itself came from. In 1893, two brothers-in-law from Austria-Hungary, Emil Reichel and Sam Ladany, sold their all-beef sausages to the crowds at the World's Columbian Exposition. The next year, by the company's account, they opened a shop on Halsted Street, and their business, Vienna Beef, went on to supply hot dog stands all over the city. At first they delivered by horse and wagon, and their fleet didn't switch to trucks until 1928.

Today, look for the company's name on the signs of hot dog stands across Chicago. For many Chicagoans, it's a promise that what's inside is the real thing.

=== DEEPER Bloody Maxwell | history
In 1906, the Chicago Tribune called the Maxwell Street district the wickedest police district in the world. That may have been newspaper exaggeration, but not by much. The officers who worked out of this station patrolled some of the most crowded blocks in America, where tens of thousands of immigrants, Jewish, Italian, Irish, Greek, and Bohemian, lived packed into wooden cottages and rear tenements, many of them desperately poor.

Most of the district's crimes were the crimes of poverty and crowding: theft, fights, gambling, and the robbing of workingmen on payday. But it had its own particular terrors, too. In the Italian blocks to the north, extortionists sent letters signed with the mark of a black hand, demanding money from shopkeepers and families. Those who refused risked having their homes or shops bombed. The victims rarely came to the police, because they didn't trust them, or because they feared what would happen if they did.

Nearby was the territory of the Valley Gang, an Irish gang of thieves and brawlers. During Prohibition, its leaders, Terry Druggan and Frankie Lake, went into beer, running breweries in partnership with Johnny Torrio and Al Capone. They're remembered for one of the strangest stories of the era. Jailed in 1924 for defying a court order against their brewing, they are said to have come and gone from the county jail almost as they pleased, after spreading money among the jailers. When the newspapers found out, the story goes, the sheriff himself was sentenced to jail.

The station was built to stand for order in the middle of all that disorder, and it did, for a long time. Chicago police officers worked out of it for one hundred and ten years, until 1998, when the district moved to a new building.
=== DEEPER Television's Station House | culture
Hill Street Blues first went on the air in January 1981, and it didn't look like any police show before it. Its creators, Steven Bochco and Michael Kozoll, wanted it to feel real, and the director of its first episode, Robert Butler, borrowed the look of a real documentary: The Police Tapes, a 1977 film about a police precinct in the South Bronx, with its handheld cameras and its morning roll call.

On Hill Street, the camera jostled through crowded rooms, and several conversations overlapped at once. Stories ran on from one week to the next, and some never quite ended. Most episodes opened at roll call, where Sergeant Phil Esterhaus ran through the day's business and sent the officers out with the words that became a national catchphrase: let's be careful out there.

The show never said which city it was in. Most of it was filmed in Los Angeles, but its opening credits and some of its street scenes were shot here, in Chicago, and the gritty, wintry, nameless city on the screen looked a lot like this one.

At first, almost nobody watched. By one count, the show finished eighty-seventh out of ninety-six shows in its first season, which made it one of the lowest-rated dramas ever brought back for a second. But the critics loved it, and that fall it won eight Emmy Awards, a record for a drama series in a single year that stood until The West Wing won nine in 2000. It ran for seven seasons and changed the way television told stories.

Then the building found a second career. When another police drama, Chicago P.D., began in 2014, its producers chose this doorway as the entrance to their own fictional station, the Twenty-first District. The rooms you see inside are a set on a soundstage on the West Side. So the officers you see walking through this door on screen are actors, while the real ones, the university's, work inside.
=== DEEPER The Champion From Maxwell Street | people
Of all the boys who grew up in this police district, the most famous in his own day was a prizefighter. He was born Dov-Ber Rasofsky in New York in 1909, and his family moved to Chicago when he was small. His father, a devout scholar of the Talmud, ran a little grocery near Maxwell Street and hoped his son would follow him into a life of study. Then, when the boy was in his early teens, robbers came into the store and shot his father dead.

The family fell apart. His mother suffered a breakdown, and the younger children were sent to an orphanage. The boy ran wild in the streets, and by his own account, he ran errands for gangsters, among them Al Capone. Then he found boxing. He fought under a new name, Barney Ross, so his mother wouldn't find out, and he saved his purses to bring his family back together.

In 1933, he beat Tony Canzoneri for the lightweight championship of the world, and the next year, he took the welterweight title from Jimmy McLarnin. He went on to hold world titles in three weight classes, and in a decade of rising hatred of Jews, at home and abroad, Jewish fans across the country cheered him as their own.

When the United States entered the Second World War, Ross, by then past thirty, joined the Marines. On Guadalcanal, late in 1942, he and several wounded comrades were cut off overnight under enemy fire, and Ross, wounded himself, held off the enemy until morning. He was awarded the Silver Star. But the morphine he was given for his wounds and for malaria left him addicted, and the habit nearly destroyed him. In 1946, he checked himself into a federal hospital, and he came out clean. He told his story in a book, and Hollywood turned it into a film, Monkey on My Back.

He spent his last years in Chicago, and he died here in 1967.

=== DEEPER The Missionary | people
Arnold Damen was born in 1815 in a village in the south of the Netherlands. As a young man, he heard a Belgian Jesuit, Pierre-Jean De Smet, who had come back to Europe from the American frontier to recruit young men for the missions among the Native nations of the West. In 1837, Damen sailed with him to America.

He never made it to the frontier. The Jesuits trained him in Missouri, ordained him a priest, and put him to work in Saint Louis, where he became a popular pastor and preacher. In 1857, the bishop of Chicago invited the Jesuits to open a parish in his city, and Damen was the one they sent. His new parish covered something like fifty square miles, most of it prairie.

But Damen's real fame came from the road. With a small band of fellow Jesuits, he traveled from city to city giving parish missions, a kind of Catholic revival that could last a week or more, with sermons every day, crowds that packed the churches to the doors, and long lines outside the confessionals. His voice, people said, could fill the largest church without strain, and his sermons on sin, death, and judgment brought whole congregations to tears. By one count, made in 1879, he had personally led more than two hundred missions in his twenty-two years in Chicago.

At home, he built. Besides the church and the college next door, he opened schools for thousands of the parish's children, taught largely by religious sisters. And when the Great Fire left about a hundred thousand people homeless, his parish, untouched by the flames, helped feed and shelter the victims.

In his last years, he left the running of the parish to others and kept on preaching. He was in Omaha, at the Jesuit college there, when he died, on New Year's Day, 1890, at the age of seventy-four.
=== DEEPER Chicago's Oldest Windows | look
Holy Family was dedicated on August twenty-sixth, 1860, with thirteen bishops in attendance, a sign of how much the young Catholic Church in America expected of it. Its style is Victorian Gothic, and it's the only Gothic church left in Chicago from before the Civil War. The plans came from an architectural firm called Dillenburg and Zucker, and the interior was completed by John Van Osdel, who is often called the city's first professional architect.

If the church is open, step inside and look up. High along the walls of the nave, above the arches, run the clerestory windows, which let light pour down into the church from above. They were made in 1860 by a stained glass maker named W. H. Carse, and they're believed to be the oldest stained glass in Chicago. They have survived the fire, a century and a half of coal smoke and city grime, and the decades when the church nearly fell apart.

Then look toward the front of the church, at the tall high altar, with its forest of Gothic spires, and at the carved figures of saints and angels. Many of the church's statues were carved by Anthony Buscher, a Chicago woodcarver who worked on the church in its early years.

Next door, the school's building, designed by Toussaint Menard, went up in 1869, with a western addition five years later. Its style is French Second Empire, which was the height of fashion in the years after the Civil War, and you can see it in the steep roofline, the dormer windows, and the stacked rows of tall windows. It's a Chicago Landmark. Inside, the school still keeps some of its grand nineteenth-century rooms, including a great gallery on the fourth floor, where a marble plaque honors the school's graduates who served in the nation's wars.

Step back to the sidewalk, and look at the two buildings side by side, the church and the school, a pair of survivors from the city that burned.
=== DEEPER The Altar Boys | legends
Look high above the sanctuary, to either side, and you'll find two small statues of boys dressed as altar servers. They come with one of the best-loved stories in Catholic Chicago.

One stormy night, the story goes, two boys knocked at the door of the priests' house and asked for Father Damen. Their mother was dying, they said, and she needed a priest. Damen took his holy oils and followed them out into the dark, across the muddy prairie, to a poor cottage at the edge of the parish. Inside, he found a woman close to death, and he heard her confession and gave her the last rites. When he told her that her sons had brought him, she stared at him. Her only two sons, she said, had drowned some time before.

Damen, the story continues, had statues carved in memory of the boys and set them up in the church, where you see them now. And people say that their eyes follow the priest as he moves around the altar.

Is any of it true? There's no account of the story from Damen's own lifetime, and historians treat it as folklore. Similar tales, of a priest led through the night by a mysterious messenger to someone who is dying, are told in other places too.

The statues, though, are real, and they're old. They're attributed to Anthony Buscher, the woodcarver who made many of the church's figures in its early years. And the story has lasted for generations, passed down by parishioners to their children, and retold in books of Chicago ghost stories.

When you look up at the two boys, notice how they seem to be watching the altar. Then see whether you can catch them watching you.
=== STOP Taylor Street & Little Italy
=== DEEPER Mother Cabrini | people
Of all the people who worked among the Italians of Chicago, one became a saint. Frances Xavier Cabrini was born in 1850 in Lombardy, in northern Italy, the youngest of thirteen children, so small and frail that few expected her to live long. As a girl, she dreamed of being a missionary in China. In 1880, she founded her own order, the Missionary Sisters of the Sacred Heart, and she asked the pope to send her to the East. Pope Leo the Thirteenth, the story goes, told her instead: not to the East, but to the West.

So in 1889, she sailed for New York, to serve the Italian immigrants who were arriving by the hundreds of thousands, many of them to lives of grinding poverty, in an American Church run mostly by Irish and German clergy. For the next twenty-eight years, she hardly stopped moving. She crossed the Atlantic again and again, and her sisters opened schools, orphanages, and hospitals from New York to Buenos Aires.

She came to Chicago more than once. In 1905, she opened Columbus Hospital, on the North Side, facing Lincoln Park. A few years later, she bought land a few blocks from here, at the corner of Lytle Street and Gilpin Place, and opened a second hospital, for the Italians of the Near West Side. It was later renamed for her, and so was the little street beside it, today's Cabrini Street.

She died in Chicago, at Columbus Hospital, on December twenty-second, 1917, at the age of sixty-seven. She had become an American citizen in 1909, and so, when the Church canonized her in 1946, she became the first citizen of the United States to be made a saint. A few years later, she was named the patron saint of immigrants.

And when the housing authority built a project on the Near North Side in 1942, in what was then an Italian neighborhood, it was named for her: the Frances Cabrini Homes, the beginning of what became Cabrini-Green.
=== DEEPER The Beef Debate | food
Ask who invented the Italian beef sandwich, and you may start an argument on Taylor Street. Everyone agrees on the outline. In the early twentieth century, Italian families on the Near West Side celebrated weddings on very little money. Some were called peanut weddings, because the guests were treated to peanuts, beer, and sandwiches. To feed a big crowd from one roast, the cooks sliced the beef as thin as they could, simmered it in a seasoned broth, and piled it onto Italian bread, so a little meat went a long way.

After that, the stories part ways. The family behind Al's traces the sandwich to Anthony Ferreri, who, they say, was making it for weddings and feasts in the 1920s, before his son Al and other family members opened their stand. Another family, the Scalas, says that its founder, Pasquale Scala, a butcher and sausage maker, was making it in the 1920s too, and the Scala company now supplies beef to many stands around the city. Nobody wrote it down at the time, and the truth, like the gravy, is hard to pin down.

Ordering one takes a little vocabulary. A beef can be dry, with the meat lifted out of the juice and drained; wet, with extra juice spooned on top; or dipped, with the whole sandwich plunged into the gravy until it's soaked through. Then come the toppings: sweet, for roasted green peppers, or hot, for giardiniera, a spicy relish of chopped vegetables, pickled and packed in oil. Ask for a combo, and you'll get a grilled Italian sausage tucked into the same bun. Regulars eat it standing up, leaning over the counter with their elbows out, so the juice drips on the counter and not on their shoes, a posture some Chicagoans call the Italian stance.

In 2022, a television series, The Bear, carried the Italian beef far beyond Chicago. Its story is set in a fictional family sandwich shop, and a real one, Mr. Beef, on Orleans Street in River North, stands in for it on screen.
=== DEEPER The Empty Pedestal | politics
A few blocks west of here, and a little to the north, on Loomis Street, is a small park called Arrigo Park, with a tall pedestal that has stood empty since 2020. For more than half a century, it held a bronze statue of Christopher Columbus, by Moses Ezekiel, an American sculptor who lived and worked in Rome. He finished it in 1892, and it was shown in the Italian pavilion at the World's Columbian Exposition the next year. Then, for decades, it stood in a niche above the entrance of the Columbus Memorial Building, on State Street.

After that building was torn down in 1959, a state legislator from this neighborhood, Victor Arrigo, led a campaign to bring the statue here. It was dedicated on Columbus Day, October twelfth, 1966, and after Arrigo died, in 1973, the park was renamed for him.

For many Italian Americans, the statue stood for pride, in a neighborhood that had lost so much. For others, Columbus stood for conquest, and for the suffering of the Native peoples of the Americas. In July 2020, during a summer of protests across the country, demonstrators tried to pull down the Columbus statue in Grant Park, and there were clashes with the police. A week later, before dawn, crews acting on Mayor Lori Lightfoot's orders took this statue down too.

The Joint Civic Committee of Italian Americans went to court, arguing that a 1973 agreement bound the Park District to keep the statue on display. In 2025, the two sides settled. The statue is to be lent to the committee and shown indoors, in a museum of Italian American history that, by one report, will be named for Florence Scala. And the park is to get a new monument. In February 2026, after a public call that drew more than one hundred and fifty nominations, the Park District announced its choice: Mother Frances Xavier Cabrini, the nun who spent her life serving Italian immigrants.

Until the new monument is built, the pedestal waits.
=== STOP The Jane Addams Homes
=== DEEPER Elizabeth Wood's Fight | politics
When the Chicago Housing Authority was created in 1937, it chose as its first executive secretary a thirty-eight-year-old woman named Elizabeth Wood. She had been born in Japan, the daughter of American missionaries, and for seventeen years, she ran the authority. She believed, against much of the city, that public housing should be built wherever there was land for it, including in white neighborhoods, and that its tenants should not be sorted by race.

In 1949, after Congress passed a major new housing act, Wood's staff proposed sites for thousands of new apartments, many of them on vacant land in white parts of the city. The aldermen, who under a new state law had to approve every site, threw most of them out and substituted their own, in neighborhoods that were already Black and poor.

Then came Trumbull Park. In 1953, a Black family, Betty and Donald Howard, moved into the Trumbull Park Homes, an all-white project on the Far South Side, apparently because an official took Betty Howard, who was light-skinned, to be white. For months, mobs surrounded the project, throwing stones and setting off firecrackers and small bombs, and the family had to be escorted in and out by the police. Wood refused to back down, and more Black families moved in.

In 1954, the authority's board pushed her out. Civil rights groups asked whether she had been punished for her stand on integration, and many historians believe she was.

Her fight was taken up in court. In 1966, tenants led by Dorothy Gautreaux sued the authority, with Alexander Polikoff, a lawyer working with the American Civil Liberties Union. In 1969, a federal judge, Richard Austin, ruled that the authority had chosen its sites and its tenants by race, and ordered that new public housing be small and scattered across the city. From 1987 on, for more than twenty years, a court-appointed receiver ran that program.
=== DEEPER Animal Court | look
The animals were meant to be played on. Edgar Miller designed them for a courtyard with a fountain at the heart of the project, and he gave them simple, rounded, heavy forms, in the streamlined style of the 1930s, the kind of shapes that seem made for small hands and feet. There were seven pieces in all, carved from blocks of limestone, among them a bear, a bull, a buffalo, a lion, and sheep.

=== DEEPER A Museum of Home | culture
The National Public Housing Museum opened its doors here on April fourth, 2025, and the idea for it came from people who had lived in public housing themselves. When the rest of the Jane Addams Homes were being torn down, a longtime tenant leader, Deverra Beverly, rallied residents and their allies to save this one building, and to make it a place where the story of public housing would be told by the people who knew it from the inside.

The Jane Addams Homes were one of four neighboring projects on the Near West Side, which came to be known together as Abla, a name made from their initials: the Jane Addams Homes, the Robert Brooks Homes, Loomis Courts, and the Grace Abbott Homes. Together, they were home to thousands of families.

The heart of the museum is three apartments, re-created room by room, each one the home of a real family that lived here. The first belonged to the Turovitz family, a Jewish family who moved in when the project opened, in 1938. The second recalls an Italian American family in the 1950s. The third belonged to the Hatch family, a Black family who lived here in the 1960s and early 1970s. The rooms were furnished with the help of the families' own memories.

Walk through them in order, and you can watch the story of public housing change. For the families who moved in early on, an apartment here often meant the first home they'd ever had with heat, hot water, and a bathroom of their own. The museum doesn't hide what went wrong in the decades that followed, but it insists on something the usual story leaves out: that for millions of Americans, the projects were, first of all, home.
=== STOP Cook County Hospital
=== DEEPER The Bank of Blood | history
Bernard Fantus was born in Budapest in 1874 and went to school in Vienna before he came to Chicago, where he trained as a doctor and became a professor of therapeutics, the study of medicines and treatments. He was a practical man with a soft spot for his youngest patients. Years before the blood bank, he wrote a book on how to hide bitter medicines in candy, so that sick children would take them.

Before the 1930s, a blood transfusion was an emergency all its own. A donor had to be found, tested, and brought to the patient's bedside, often in the middle of the night, and the blood went from one person to the other almost at once. Fantus had read reports from the Soviet Union of blood being collected and stored for later use, and he set out to do the same here. Mixed with a chemical to keep it from clotting, and kept cold, donated blood, he found, could be saved for up to ten days.

He opened his laboratory on March fifteenth, 1937. At first he called it a blood preservation laboratory, but he soon gave it a plainer name, and the name explained how it worked. Like a bank, it took deposits and made withdrawals. A patient who received blood was expected to have family or friends give blood to replace it, so that the bank would never run dry. In its first year, it supplied more than thirteen hundred transfusions.

Fantus described the bank in the Journal of the American Medical Association that same year, and within a few years, hospitals across the country and around the world were opening blood banks of their own. He didn't live long enough to see much of it. He died in 1940, and the county named the hospital's outpatient clinic, the Fantus Clinic, in his honor.
=== DEEPER A Palace for the Poor | architecture
The building was designed by Paul Gerhardt, the county's architect, and when it opened in 1914, it was meant to say something: that the sick poor of Cook County deserved a hospital as dignified as any bank or courthouse in the city. Its style is Beaux-Arts, the formal classical manner taught at the great architecture school in Paris, and its eight stories are dressed in columns, carved ornament, and a grand central entrance.

In 1993, while it was still a working hospital, the building had a starring role in a movie. In The Fugitive, Harrison Ford plays a doctor on the run, who sneaks in disguised as a janitor to search the records of the prosthetics department for the one-armed man who killed his wife.

After the county moved its patients out, it planned to tear the old building down. Preservationists fought back, and Preservation Chicago put it on its list of the most endangered buildings in the city in 2003, and again in 2004. Eventually, the building was named a Chicago Landmark and listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and the county chose a private team to restore it.

The restoration took five years, from 2015 to 2020, and cost about one hundred and forty million dollars, with the help of historic tax credits. About eighteen million dollars of it went to the facade alone, which was cleaned and repaired piece by piece. Inside, the old hospital wards became two hotels, with more than two hundred rooms between them, along with a food hall on the ground floor and offices for doctors of the county health system.

Look up at the facade as you pass, and imagine it as it was for most of the twentieth century: dark with soot, with ambulances pulling up at the doors, and patients waiting inside on long wooden benches, sometimes for hours, to see a doctor.
=== DEEPER The Doctors Next Door | people
The old county hospital was the center of a whole neighborhood of medicine, and its oldest neighbor is Rush Medical College. Rush was chartered on March second, 1837, two days before the city of Chicago itself, by a young doctor from New York named Daniel Brainard. He named it for Benjamin Rush, the Philadelphia physician who signed the Declaration of Independence. Its building on the North Side burned in the Great Fire, and a few years later, the college moved here, beside the county hospital, where its students could learn at the bedsides of the city's poor.

Next door to Rush stood Presbyterian Hospital, and it was there that one of Rush's professors, James Herrick, made a famous discovery. In 1904, a young dental student from the Caribbean island of Grenada, Walter Clement Noel, came to the hospital with anemia. When Herrick's intern, Ernest Irons, looked at the young man's blood under a microscope, he saw red blood cells stretched into thin, curved shapes, like sickles. Herrick's paper about the case, published in 1910, was the first description in Western medicine of what we now call sickle cell disease. Two years later, Herrick published another landmark paper, one of the first to describe a heart attack in a living patient and tie it to a blocked coronary artery.

Rush and Presbyterian eventually joined forces, along with Saint Luke's Hospital, and today the medical center's campus is crowned by a tower that, seen from above, is shaped like a butterfly. It opened in 2012, a few blocks east of here, near Ashland Avenue.

And the modern county hospital that replaced this one bears the name of John H. Stroger Junior, who in 1994 became the first Black president of the Cook County Board.
