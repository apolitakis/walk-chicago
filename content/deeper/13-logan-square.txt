=== DEEPER A Ring of Parks | history
When the legislature drew up the park plan in 1869, Chicago was a city of about three hundred thousand people with only a few parks, most of them small. Its motto, adopted in 1837, was Urbs in Horto, Latin for city in a garden, but the garden was mostly a hope. Doctors believed that crowded streets bred disease and that open air could cure it, and real estate men noticed that land beside a park sold for more than land beside a railroad yard.

Instead of one park board, the state created three, one for each of the sides of the city that the river divides, and each with its own commissioners and its own taxes. That's why the system was never quite a single design. On the West Side, the commissioners hired William Le Baron Jenney, and his plan of 1871 started right about here. A boulevard would run south from this corner of the city past three new parks, Humboldt, Central, and Douglas, and then turn east toward the South Side. Central Park was renamed Garfield Park in 1881, after President Garfield was assassinated.

Then the city burned, in October 1871, and the financial panic of 1873 followed two years later. Money dried up, and the boulevards were built in pieces over the next two decades, many of them across open prairie where almost no one lived yet. That was partly the point. The commissioners were betting that if they built the boulevards, the houses would follow, and around Palmer Square, eventually, they did.

The separate boards lasted for more than sixty years, joined over time by nineteen smaller park districts formed in outlying neighborhoods. Park jobs were political plums, as you heard on the Division Street tour, and by one account the West Park Commission alone was some twenty million dollars in debt by the depths of the Depression. In 1934, all twenty-two districts were merged into the single Chicago Park District that runs the parks today.

=== DEEPER The Other Palmer | names
So who was the Palmer this square honors? John McAuley Palmer was born in Kentucky in 1817 and came to Illinois with his family as a teenager. He became a lawyer, and in the Civil War he rose to major general.

In 1865, the army put him in command in Kentucky. It was a slave state that had stayed in the Union, so the Emancipation Proclamation had never applied there, and slavery was still legal. Palmer set out to end it anyway. He issued passes that let enslaved people leave their owners and travel to find paid work, and white Kentuckians called them Palmer passes. Slaveholders were furious, and a Kentucky grand jury indicted the general for helping an enslaved woman escape.

As governor, he was a fierce defender of the rights of the states, even against his own party. After the Great Fire, when Chicago's mayor called in federal troops under General Philip Sheridan to keep order, Palmer protested that the city had no right to do it.

He drifted from the Republicans to the Democrats, and the legislature sent him to the United States Senate in 1891. Then, in 1896, came the strangest race of his life. When the Democrats nominated William Jennings Bryan, who wanted to back the dollar with silver as well as gold, the party's gold men bolted and nominated Palmer for president, with a former Confederate general, Simon Bolivar Buckner, as his running mate: two old soldiers from opposite sides of the war, on one ticket. They won about one percent of the vote, and many of their backers quietly hoped the Republican, William McKinley, would win.

When it was laid out, this whole route, from here north past Logan Square and east along Logan Boulevard, was called Humboldt Boulevard. The stretch north of here now carries the name of John Kedzie, a real estate developer and outspoken critic of slavery who helped organize the Republican Party in Illinois. Potter Palmer, by the way, has a park of his own, far to the south, at 111th Street.

=== DEEPER The Stone From the Sea | nature
Put your hand on the front of a greystone, and you're touching the floor of an ancient sea.

About three hundred and forty million years ago, a warm, shallow sea covered what's now southern Indiana, which then lay in the tropics. Its floor was alive with small creatures that built skeletons of lime: moss animals called bryozoans, which grew in lacy colonies; sea lilies called crinoids, starfish relatives that waved on long, jointed stalks; and single-celled creatures no bigger than a grain of sand. Their broken remains piled up in sandy shoals and were cemented, over time, into the rock that geologists call the Salem Limestone, and that everyone else calls Indiana limestone.

Because the grains are small and even, the stone has no direction to it, the way wood does. Stonecutters call that a freestone: you can saw or carve it any way you like, and it won't split along hidden layers. By the 1880s, quarries around Bedford and Bloomington were cutting it from the hills in enormous blocks with steam-powered machines and sending it north by rail, about two hundred and fifty miles to Chicago.

Look closely at a weathered stone front, and you may see what it's made of: tiny specks and ovals, and now and then a little ring or a short stack of disks, which are pieces of crinoid stalk. Notice the color, too. Some fronts are a warm buff, and others a cool gray. The difference is iron. Stone that lay above the water table, where the iron in it slowly rusted, turned buff, and stone from deeper down stayed gray.

And step around to the side of a greystone if you can. The stone is usually only on the front, with plain brick on the sides and back, because the builders spent their money where the street could see it.

Quarry families cut stone for generations. If you've seen the 1979 movie Breaking Away, set in Bloomington, you've met their descendants: the local kids the college students look down on are called cutters, after the stonecutters.

=== DEEPER Little Norway | history
Norwegians reached Chicago in the 1830s, when it was still a muddy little town, and for most of the next century, the city was the capital of Norwegian America. Many newcomers passed through on their way to farms in Illinois, Wisconsin, and Minnesota, but many stayed, working as sailors on the lake boats, as carpenters, and as builders. They settled first near the river, close to downtown, and then moved steadily northwest, up Milwaukee Avenue, to Wicker Park and Humboldt Park, and finally here.

By 1900, there were more than forty-one thousand Norwegians in Chicago. By a common reckoning, only two cities in the world had more: Kristiania, which is now called Oslo, and Bergen.

They also had one of the most remarkable newspapers in America. Skandinaven was founded in Chicago in 1866 by John Anderson, a Norwegian-born printer, and a few partners. It grew into the largest Norwegian-language newspaper in the world, and no paper in Norway itself came close. It was read in Norwegian homes all over the Midwest.

One of Norwegian Chicago's proudest moments came in 1893. For the World's Fair, a Norwegian sea captain named Magnus Andersen had a copy built of the Gokstad ship, a real Viking vessel that had been dug out of a burial mound in southern Norway in 1880. To prove that such a ship could really cross an ocean, Andersen and a crew of eleven sailed it from Bergen across the Atlantic and on through the Great Lakes, reaching Chicago in July, to cheering crowds. The ship still survives, at Good Templar Park in Geneva, Illinois, west of the city, where volunteers are working to preserve it.

The church in front of you has a quieter history. It was built as Christ Norwegian Lutheran Church, and its first congregation lost the building in the Depression. In 1934, a new congregation bought it, reopened it, and gave it the name it carries now.

=== DEEPER Knute Rockne | people
The most famous boy to grow up in Norwegian Logan Square was Knute Rockne, who became the most celebrated football coach in America.

He was born in 1888 in Voss, in western Norway. His father, Lars, a blacksmith and carriage maker, came to Chicago in 1891 to show a carriage he had built at the coming World's Fair, and the family followed. Knute was about five. He grew up around here, went to Brentano School and then to North West Division High School, later renamed Tuley, played football and ran track, and left without a diploma.

For about four years he worked as a mail dispatcher at the Chicago post office, and in 1910, at twenty-two, he went to Notre Dame, where two friends had urged him to join them. He was older than his classmates, and he studied pharmacy.

On November first, 1913, Notre Dame played Army at West Point. Rockne, an end, and his quarterback stunned the Army team with a barrage of forward passes, and Notre Dame won, thirty-five to thirteen. The forward pass had been legal for years, but that game made it famous.

Rockne stayed on to teach chemistry and help coach, and from 1918 until his death, he was the head coach. His teams won one hundred and five games, lost twelve, and tied five, one of the best records in the history of college football. Then there's the Gipper. George Gipp, his greatest player, died of an infection in December 1920. Eight years later, at halftime against Army, Rockne told his team that Gipp, on his deathbed, had asked that some day the boys win one for him. Notre Dame won. The only source for that request was Rockne himself.

He died on March thirty-first, 1931, when a passenger plane carrying him crashed in Kansas after a wooden wing came apart, and the outcry helped push airlines toward all-metal planes. In 1940, Hollywood made his life into a movie, Knute Rockne, All American, with a young Ronald Reagan as the Gipper. Reagan carried the nickname the rest of his life.

=== DEEPER The Monument's Makers | culture
Three people made the column in front of you: a lumber dealer who never saw it, the architect of the Lincoln Memorial, and a sculptor who had learned her trade in Chicago.

The lumber dealer was Benjamin Ferguson. When he died in 1905, he left about a million dollars to the trustees of the Art Institute, to pay for lasting statues and monuments in Chicago's parks and boulevards, honoring worthy Americans and important events in the nation's history. The first work it paid for was Lorado Taft's Fountain of the Great Lakes, beside the Art Institute, in 1913, and the fund still looks after this column today.

For the state's hundredth birthday in 1918, the fund helped pay for two monuments. One was a smaller, gilded copy of the Republic, the giant statue from the World's Fair of 1893, by Daniel Chester French, which still stands in Jackson Park. The other was this column. Henry Bacon designed the Republic's pedestal and this column while he was building the Lincoln Memorial in Washington, where French made the great seated Lincoln.

The sculptor was Evelyn Longman. Born in Ohio in 1874, she paid for her training at the Art Institute in Chicago with money she had saved working in an office. In 1901 she went to New York, where French took her on as an assistant. Her first task was the lettering on a set of bronze doors he was making for the Boston Public Library, a job he disliked.

She soon outgrew it. In 1906 she beat more than thirty other sculptors in a competition to design bronze doors for the chapel of the Naval Academy at Annapolis. Later she made a gilded winged giant called the Genius of Telegraphy, which stood for decades on top of the headquarters of the American Telephone and Telegraph Company in Manhattan. New Yorkers called it Golden Boy, and it now stands in Dallas. In 1919, the year after this column was finished, she was elected a full member of the National Academy of Design, often described as the first woman sculptor so honored.

=== DEEPER Reading the Column | look
Start at the top. The eagle is about ten feet tall and weighs something like eight tons, and it's carved from marble, like the column, though the first plan called for bronze. It perches on a rock, looking out over the Northwest Side.

Now look at the column itself. It's in the Doric style, the plainest and sturdiest of the classical orders, with a simple, cushion-like capital at the top and shallow vertical grooves, called flutes, running up the shaft. It's the same order Henry Bacon chose for the ring of columns around the Lincoln Memorial, which he was building at the same time. This column is made of marble from Tennessee, and it's said to be built of thirteen solid drums, stacked one on another.

Then walk around the base, which is carved with Evelyn Longman's reliefs. The story starts on the south side, with a woman seated on a throne, who stands for Illinois. Around her, in procession, come the figures who made the state, as the artists of 1918 saw it. Descriptions of the carvings name a Native American figure called Hiawatha, after the hero of Longfellow's poem, a frontiersman in the mold of Daniel Boone, and Ceres and Mercury, the Roman gods of the harvest and of trade.

That's a very 1918 way to tell the story, and it has drawn criticism. After the protests of 2020, Chicago set up a committee to review its public monuments, and in 2021 it published a list of forty-one works to reconsider. This column was on it. The committee said it was one of many works that use images of Native Americans to illustrate the pageant of the region's history without recognizing their violent removal from it.

The committee leaned toward adding to monuments rather than taking them down, with new public art and new lessons for schools. The column stands where it always has, and it's still the neighborhood's gathering point, where people meet under the eagle for markets, festivals, celebrations, and protests.

=== DEEPER Walt Disney's Chicago | people
Walt Disney's father, Elias, was a carpenter and contractor who built houses in this part of the city, and he also built a church. Saint Paul Congregational Church was dedicated on October fourteenth, 1900. Elias sometimes preached when the minister was away, and Flora served as treasurer. The minister was Walter Parr, a close friend, and when the Disneys' fourth son was born the next year, they named him Walter. The church still stands, on Keeler Avenue, a block or so from the house where Walt was born, and it still serves the neighborhood, now as a Spanish-speaking Baptist congregation.

Why did they leave? The family story is that Elias was frightened by crime. Two teenage boys from a family nearby were caught up in a robbery at a streetcar barn, and he decided the city was no place to raise his children. He sold the house on Tripp Avenue in February 1906, and that spring, the Disneys were farming near Marceline, where his brother already owned land.

They came back in 1917, after Elias invested in O-Zell, a Chicago company that made jelly and fruit juice. Walt enrolled at McKinley High School, on the Near West Side, and drew cartoons for its paper, the Voice. In the evenings he studied drawing at the Chicago Academy of Fine Arts, a school often confused with the Art Institute. He also worked at the jelly plant.

In September 1918, after the Army turned him away as too young, he joined the Red Cross ambulance corps by claiming to be older than he was. By the time he reached France, the war was over. He drove ambulances, decorated their canvas sides with cartoons, and kept sending drawings home to the Voice. He came home in 1919 but soon left for Kansas City to make his way as an artist, and O-Zell failed, leaving his father's stock worthless.

Two Los Angeles designers, Brent Young and Dina Benadon, bought the birthplace on Tripp Avenue in 2013 and restored it, and in October 2023 it opened for public tours for the first time.

=== DEEPER Logan Square at the Table | food
Logan Square's fame as a place to eat began, many people would say, with a small café on the boulevard. Lula Café opened in 1999, on Kedzie Boulevard just south of the square, when this was still a mostly Latino, working-class neighborhood. Its cooks built the menu around what they could get from Midwestern farms and changed it with the seasons, and it became a pioneer of Chicago's farm-to-table movement, and a magnet for the artists and young people who were beginning to move here. A quarter century later, it's still going.

Eleven years after Lula, Longman and Eagle opened on Kedzie Avenue, just north of the square. It's a tavern, with a long whiskey list and a menu of rich, inventive food, and upstairs there are six small rooms for overnight guests. Its name is said to come from the monument in the square: the eagle on top, and Evelyn Longman, the sculptor who carved the reliefs. When the Michelin guide arrived in Chicago, it gave this neighborhood tavern a star, and Longman and Eagle kept it for seven years, before moving to the guide's list of restaurants that offer good food at moderate prices.

On Logan Boulevard, the chef Diana Dávila runs Mi Tocaya, which serves antojos, the snacks and small plates of Mexican markets and street stalls, and it, too, has been recognized by Michelin. Its signature cocktail is called the Chicana, with hibiscus and mezcal.

Those restaurants put Logan Square on the city's food map, and they brought in visitors from all over Chicago and beyond. But the neighborhood's older food is worth seeking out, too. Along Milwaukee Avenue and the side streets, you can still find Mexican taquerias and bakeries with trays of sweet bread, Puerto Rican and Cuban counters, and a few reminders of the Polish neighborhood farther up the avenue. Some have been here for decades, and rising rents have made each of them a little harder to keep open. If you're hungry, the best way to help keep the old places alive is simple: eat there.

=== DEEPER Who Gets to Stay | politics
In 2000, about two of every three people in Logan Square were Latino. By 2020, it was closer to one in three. Over the same years, the white population grew by more than two thirds, household incomes rose sharply, and white residents came to outnumber Latinos. For a neighborhood that had been a center of Latino Chicago for a generation, it was a startling change, and it happened within twenty years.

Why? Rents and prices climbed as the neighborhood became fashionable, and many longtime renters couldn't keep up. Landlords sold, new owners renovated, and some two-flats and three-flats, the buildings that once let working families buy a home and pay the mortgage with the rent from upstairs, were turned into single-family houses. Researchers still argue about how much each of these mattered. One analysis found that smaller households, with more singles and couples and fewer children, explain more of the neighborhood's falling population than conversions do. You could see it in the schools, as neighborhood elementary schools lost students and fought to stay open.

The most visible fight was over a city-owned parking lot on Emmett Street, across from the Logan Square Blue Line station. Neighborhood groups and the local alderman, Carlos Ramirez-Rosa, pushed for an apartment building there that would be entirely affordable, so that people of modest means could live next to the train. Some nearby property owners fought it, and in 2020 a group of them sued. The city sold the lot to a nonprofit developer for one dollar and put in ten million dollars of public financing.

In May 2022, after eight years of planning, argument, and litigation, the building opened: seven stories, one hundred apartments, half of them for public housing tenants. Almost seven hundred people applied for the other fifty. It has since been named for Lucy Gonzalez Parsons, the labor radical, and it carries a mural honoring the neighborhood's Latino community.

One building can't reverse a trend. But it was a statement about what this neighborhood should be, and who it should be for.

=== DEEPER Black Jack Logan | people
John Alexander Logan, the general this neighborhood honors, was one of the most complicated men in Illinois history. He was born in 1826 in southern Illinois, the region called Little Egypt. He served in the Mexican War, became a lawyer, and went into politics as a Democrat. In 1853, as a young state legislator, he pushed through one of the harshest laws in the North, barring free Black people from settling in Illinois. A Black newcomer who stayed more than ten days could be fined fifty dollars, and one who couldn't pay could be auctioned off to work for whoever paid it.

When the Civil War came, his district was full of Southern sympathizers, and for a while his loyalty was in doubt. He chose the Union. He is said to have fought as a volunteer at Bull Run while still a congressman, and then he went home and raised a regiment. His dark hair and complexion earned him the nickname Black Jack, and he's often called the best of the political generals, who rose through politics, not West Point.

On July twenty-second, 1864, outside Atlanta, the commander of the Army of the Tennessee was killed, and Logan took over in the middle of the battle. But General Sherman gave the permanent command to a West Point man, and Logan never quite forgave it. He finally got the army in May 1865, in time to lead it through Washington in the Grand Review.

After the war, he returned to Congress as a Radical Republican, of the wing that fought hardest for the freed slaves, and in 1868 he helped prosecute President Andrew Johnson at his impeachment trial. He went on to the Senate, and in 1884 he ran for vice president with James G. Blaine. They lost narrowly.

Logan died the day after Christmas in 1886, and his coffin lay in the Capitol, on the bier that had held Abraham Lincoln's. The man behind the Black Law ended his career on the side of the people it was written to keep out. Conscience or politics? You can decide.

=== DEEPER The Courtyard Building | architecture
The big U-shaped apartment buildings along the boulevard, wrapped around a garden, are worth a closer look, because they solved a very Chicago problem.

Most Chicago lots are long and narrow, typically twenty-five feet wide and a hundred and twenty-five feet deep, with an alley behind. That shape suits a two-flat. But crowd a big apartment building onto a row of those lots, and the apartments in the middle are left dark and stuffy. The courtyard building, which first appeared in the late 1880s and came into its own in the 1910s and 1920s, bends the building into a horseshoe around a garden that opens to the street.

Notice the doors. Instead of a single lobby and long, dark hallways, there are several entrances around the court, each opening onto its own stairway, and each stairway usually serves just two apartments on each floor. So nearly every apartment has windows on at least two sides, and in the days before air conditioning, a breeze could pass straight through. At the back, open wooden porches and stairs climbed to every kitchen door, for deliveries of ice and coal and for carrying out the trash, and those back porches became a Chicago institution of their own.

The builders were competing with the single-family house, which in Chicago was cheap enough that many families could buy one. So the courtyard building was designed to feel like a home, not a tenement. The garden served as a shared front yard. Many apartments had a sunroom across the front and a dining room with a built-in china cabinet. Builders gave the buildings names, carved over the doors, and often put them near parks, boulevards, and elevated stations, so tenants had greenery outside and a quick ride downtown.

The courtyard building's heyday ended with the Depression, when building stopped, and Chicago's zoning code of 1957 effectively ruled out new ones. So nearly every courtyard building you see in the city comes from a window of about forty years. Look for the name over the entrance, and the year, if the builder carved one.

=== DEEPER Elms, Ash, and What Came Next | nature
Picture this boulevard a century ago, and you have to imagine a different kind of tree. The pleasure drives were lined with American elms, and when the trees were grown, their high, arching branches met overhead, so a carriage rolled down a green tunnel, like the nave of a cathedral. Towns all over America planted elms that way, because they grew fast, tolerated soot and poor soil, and left room for traffic underneath.

Then came Dutch elm disease. It's named for the Dutch scientists who identified it, not for where it came from. It's a fungus, carried by bark beetles, that clogs the vessels carrying water up the trunk until the crown wilts and dies. It reached America around 1930, probably in logs from Europe, and spread across the Midwest after the Second World War. In the 1970s and 1980s, as it swept through Chicago, the city cut down elm after elm along streets like this one.

The tree most often planted in their place was ash, which grew fast and seemed indestructible. Chicago ended up with tens of thousands of ash trees on its parkways. Then, in 2006, a small, shiny green beetle from Asia called the emerald ash borer turned up in Illinois. Its larvae tunnel under the bark, and an infested ash usually dies within a few years. The city fought back with the largest treatment program of its kind in the country, injecting about sixty-six thousand parkway ash trees with insecticide in 2013 and 2014. But in 2018 it stopped, and many of those trees have since come down.

The lesson foresters took is variety. A boulevard of a single kind of tree is beautiful, and it's also a feast for a single pest. Today the city mixes species, so the next disease can't take a whole street at once. Among them you may spot young elms again, of varieties that resist the disease, some developed at the Morton Arboretum, in the western suburbs. As you walk, look up and count how many kinds of trees you can find on one block.
