=== STOP Saint Stanislaus Kostka & Polish Downtown
=== DEEPER For Bread | history
For most of the nineteenth century, there was no Poland on the map. At the end of the 1700s, Prussia, Russia, and Austria had divided the old Polish kingdom among themselves, and for more than a century, Poles lived as subjects of three foreign empires. They kept their language, their Catholic faith, and the memory of a lost country, and they rose up more than once, most famously in 1830 and in 1863. Each time, the rebellion was crushed.

But the great migration to America wasn't mainly about politics. Polish historians call it the emigration for bread. Most of the people who came were peasants, from villages where families had too many children and too little land, and where a farmhand earned a small fraction of what a laborer could make in an American factory.

They came in waves, roughly in the order that the three empires let them go. The first large groups came from the Prussian part of Poland, often as whole families who meant to stay. Later, in the decades before the First World War, came the Poles of the Russian and Austrian lands, many of them young men who planned to work a few years, save their wages, and go home to buy land. Some did go home. Far more stayed.

In America they took the hardest and lowest-paid work there was, and Chicago had plenty of it. Polish neighborhoods grew up wherever the jobs were: here on the Northwest Side, near the tanneries and factories along the river, behind the stockyards, and beside the steel mills of the far South Side.

Altogether, by one estimate, some two and a half million Poles came to the United States between 1850 and 1920, among them smaller groups with identities of their own, like the Kashubes of the Baltic coast and the highlanders of the Tatra Mountains. Wherever they settled, the center of their new lives was usually a Polish parish like this one, where the priest, the language, and the saints on the walls were familiar.
=== DEEPER Father Barzynski | people
The priest who built this parish into a giant was a veteran of a lost revolution. Vincent Barzynski was born in 1838 in the Russian part of Poland, and he was ordained a priest in 1861. Two years later, Poland rose against the tsar in what became known as the January Uprising, and Barzynski worked for the rebels' secret national government, getting supplies to the insurgents. When the uprising was crushed, he fled, first to Austrian territory and then to France.

In Paris, in 1866, he joined the Congregation of the Resurrection, a young religious order founded by Polish exiles, and the order sent him to America, to minister to the Polish settlers of Texas. After several years there, he came north to Chicago, and in 1874 he was made pastor of Saint Stanislaus Kostka.

He was a builder of almost frightening energy, and he thought big. In 1873 he was one of the founders of the Polish Roman Catholic Union, a fraternal society that offered its members life insurance and a sense of belonging, and that became one of the pillars of Polish American life. His order came to run much of Polish Catholic life on the Northwest Side, and in 1898, when it organized an American province, he became its first head.

Barzynski had a clear idea of what Polish America should be: Catholic first, organized around the parish, and obedient to the church. That put him on a collision course with the nationalists, who wanted Polish life organized around the cause of a free Poland, and with parishioners who wanted a say in running their own churches. The long war with Holy Trinity, which you heard about at the stop, was one result.

He died in May 1899, at the age of sixty, and his funeral was one of the largest Chicago had ever seen. The order he served went on running this parish for generations after him.
=== DEEPER Never Come Morning | culture
Nelson Algren wasn't Polish, but he spent a great deal of time in this neighborhood, in its taverns and at its boxing matches, listening to the way people talked, and in 1942 he turned what he heard into Never Come Morning. Its hero, Bruno Bicek, is a teenage tough who dreams of becoming a prizefighter, caught up in a world of gangs, pimps, and crooked cops, and the book follows him to a killing and an arrest he seems to have expected all along.

The novel came with an introduction by Richard Wright, the author of Native Son, who had become Algren's friend in the radical writers' circles of 1930s Chicago. Ernest Hemingway praised it in a letter to his editor.

Many Polish Americans saw something else: a slander. Here was a portrait of their community as a world of thugs and prostitutes, published at the very moment when Poland itself was being crushed under Nazi occupation. Polish American organizations, among them the Polish Roman Catholic Union, protested, and some critics went so far as to call the book pro-Nazi. They persuaded Mayor Edward Kelly to have it pulled from the shelves of the Chicago Public Library.

Algren's defenders answered that the book was about poverty, not about Poles, and that the same story could have been told about any people trapped at the bottom of a big city. Algren himself kept writing about these streets for the rest of his career, and his relationship with Chicago stayed prickly to the end.

The hurt took decades to fade. But time has a way of settling these arguments, and at the next stop, in the heart of the neighborhood that once wanted his book banned, you'll find his words carved into a fountain.
=== DEEPER The Museum Up the Street | look
The Polish Museum of America was started by the Polish Roman Catholic Union, and it still shares a building with the union on Milwaukee Avenue. It's worth the detour for a few remarkable things.

Start with the Paderewski Room. Ignacy Jan Paderewski was the most famous pianist of his age, and in 1919 he served as prime minister of a newly independent Poland. The room holds one of the largest collections of his belongings anywhere, among them his practice piano, the gold pen he used to sign the Treaty of Versailles, and his death mask, made by the American sculptor Malvina Hoffman.

Then look for the great stained glass window called Poland Reborn. It was made for Poland's pavilion at the New York World's Fair, which opened in the spring of 1939. That September, Germany invaded Poland, and the pavilion's treasures no longer had a country to go home to. Some of them found a home here instead.

And find the big painting of Casimir Pulaski at Savannah, by the Polish painter Stanisław Batowski. Pulaski was the Polish cavalry officer who fought for American independence and died of the wounds he received at the siege of Savannah, in 1779. The painting won first prize when it was shown at Chicago's Century of Progress fair in 1933, and the Polish Women's Alliance bought it for the museum in 1939. Illinois still honors Pulaski with a state holiday, on the first Monday of March.

The museum also keeps a library and archives that Polish American families, scholars, and genealogists have used for decades, full of newspapers, letters, and records from the neighborhoods you're walking through. It keeps limited hours, so check before you go.
=== STOP The Polish Triangle
=== DEEPER Faith or Nation | politics
For half a century, Polish Chicago argued with itself about a basic question: what did it mean to be Polish in America? Was it, first of all, to be Catholic? Or was it to work for the rebirth of Poland?

Two great organizations stood for the two answers. The Polish Roman Catholic Union, which Father Barzynski helped found in 1873, spoke for the church: its members were Catholics, and its leaders were often priests. The Polish National Alliance, founded in 1880 by Polish exiles in Philadelphia and Chicago, spoke for the nation. It welcomed people whose roots lay in the lands of the old Polish Commonwealth, whatever their religion, and its great cause was an independent Poland. Both were fraternal societies, part club and part insurance company, and both grew into some of the largest ethnic organizations in America.

In Chicago, the fight was waged parish by parish. Saint Stanislaus lined up with the Catholic Union, and its rival, Holy Trinity, with the Alliance. Each side had its own newspapers, its own heroes, and its own celebrations, and their quarrels filled the pages of the Polish press for years.

Barzynski fought the Alliance with everything he had, and by one account it only began to grow quickly after his death in 1899.

Over time, the two camps learned to work together. The First World War, and the real chance it offered of a free Poland, gave them a common cause. And in 1944, they joined with other Polish American groups in the Polish American Congress, to press Washington on Poland's fate as the Second World War came to an end.

Both organizations are still headquartered in Chicago. Their old argument, between faith and nation, never entirely ended, but it softened into something more like a family disagreement, the kind that gets aired every year at the same dinner table.
=== DEEPER Volunteers for Poland | history
When the First World War broke out in 1914, the three empires that had divided Poland went to war with each other, Germany and Austria on one side and Russia on the other. Poles were drafted into all three armies, and sometimes they fought each other. For Polish Americans, the war brought terrible news from home, and something they had waited for all their lives: a real chance that Poland might be reborn.

The pianist Ignacy Jan Paderewski became the voice of that hope in America. He crossed the country giving concerts and speeches to raise money for Polish war relief, and he lobbied President Woodrow Wilson. In January 1918, when Wilson laid out his Fourteen Points for the peace, one of them called for an independent Poland with access to the sea.

Paderewski also called for a Polish army of volunteers from America. The Polish Falcons, a network of gymnastic clubs that had long drilled young Polish men, became its recruiting machine, along with the Polish National Alliance. Many of the volunteers were immigrants who weren't American citizens, and so weren't subject to the American draft. They trained at a camp in Canada, at Niagara-on-the-Lake in Ontario, and then sailed for France, where a Polish army was taking shape under French command. Its men wore blue French uniforms, and in the last weeks of the war, General Józef Haller took command of it. People called it the Blue Army, or Haller's Army.

Some thirty-eight thousand men volunteered, and more than twenty thousand were accepted and shipped overseas. By one estimate, about three thousand of them came from Chicago. One photograph from December 1918 shows a couple of hundred volunteers gathered in Humboldt Park, about to leave for the camp.

After the war, the Blue Army went on to Poland and fought in the wars over the new country's borders. About twelve and a half thousand of the volunteers eventually came back to the United States, and many found that service in a foreign army gave them no claim to the benefits that American veterans received.
=== DEEPER Pierogi and Pączki | food
Polish Chicago ate well, and it still does. The old Polish delis and bakeries followed their customers northwest along Milwaukee Avenue long ago, but the food is easy to find, and a few dishes are worth knowing.

Start with pierogi, the half-moon dumplings that every Polish grandmother seems to make better than anyone else's. The classic filling is potato and farmer's cheese, often with fried onions. There are also sauerkraut and mushroom, ground meat, sweet cheese, and, in summer, blueberries, served with sour cream and sugar.

Then there's kielbasa, which simply means sausage in Polish. A good Polish deli might carry a dozen kinds: smoked and fresh, garlicky and mild, thin and dried for snacking. Along with it come rye bread, dill pickles, and soups, among them a clear red beet broth served at Christmas, and a sour rye soup that's an Easter favorite.

The holidays bring out the best. On Christmas Eve, Polish families wait for the first star to appear before sitting down to Wigilia, pronounced roughly vee-geel-ya, a meatless feast of many dishes, traditionally twelve. Before eating, everyone shares a thin wafer, breaking off pieces for one another and exchanging wishes, and an extra place is set for an unexpected guest. On the Saturday before Easter, families carry baskets of eggs, sausage, bread, and salt to church to be blessed, a custom you'll still see in Chicago's Polish parishes.

And then there are pączki, pronounced roughly poonch-key: rich, yeast-raised doughnuts, filled with jam, often rose or plum, or with custard. In Poland, the big day for pączki is the last Thursday before Lent. In Chicago, as in Detroit, it has moved to Fat Tuesday, and lines form outside Polish bakeries before dawn.

=== DEEPER Simone and Nelson | people
In February 1947, Simone de Beauvoir came to Chicago. She was already famous in France, as a novelist, as a philosopher, and as the companion of Jean-Paul Sartre, and she was crossing America on a lecture tour. A friend had given her the phone number of a Chicago writer. She called Nelson Algren, and he showed her his Chicago: the dives, the flophouses, and, by her own account, a police lineup.

Within a few months, they were lovers. She came back to stay with him for long stretches in his cramped flat on Wabansia Avenue, a short way from here, and the two of them traveled together as far as Mexico and Central America.

In her letters she called herself his wife. He gave her an inexpensive ring, which she wore for the rest of her life, and she is said to have been buried wearing it.

But she would not give up Paris, her work, or Sartre, and Algren would not move to France. The affair went on for years, much of it by mail. More than three hundred of her letters to him survive, and they were published after both of them had died.

She put him into her novel The Mandarins, as the Chicago writer Lewis Brogan, and dedicated the book to him. It won France's most famous literary prize, the Goncourt, in 1954. Later, she wrote about their affair in her memoirs. Algren, a private man, felt by most accounts that she had exposed him, and he let his anger show in print.

Algren left Chicago in 1975, feeling that the city had never appreciated him, and moved east. In 1981 he was elected to the American Academy and Institute of Arts and Letters, a late honor, and that May he died at his new home in Sag Harbor, on Long Island. De Beauvoir died five years later, in Paris.
=== DEEPER The Golden Arm on Screen | culture
The Man with the Golden Arm made Algren famous, and Hollywood came calling. The actor John Garfield bought the film rights first, and after he died in 1952, the director Otto Preminger picked them up.

Algren was brought in to work on the script, but Preminger soon replaced him with a Hollywood screenwriter and changed the story a great deal. In the novel, Frankie Machine, the card dealer with the golden arm, ends up hanging himself in a flophouse. In the film, he kicks his habit and walks off into the night with the woman who loves him. Algren was bitter, and he went to court over the film's credits, though he later dropped the case.

The film, which opened at the end of 1955, starred Frank Sinatra as Frankie, and it earned him an Academy Award nomination. Its jazz score, by Elmer Bernstein, and its opening titles, by the designer Saul Bass, with a jagged white arm cutting across a black screen, became classics in their own right.

Its biggest impact was on censorship. Hollywood's Production Code, the industry's rules of decency, banned stories about drug addiction, and the censors refused the film their seal of approval. Preminger and his distributor, United Artists, released it anyway. The film was a hit, and in December 1956, the Code was rewritten to allow movies to deal with narcotics, within limits. The rules that had governed American movies since the 1930s were beginning to crack, and in 1968 they gave way to the ratings system.

For Algren, though, the whole experience confirmed his suspicion of Hollywood. He had written about real people on real streets, not far from here, and the movie was shot on studio sets, with a happy ending. He never wrote another book with the success of the one Hollywood had bought.
=== DEEPER Reading the Mansions | architecture
In the decades after the Great Fire, this was one of the most fashionable addresses on the Northwest Side. The people who built here wanted everyone to know they had arrived, and they used every style in the book. With a little practice, you can tell the styles apart.

The oldest are often Italianate: tall, narrow windows topped with heavy hoods, and a deep cornice under the roofline, held up by brackets. Next, look for a mansard roof, steep and almost vertical, with dormer windows poking out of it. That's the Second Empire style, borrowed from Paris in the age of Napoleon the Third, and it let builders squeeze a full extra floor under the roof.

Then come the showoffs. Queen Anne houses love asymmetry: a round or many-sided tower on one corner, a porch wrapping around another, bay windows, and a different texture on every floor, from brick and stone to wooden shingles. Romanesque houses go the other way, toward weight and power: rough-cut stone, deep round arches over the doors and windows, and walls that look as if they could withstand a siege. That style was made popular in the 1880s by the Boston architect Henry Hobson Richardson.

Many houses mix several styles at once, which tells you something about the people who built them. They were self-made men, many of them immigrants, and they wanted a little of everything.

As the neighborhood declined, many of these mansions were carved up into apartments and rooming houses, and some were lost. Then, from the 1970s on, new owners began buying them and restoring them, often one room at a time. The neighborhood was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1979, and in 1991 the city made the heart of it a landmark district, which means that the facades you see can't be torn down or altered without review.
=== STOP Wicker Park & the Six Corners
=== DEEPER Robey, Hoyne, and the Wicker Brothers | names
The Wicker brothers, Charles and Joel, came to Chicago around 1839 and went into the wholesale grocery business, and Charles also went into politics, serving on the City Council. The park, as you heard at the start of the tour, was their gift. In 1870 they had bought about eighty acres along Milwaukee Avenue and divided the land into lots, and the four acres in the middle that they gave the city were probably as shrewd as they were generous, since a park next door made the lots around it easier to sell.

The street that runs past the six corners, Damen Avenue, had a different name until 1927. It was Robey Street, named for a local politician, James Robey. That year, over the protests of many residents, the City Council renamed it for Father Arnold Damen, the Jesuit who founded Holy Family Church and the college that became Loyola University. The old name hasn't quite disappeared: the hotel in the Art Deco tower above the corner is called the Robey.

Hoyne Avenue, where the beer barons built their mansions, carries the name of the man at the center of one of the strangest episodes in Chicago politics. In April 1876, a lawyer named Thomas Hoyne was elected mayor as a write-in candidate, with about thirty-three thousand of roughly forty thousand votes. But the sitting mayor, Harvey Colvin, refused to step aside, arguing that the election had been illegal. For about four weeks, Chicago had two men who each claimed to be its mayor. That June, a court threw out Hoyne's election, the city ended up paying both men for the weeks in dispute, and a special election that summer chose someone else entirely.

So the three names around you tell three Chicago stories: a real estate deal, a fight over a street sign, and a mayor who never legally was.
=== DEEPER The Howling Coyote | culture
In the 1980s, Wicker Park was cheap, a little dangerous, and full of empty space, which is exactly what artists look for. Painters, sculptors, and photographers moved into lofts and storefronts around the six corners, and small galleries opened in buildings that landlords could hardly rent.

One of them, the Tower Coyote Gallery, opened in the Northwest Tower. The usual story is that the artists thought the building looked like a coyote throwing back its head to howl. Whether or not you can see it, the name stuck, and the tower has been the Coyote Building ever since.

In 1989, the neighborhood's artists launched a festival of their own, called Around the Coyote. For a few days each fall, studios and galleries opened their doors, and crowds came to look, to buy, and to party. Its home base was the Flat Iron Building, the one you heard about at the stop.

The festival did what such festivals do. It showed the rest of Chicago that Wicker Park was interesting, and the rest of Chicago came. Many of the artists who had given the place its character were soon priced out, and they moved on, to Pilsen, to Logan Square, and beyond. Around the Coyote itself eventually faded away.

If you look up at the tower today, try squinting at its outline against the sky. Some people see the coyote right away. Others never do. Either way, the name is a small monument to the artists who were here first, and who could no longer afford to stay.
=== DEEPER Chicago's Indie Labels | companies
The music you heard about at the stop didn't just happen in clubs. In the 1990s, Chicago was home to a cluster of small, stubborn record labels that shaped independent music around the world.

The oldest was Touch and Go. It started in 1979 in East Lansing, Michigan, as a homemade punk fanzine, and became a record label a couple of years later. Its owner, Corey Rusk, moved it to Chicago in the 1980s. Touch and Go was known for doing business on a handshake, without long contracts, and for splitting profits evenly with its bands. Its roster included Big Black, the Jesus Lizard, and Slint, whose 1991 album Spiderland is still treated as a landmark.

Drag City was founded in Chicago at the end of the 1980s by Dan Koretzky and Dan Osborn, and it became known for singular songwriters like Will Oldham, Bill Callahan, and Joanna Newsom. Thrill Jockey was started in New York in 1992 by Bettina Richards, a former major-label talent scout, and she moved it to Chicago a few years later. It became the home of Tortoise, the Chicago band that helped define the sound critics called post-rock.

And then there was Steve Albini, who grew up in Montana, came to the Chicago area for college, and led the abrasive band Big Black. As a recording engineer, he refused to call himself a producer and refused to take royalties, charging bands a flat fee instead. He recorded a staggering number of albums, among them the Pixies' Surfer Rosa and Nirvana's In Utero, and in the 1990s he built his own studio, Electrical Audio, on the Northwest Side. When he died in 2024, musicians around the world mourned him.

What tied them together was an attitude: that you could make records your own way, on a small scale, and still reach listeners everywhere. For a while, that attitude made Chicago one of the capitals of the music world.
=== STOP The 606
=== DEEPER Raising the Tracks | history
By the 1890s, Chicago was the railroad capital of America, and it paid a terrible price for it. Railroads crossed the city's streets at grade in many hundreds of places, and trains ran right through crowded neighborhoods, past schools, stores, and homes. People were killed and maimed at the crossings with grim regularity.

In May 1892, with the World's Fair coming, the City Council ordered the Illinois Central to raise its tracks on the South Side, near the fairgrounds. Early the next year, the city set out to get rid of grade crossings everywhere. Railroad by railroad, ordinance by ordinance, the tracks went up onto embankments of earth held in by walls, with bridges over each street. By around 1920, by one count, more than five hundred miles of track had been raised, and nearly a thousand street crossings had been eliminated. The Depression finally brought the program to a halt.

The Bloomingdale Line's turn came in the 1910s, as you heard at the stop, and if you look at the trail's edges, you can still read the engineering. The path runs along the top of the old earthen fill, held in by the railroad's concrete retaining walls. And at each cross street, look down from the bridge: for decades, the dark underpasses below were the kind of place people hurried through.

That's the quiet irony of this trail. All over the city, raised railroad embankments became walls between neighborhoods, splitting them into separate worlds. Here, one of those walls has been turned into a path that ties them together.
=== DEEPER From Weeds to Park | politics
The overgrown embankment didn't turn itself into a park. It took years of organizing.

In 2003, a group of neighbors formed the Friends of the Bloomingdale Trail. The next year, the idea of a trail made it into a plan for adding open space to Logan Square, a neighborhood short on parkland. A national nonprofit, the Trust for Public Land, became the city's lead private partner, bringing together agencies, donors, and designers. Public design workshops began in 2007, and it took eight more years, and a great deal of money, to get the trail built.

The rising prices you heard about at the stop were steepest along the trail's western half. By one study, from DePaul University, the value of small buildings near that part of the trail more than quadrupled between 2012 and 2018, far faster than in the city as a whole.

Developers bought old two-flats and small apartment buildings, tore them down, and replaced them with expensive new homes. The city pushed back. In 2020 it temporarily froze demolition permits in parts of the area, and in 2021 the City Council passed a special fee on demolitions along the trail and in Pilsen, of at least fifteen thousand dollars per building, and more for larger ones. The city later reported that demolitions near the trail had dropped sharply.

The argument goes on, here and in cities around the world that have copied the idea: how do you build a beautiful park without pushing out the people it was meant for?
=== DEEPER Gardens in the Air | nature
The 606 is a park as well as a path, and it was planted with care. The landscape architects, Michael Van Valkenburgh Associates, working with the lead artist Frances Whitehead, designed it as a sequence of garden rooms, each with its own mix of trees, shrubs, and grasses, so that the trail changes as you move along it.

Look for serviceberries, small trees with smooth gray bark. In early spring they're covered with white blossoms, and in early summer they carry dark berries that birds love, and that people can eat too. These trees are part of an experiment devised with the artist. They were planted at chosen spots along the trail, in its different microclimates, warmer or cooler, sunnier or shadier, and volunteers have recorded when they leaf out, bloom, and fruit, following the methods of a national network that tracks the timing of the seasons. Over the years, records like these can show how a changing climate is shifting spring in Chicago.

At the western end of the trail, near Ridgeway Avenue, there's an observatory of a very old kind. A path climbs a small mound to a steel blade with a notch cut in it, and the notch is aligned to frame the setting sun on the first day of each season, at the solstices and the equinoxes. On those evenings, people often gather on the mound to watch the sun go down through the slot, the way people have marked the turning of the year for thousands of years.

Between the two ends, the trail does quieter work. Its trees and flowers give birds, bees, and butterflies a green corridor across a crowded part of the city, high above the traffic, and on a spring morning, it's one of the best places on the Northwest Side to hear birdsong.
=== STOP Bucktown & Margie's Candies
=== DEEPER A Century of Sundaes | companies
Margie's began in 1921 as the Security Sweet Shop, opened here by a Greek immigrant named Peter George Poulos. Like many Greeks of his generation in Chicago, he found his way into the candy and ice cream business, and he soon handed the shop to his son, George.

George had met a girl named Margie at the shop when they were both young. They married, and in 1933 he renamed the business for her. In the 1940s, while George was away in the military, Margie ran the shop, and from 1954 she ran it on her own. She was behind the counter for decades, until her death in 1995, at the age of eighty.

Her son Peter, a doctor, kept the shop going after her, the third generation of the family to run it. Part of the charm is how little seems to have changed. The booths, the counter, and the candy cases look much as they must have looked to Margie.

The sundaes are the reason to come. The hot fudge usually comes on the side, in its own little pitcher, so you can pour as much as you dare, and the biggest sundaes are meant to be shared.

Margie's has always been a candy shop, too, and you can still take home a box of chocolates. It's one of the last of a kind of place that once stood on neighborhood corners all over Chicago, the sweet shop where families came after church, couples came on dates, and kids spent their allowance.

A hundred years on, it has outlasted the factories, the goats, and most of the old Bucktown, and it's still on the same corner, scooping the same kind of sundae.
=== DEEPER The Beatles' Booth | legends
Every old Chicago institution seems to have a celebrity story, and Margie's has one of the best. Here's how the family tells it.

On August twentieth, 1965, the Beatles played two shows at Comiskey Park, the White Sox ballpark on the South Side. After the second show, the story goes, John, Paul, George, and Ringo came up here with a few young women in tow, slid into a booth, and ordered the Atomic Sundae, a towering six-scoop creation, before heading off to their hotel near O'Hare.

The shop has made the most of it. The Beatles pictures and souvenirs on the walls are there to commemorate the visit, and fans still ask to sit in the booth where the band is said to have sat. One television report even claimed that Mick Jagger, on a later visit, asked for the same booth.

So what's documented? The concerts certainly are. The visit itself rests mostly on the family's memory and on the stories of people who say they were there, and the details shift in the telling. One account even puts the visit a year earlier, in 1964, when the Beatles played a different Chicago arena.

That's how legends like this usually work. Nobody kept a record, because nobody thought they needed one, and the story was told and retold until it became part of the place. Whether or not the Beatles ever sat there, plenty of people have sat in that booth since, hoping they did.
=== DEEPER Western Avenue | names
The busy street at the corner is Western Avenue, and its name is a fossil. From 1851 to 1869, this was the western edge of Chicago. Everything beyond it was outside the city, and the name stayed long after the city had swallowed the prairie on the other side.

Western is generally called the longest continuous street in Chicago, running about twenty-four miles inside the city limits, from Howard Street on the northern border to 119th Street on the far South Side, and it keeps going into the suburbs beyond. Driving its whole length is a tour of the city in itself, through dozens of neighborhoods, past car dealers, factories, churches, and restaurants of every kind. On the far South Side, it's the route of the South Side Irish Parade every March.

About a mile and a half north of here, at Belmont Avenue, Western ran past the most famous amusement park Chicago ever had. Riverview opened in 1904 on about seventy acres along the river, and for more than sixty years it was where Chicago went to scream. It had one of the first roller coasters with cars hanging below the track, an early parachute drop, and a beloved wooden coaster called the Bobs.

Not all of it was innocent. For years, one attraction invited customers to throw balls at a target that dropped a Black man into a tank of water, until protests forced a change in the 1950s.

Riverview closed suddenly after the 1967 season, and the land became a shopping center, a college campus, and a park. Its grand carousel survived. It was sold, and it now turns at an amusement park in Georgia, where it's listed on the National Register of Historic Places.
