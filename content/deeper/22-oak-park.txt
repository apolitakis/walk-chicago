=== STOP Unity Temple
=== DEEPER Thirty-Four Studies | history
You heard that lightning burned the congregation's old wooden church in 1905. It happened on June fourth, during a storm, and the water pressure in the village was too low for the firemen to save the building.

The congregation itself was already more than thirty years old. It began in 1871, when a handful of members of the village's Union Church decided to start a church of their own, and it brought together two groups of religious liberals, Unitarians and Universalists. In 1886, a Universalist named Augusta Chapin became its minister. She was one of the first women ordained as a minister in America, and she drew in new members, among them Anna Wright, the architect's mother.

So when the old church burned, the congregation had an architect close at hand. The minister at the time helped steer the commission to Wright, and the building committee soon learned what that meant. According to the Frank Lloyd Wright Trust, Wright worked through some thirty-four studies before the final drawings were done. The idea at the heart of them was simple: a single square room, lit from above, with everyone gathered close around the pulpit.

Money was the constant worry. Early estimates in the newspapers ran to thirty-five or forty thousand dollars, more than the congregation had in hand, and the final cost was far higher, by one account more than half again the budget. Construction dragged on for years, and the temple wasn't dedicated until September 1909, four years after the fire.

Wright even thought about the way you leave. At the end of a service, the congregation doesn't turn its back on the pulpit and file out the way it came in. The exits are placed beside the pulpit, so people walk forward on their way out, passing the minister as they go.
=== DEEPER The Preacher of All Souls | people
Wright got his religion, and a good deal of his early luck, from his mother's family, the Lloyd Joneses, Welsh Unitarians who came to Wisconsin in 1844. The member of that family who mattered most to his start in Chicago was his uncle, the Reverend Jenkin Lloyd Jones.

Jenkin Lloyd Jones was born in Wales in 1843 and came to America as a baby. He fought for the Union in the Civil War as a young artilleryman, and afterward he became a Unitarian minister. In 1882, he began preaching to a small congregation on Chicago's South Side, and he served it for the rest of his life. He called it All Souls Church, and he meant the name. He wanted a church that welcomed people of any creed, and that worked for its neighborhood as hard as it prayed.

When Wright came to Chicago in 1887, at nineteen, his uncle's world opened the first doors. Jenkin Lloyd Jones had hired the architect Joseph Lyman Silsbee to design a building for All Souls, and Silsbee gave the young man his first job. A year earlier, Silsbee had designed a little shingled chapel for the Lloyd Jones family's own valley in Wisconsin, and the teenage Wright looked after its interior. It's counted as his earliest known work.

All Souls gave Wright a wife, too. He met Catherine Tobin at a party at his uncle's church, and they were married there on June first, 1889.

Uncle and nephew didn't always agree. Around 1900, Wright designed a new home for the congregation, a combined church and community center to be called the Abraham Lincoln Centre. The two men quarreled over the design, and another architect, Dwight Perkins, finished it. It opened in 1905 on Oakwood Boulevard, and the building is still there, used today by Northeastern Illinois University.

Jenkin Lloyd Jones kept crusading to the end. In 1915, at seventy-two, he sailed for Europe on Henry Ford's Peace Ship, a private expedition that hoped to talk the warring nations into ending the First World War. It failed. He died three years later.
=== DEEPER Saving the Concrete | architecture
You heard that Unity Temple was carefully restored. It needed to be. Poured concrete was a bold choice in 1905, and the building paid for that boldness. Water worked its way into the flat roofs and the walls, the surface cracked, and leaks stained the plaster inside. For decades, patch after patch kept the building going, but the repairs it really needed were far beyond what one congregation could pay for.

The turning point came in 2013, when a Chicago charity, the Alphawood Foundation, pledged ten million dollars, on the condition that the rest be raised by others. Work began in the spring of 2015 and took more than two years. The bill came to more than twenty-three million dollars, including one and three-quarter million from the congregation itself.

The architects, Harboe Architects of Chicago, treated the building almost like an archaeological site. Outside, crews repaired the concrete walls, the chimney and the roof slabs, and put on new roofs and drains. Inside, they restored the plaster, the oak trim, the light fixtures, and floors made of magnesite, a hard, cement-like material. Every piece of art glass in the building was taken out, catalogued, crated, and shipped to Judson Studios in California for repair, then brought back and set in place again.

Then there was the question of color. The interior had been repainted over the years, and the restorers experimented on a section of wall until they were confident they had found Wright's original scheme, the soft, warm tones that keep this concrete room from ever feeling gray.

Some of the work you'll never see. The temple now has new heating and cooling equipment, including a geothermal system that draws on the steady temperature of the earth beneath it.

The building reopened in the summer of 2017, the year of the one hundred fiftieth anniversary of Wright's birth. And it isn't only a museum. The congregation still worships here every week, as it has for more than a century.
=== STOP Hemingway's Birthplace
=== DEEPER Grace | people
You heard that Hemingway's mother, Grace, had trained for an opera career. She had real talent, and for a few years, a real chance.

Grace Hall's family lived on this street. Across the road from the Hall house lived the Hemingways, Anson and Adelaide, with their six children. Their oldest son, Clarence, was a year older than Grace, and the two knew each other, casually, in high school.

In the fall of 1895, Grace went to New York to study voice with Luisa Cappiani, a well-known opera coach. In 1896, she made her debut as a contralto with the Apollo Club, in Madison Square Garden. By family tradition, she was offered a contract with the Metropolitan Opera and turned it down, because the glare of the stage lights hurt her eyes. Whatever the reason, she came home that year and married the boy from across the street.

She never stopped working. Grace taught voice to a long list of pupils, directed the children's choir and an orchestra at the First Congregational Church, and for years she earned more money than her husband, the doctor. She also had decided ideas about raising children. For a time she dressed Ernest and his older sister, Marcelline, alike, as if they were twins, and by family accounts, she held Marcelline back a year so the two could go through school in the same class.

Late in life, she found a second art. She took up painting, taught it alongside her singing lessons, and by one count, finished some six hundred landscapes. After Clarence's death, she lived on her own for twenty-three years, with help from a trust fund that Ernest and his second wife, Pauline, set up for her. She died in Memphis in 1951, and she's buried at Forest Home Cemetery, in Forest Park, next door.

Her son rarely had a kind word for her. Many of his biographers have been gentler. They see a gifted, ambitious woman whose own career was cut short, and who may have passed more of that ambition to her son than he ever cared to admit.
=== DEEPER Overdrawn | culture
You heard that Hemingway came home from the war in January 1919. What happened next between him and his parents is one of the most famous family quarrels in American letters, and he later told a version of it himself.

He spent much of the next year and a half recovering, fishing, and writing stories that magazines sent back. His parents grew impatient. The break came in the summer of 1920, at the family's cottage on Walloon Lake in northern Michigan. By most accounts, the last straw was a secret late-night picnic with his younger sisters and some neighbors' children, which scandalized the other families on the lake. Grace told him to leave, and she put her reasons in writing.

Her letter is dated July twenty-fourth, 1920, three days after his twenty-first birthday, and it was handed to him on the twenty-seventh. In it, she compared a mother's love to a bank account. A child draws on it freely, she wrote, but a grown son has to make deposits too, and hers was overdrawn. A copy of the letter survives at the University of Texas.

Five years later, Hemingway published a story called Soldier's Home, in In Our Time, his first book published in America. Its hero, Harold Krebs, comes back from the war to a small town in Oklahoma, long after the parades are over. Nobody wants to hear what really happened, so he finds himself telling lies about the war, and then hating the lies. He sleeps late, plays pool, watches the girls go by, and can't bring himself to want anything. One morning at breakfast, his mother asks whether he loves her. He says no, then has to take it back, and she asks him to kneel and pray with her.

The town isn't Oak Park, and the family isn't the Hemingways, but readers have always seen through the disguise. The books kept the wound open. When The Sun Also Rises came out in 1926, Grace wrote to her son that it was a doubtful honor to have produced one of the filthiest books of the year.
=== DEEPER Tarzan, Incorporated | companies
A few doors south of here, at number 325, stood a house that's gone now. In 1918 and early 1919, it was rented by another Oak Park writer, then far more famous than young Ernest Hemingway: Edgar Rice Burroughs, the creator of Tarzan.

Burroughs lived in Oak Park for most of the 1910s, in four different houses, and his story is one of the great turnarounds in American publishing. He was born in Chicago in 1875 and tried one job after another. He collected bills for an ice company, worked as a railroad policeman, sold things door to door, and managed a department at Sears, Roebuck. By his mid-thirties, by his own reckoning, he was a failure in business, with a wife and young children to support.

Then, as he later told it, he noticed the stories in the pulp magazines and decided he could write stories at least as bad, and get paid for them. His first, about a former Confederate soldier who wakes up on Mars, sold in 1911. In 1912, a pulp magazine called The All-Story printed Tarzan of the Apes, and in 1914 a Chicago publisher, A. C. McClurg, brought it out as a book.

What makes Burroughs a business story is what came next. He learned to sell the same characters again and again: to magazines, to book publishers, to the movies, and later to newspaper comic strips, radio and toy makers. The first Tarzan movie came out in 1918, while he was living here. In February 1919, he moved his family to California, and he bought a ranch in the San Fernando Valley that had belonged to Harrison Gray Otis, the late publisher of the Los Angeles Times. Burroughs named it Tarzana, and the community that grew up there kept the name.

In 1923, he formed a company, Edgar Rice Burroughs, Incorporated, becoming one of the first authors to turn himself into a corporation. It controlled the rights to his characters, and in time it published his books, too. The company still exists, owned by his family, and it's still based in Tarzana.
=== STOP Frank Lloyd Wright Home and Studio
=== DEEPER The Boy Who Invented Lincoln Logs | companies
Of the six children who grew up in this house, the one who left the biggest mark on American childhood was the second son, John Lloyd Wright. You've very likely played with his invention.

Like his older brother, Lloyd, John became an architect, and during the First World War, he went to Tokyo to work for his father on the Imperial Hotel, the huge commission that kept Frank Lloyd Wright busy for years. As the story is usually told, John got his idea there, watching the workmen and the timbers of the hotel's construction: a toy of notched wooden logs that a child could stack into a cabin. One version credits the interlocking beams in the hotel's foundations. Either way, the inspiration came from Japan.

Back in Chicago, John started selling the toy in 1918 through his own small firm, which he called the Red Square Toy Company, probably a nod to his father, whose signature mark was a red square. He received a patent in 1920. And he gave the toy a brilliant name. Lincoln Logs called up the most famous log cabin in American history, and with it a whole national story about humble beginnings.

The toy sold steadily for decades. In 1943, John sold the business to Playskool, by several accounts for a strikingly small sum, just eight hundred dollars. Playskool and later owners kept making the logs, and in 1999 they were inducted into the National Toy Hall of Fame. Sets are still sold today, and generations of children have built their first houses with them.

John went on to a long career as an architect in Southern California. He also wrote a memoir of growing up in this house and working for his father, with the punning title My Father Who Is on Earth.

When you visit the children's playroom upstairs, remember that one of the boys who played under that vaulted ceiling grew up to give millions of other children their first set of building logs.
=== DEEPER The Print Dealer | culture
Japanese woodblock prints were everywhere in this house and studio, and for years they were more than decoration. They were part of how Frank Lloyd Wright paid his bills.

Wright was already a serious collector by 1905. That February, he and Catherine sailed for Japan with two of his clients, Ward and Cecilia Willits, for whom he had designed a house in Highland Park. It was his first trip abroad, and he came home with a great many prints, bought both to keep and to sell.

The next spring, in 1906, the Art Institute of Chicago showed prints by Hiroshige, the great Japanese landscape artist, in what's counted as the first exhibition of his work in the United States. Wright organized it and wrote the catalogue. In 1908, the Art Institute put on a far bigger show of Japanese prints, more than six hundred of them, and Wright designed the installation and lent almost a quarter of the prints himself. Among the collectors who bought from him was Clarence Buckingham, whose collection later went to the museum. In 1912, Wright published a short book, The Japanese Print: An Interpretation.

The trade grew. In 1915 and 1916, he bought prints in Japan as an agent for a Boston collector, William Spaulding, and that collection is now at the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston. But the business had a dark side. A sale of prints to American collectors, worth some sixty thousand dollars, turned into an embarrassment when some of the prints were found to have been recolored, their faded colors touched up to look fresh. Wright blamed a Tokyo dealer, who was later convicted in a Japanese court.

By the late 1920s, Wright was deep in debt, and he put up thousands of prints as collateral. When his bank took possession of Taliesin, it took the prints too.

Still, he never doubted what the prints had given him. He later wrote that if Japanese prints were taken out of his education, he didn't know what direction the whole might have taken.
=== DEEPER After the Studio Closed | history
You heard that Wright left his family and his practice in 1909. The house and studio he walked away from had a long and complicated life afterward, and for a while, you'd hardly have known they were his.

Catherine stayed on with the younger children. The studio closed, and to give the family an income, the property was divided into apartments. Catherine and the children still at home moved into the studio, which was remodeled as living quarters, and the other apartments were rented to tenants. She lived here until 1918. The Wrights were finally divorced in 1922, and they kept the property until 1925.

For a few years, the studio went back to being a place for art. In 1924, a local art league, known today as the Oak Park Art League, made the old studio its first permanent home, and it stayed until 1929. Over the following decades, the buildings passed through a series of owners and were cut up into as many as six apartments. By the early 1970s, they were badly run down, and people in Oak Park worried that they would be lost.

In 1974, the property was bought to save it. The National Trust for Historic Preservation took title the next year, and a newly formed foundation took charge of the house and studio, raising money and starting the restoration.

That restoration raised a hard question: which version of Wright's house should come back? He had remodeled it constantly for twenty years. The answer was 1909, the last year he lived and worked here. That meant stripping out decades of later changes, room by room, and recreating what had been lost. It took thirteen years, from 1974 to 1987.

In 2012, the foundation, by then called the Frank Lloyd Wright Trust, paid back the National Trust and took ownership of the property itself. The tours that start here every day grew out of that rescue.
=== STOP Forest Avenue: the Heurtley and Moore Houses
=== DEEPER Nothing Like Winslow | people
You heard that Nathan Moore wanted something traditional. He was quite specific about what he didn't want.

Moore had moved to Oak Park from Peoria, and by Wright's account, several other architects had been in the running before Moore chose him, in 1894. Wright had just left Adler and Sullivan, and this house became his first independent commission in Oak Park. As Wright remembered it, Moore told him not to give them anything like the house he had done for Winslow.

That was the William Winslow House, about a mile and a half west of here in River Forest, finished in 1894. Today, it's admired as one of the first great houses of Wright's career, with a broad, calm front of fine brick under a low, wide roof. Moore wanted something more familiar.

Winslow himself was no ordinary client. With his brother, he ran Winslow Brothers, one of the leading makers of ornamental iron and bronze in the country, whose metalwork decorated buildings all over Chicago. He was also an amateur printer and publisher. He and Wright set up a hand press in the basement of his new house and printed books together, including a handsome edition of The House Beautiful, an essay by the Unitarian minister William Channing Gannett, with page decorations designed by Wright.

So the young architect had two kinds of clients from the start: adventurous ones like Winslow, who wanted something new, and cautious ones like Moore, who wanted to fit in. Wright served them both. The Winslow House pointed toward the Prairie houses to come, and the Moore House showed that the young architect could give a cautious client exactly what he asked for.

Moore stayed loyal anyway. When the fire of 1922 gutted the upper floors, it was Wright the family called on to rebuild, almost thirty years after Moore had first told him what not to do.
=== DEEPER The Oak Park Strategy | politics
You heard that Oak Park set out deliberately to become an integrated community. It took work, and it was never automatic.

The warning came from next door. In the 1960s and 1970s, the Austin neighborhood of Chicago, just east of Oak Park, went through a fast and bitter change. As Black families began moving in, some real estate agents played on white homeowners' fears to get them to sell quickly and cheaply, a practice known as blockbusting, and in little more than a decade, most of the white residents had left. For a long time, conventional wisdom held that Oak Park would be next.

Oak Park's answer began with the law. Local citizens' groups had been pushing for open housing since 1963, and in 1968, the same spring that Congress passed the national Fair Housing Act, the village board passed a fair housing ordinance of its own. One of the people lobbying for it was a young woman named Roberta Raymond, known as Bobbie. In 1972, building on the research for her master's thesis, she opened the Oak Park Housing Center, now the Oak Park Regional Housing Center.

The center's approach became known as the Oak Park Strategy. It marketed the whole village to people of every race, and it encouraged apartment hunters to make what it called pro-integrative moves, toward blocks and buildings where their own race was less represented, so that no part of the village would tip. Critics have called that a kind of steering in itself, and the argument continues.

The village added other tools. In the late 1970s, it adopted an equity assurance plan, promising homeowners who signed up that if they sold at a loss years later, the village would make up much of the difference. It was meant mainly as reassurance, and by one account, no claim was ever paid. In 1977, Raymond helped start the Oak Park Exchange Congress, a network of integrated communities around the country that traded ideas.

Half a century later, Oak Park is still a racially mixed village, which is exactly what the pessimists of the 1960s said couldn't be done.
=== DEEPER Floating Balconies | look
Before you leave this stretch of Forest Avenue, look for two more Wright houses close by. They show how far, and in how many directions, his ideas traveled in just a few years.

The first is a short way south, on Elizabeth Court, the little street that runs east off Forest Avenue. At number 6 is a small house of stucco and wood on a narrow lot. You heard about the Thomas Gale House, one of the bootleg houses on Chicago Avenue. Thomas Gale was a real estate man, and after his death, his widow, Laura, came back to Wright for a house of her own. It was finished in 1909.

Look at the front. The house doesn't so much sit on its lot as hover over it. Its balconies are stacked one above another, and they reach out past the walls below them with nothing holding them up from underneath, while the rooms are gathered around a fireplace at the center of the house. It's often described as Wright's first use of the cantilever in a private house, and Wright himself is said to have called it the progenitor of Fallingwater, the famous house over a waterfall in Pennsylvania that he designed more than a quarter century later.

The second house is right on Forest Avenue, at number 238, on the east side, as you walk on toward the next stop. It's the Peter Beachy House, and at first it's a puzzle, because it began as somebody else's building. In 1906, Emma Beachy, the daughter of a wealthy Chicago doctor and real estate investor, hired Wright to rebuild an old Gothic cottage on the lot for her family. Wright remade it completely, but he kept part of the cottage's foundation, and that's why the house sits turned sideways, at a right angle to the street.

Between the two, you can see both sides of a working architect: one house a clean and daring start, the other an inventive compromise with what was already there.
=== STOP The Frank Thomas House
=== DEEPER A Home in a Prairie Town | culture
Far more people saw Wright's ideas in a magazine than ever saw a house on this street. In 1901, the year this house was finished, he published two designs in the Ladies' Home Journal, one of the most popular magazines in America.

Its editor, Edward Bok, a Dutch-born journalist who ran the magazine from 1889 to 1919, believed it could help ordinary families live in better homes, and he invited architects to publish designs that readers could actually build. Wright's first appeared in the February 1901 issue, under the title A Home in a Prairie Town. It showed a low house spread out under broad, sheltering roofs, with its main rooms opening into one another around a central fireplace, and windows gathered into long bands. It's often credited as the starting point of what came to be called the Prairie style.

A second design followed that July, under the cheerful title A Small House with Lots of Room in It. And in April 1907, the magazine published a third, A Fireproof House for Five Thousand Dollars: a compact, square house of concrete, which Wright said would stay cooler in summer and warmer in winter. It was his third and last design for the Journal.

The fireproof house was never built exactly as published, but that wasn't really the point. The magazine carried Wright's ideas into homes far from Chicago, where people who would never hire an architect could study his plans, and builders could borrow from them. The fireproof house had an especially long life. Wright, and other architects, adapted it for real clients for years afterward.

This house shows the same thinking, worked out for a real family on a real lot. Frank Thomas's father-in-law, a grain merchant named James Rogers, is said to have commissioned it as a wedding gift for his daughter and her husband. As you look at it, picture the readers of 1901, turning the pages of a women's magazine and finding there the first sketch of a new kind of American house.
=== DEEPER Kettlestrings Grove | names
Before it was Oak Park, this place went through a whole string of names, and the first one belonged to a couple from Yorkshire.

Joseph Kettlestrings was born in 1808 in a village called Newton, in Yorkshire, in the north of England. In 1832, he married Betty Willis, and soon the young couple sailed for America with two small children. In 1835, they settled on the prairie west of Chicago, near a sawmill, and built a small house on what's now Lake Street, just east of Harlem Avenue, at the western edge of today's village. They're remembered as Oak Park's first permanent settlers. In 1837, Joseph bought the land, a hundred and seventy-odd acres, for about two hundred and sixteen dollars. In 1843, with no school nearby for their children, the family moved into Chicago.

The settlement that grew up around their land was known at first as Kettlestrings Grove. Then people started calling it Oak Ridge, for the oak trees that grew on a low, sandy ridge running through it. It was also called Harlem, a name that survives in Harlem Avenue.

The name Oak Park came from the post office. When the community applied for a post office of its own, in the early 1870s, it couldn't use the name Oak Ridge, because another post office in Illinois already had it. So the post office was named Oak Park, and the railroad station soon changed its name to match.

Even with a name, Oak Park wasn't yet a town in its own right. For decades, it was part of Cicero Township, and it was governed by the township along with its neighbors. Oak Park was finally incorporated as a village on January twenty-fifth, 1902.

The village just to the west ran into the same problem a few years later. It, too, had called itself Harlem, until, by one account, the name turned out to belong to another Illinois post office. In 1907, it became Forest Park.
=== DEEPER Wright's Neighbors in the Trade | architecture
Wright gets the credit, but he wasn't the only architect building Prairie houses on these streets, and some of his rivals and followers built far more of Oak Park than he did.

Eben Ezra Roberts, known as E. E. Roberts, was born in Boston in 1866 and came to Chicago as a young man. He worked for Solon Beman, the architect of the company town of Pullman, and in 1893, the same year Wright went out on his own, he opened an office in Oak Park. Roberts was practical and enormously productive. He worked in whatever style his clients wanted, and as tastes changed, that came to mean Prairie houses with low roofs and bands of windows. He's credited with more than two hundred houses in Oak Park alone, along with commercial buildings like Scoville Square, a Prairie-style block on Lake Street downtown. In 1912, he moved his office into Chicago.

The firm of Tallmadge and Watson had a hand in this very house: in the 1920s, they built an addition at the back. The partners were best known for churches, more than thirty of them. Thomas Tallmadge was also a historian of architecture, and he's credited with coining a name for Wright and the young architects around him, in a magazine article in 1908. He called them the Chicago School.

Then there was John Van Bergen, an Oak Park boy, born in 1885, who as a child watched one of Wright's houses go up in his neighborhood. In 1909, he became the last architect Wright hired for the Oak Park studio, and when Wright left, it fell to Van Bergen and Isabel Roberts, a longtime member of the studio staff, to close it down. Van Bergen went on to design many Prairie houses of his own in Oak Park and River Forest.

So when you see a low house with a wide roof on these streets, don't assume it's by Wright. It may well be a Roberts, a Van Bergen, or a Tallmadge and Watson, and part of the pleasure of walking here is learning to tell them apart.

=== DEEPER Mamah | people
You heard that Mamah Cheney had a mind and a career of her own. Her road to Oak Park began on the prairie of Iowa.

She was born in Boone, Iowa, in 1869. By most accounts, her given name was Martha, but all her life she was known as Mamah. She went to the University of Michigan, at a time when few women went to college at all, and graduated in 1892. Later, she worked as a librarian in Port Huron, Michigan. In 1899, she married Edwin Cheney, whom she had known from her university years, and the couple settled in Oak Park. Their son, John, was born in 1902, and their daughter, Martha, in 1905.

After she left with Wright in 1909, she began the work that gave shape to the rest of her life, translating the essays of Ellen Key. A collection called The Morality of Woman came out in Chicago in 1911, billed as an authorized translation, and another, Love and Ethics, followed in 1912, with Wright credited alongside her. In August 1911, the Cheneys were divorced, and from then on, she used her own family name. She was Mamah Borthwick.

For most of the century that followed, she appeared in books mainly as a footnote, the other woman in a scandal, or the victim at the end of the story. That began to change in 2007, when Nancy Horan, a writer who had lived most of her life in Oak Park, published Loving Frank, a novel told in Mamah's own voice and grounded in Horan's research into her life and letters. It was Horan's first novel, and it became a best seller, sending a new generation of readers to look for this house.

The novel is fiction, and it takes the liberties that fiction does. But it helped restore something the scandal had flattened: a woman with her own education, her own convictions and her own work, who chose a hard road with her eyes open.
=== DEEPER Shining Brow | names
You heard that Wright called his Wisconsin house Taliesin. The name came from the poetry of his mother's people.

Wright's mother, Anna Lloyd Jones, was born in Wales. In 1844, when she was a small girl, her family sailed for America, and in time they settled in a green valley near the Wisconsin River, close to Spring Green. Her father, Richard Lloyd Jones, farmed, made hats, and sometimes preached. Several of her brothers and sisters stayed in the valley, and as a boy, Wright spent his summers there, working on his uncles' farms. The family was proud of being Welsh, and Wright later gave Welsh names to buildings he designed in the valley. The house he built in 1907 for his sister Jane and her husband was called Tan-y-deri, Welsh for under the oaks.

Taliesin was the name of a Welsh poet of the sixth century, whose verses survive in a medieval manuscript known as the Book of Taliesin, and who became a figure of legend. In Welsh, the name means shining brow. In one old tale, a baby is found in a leather bag caught in a fish weir, and the man who rescues him is so struck by the child's radiant forehead that he names him Taliesin.

The name suited the site. Wright didn't put his house on the top of the hill. He built it just below the crest, on the hill's brow, wrapping around it. As he later put it in his autobiography, no house should ever be on a hill; it should be of the hill.

There's a sad echo in the name, too, because Taliesin had to rise more than once. After the fire of 1914, Wright rebuilt the burned living quarters within about a year. In April 1925, during a storm, an electrical fire destroyed them again, and again he rebuilt. The Taliesin you can visit today grew from that third version, much enlarged over the years, and it's part of the same World Heritage listing as Unity Temple and the Robie House.
=== DEEPER The Divide on Chicago Avenue | underfoot
On your way here along Chicago Avenue, between Linden Avenue and East Avenue, you may have walked past a small sign on the north side of the street. You've just crossed the Continental Divide.

A raindrop that falls on one side of this line drains toward the Des Plaines River, just west of Oak Park, and from there into the Illinois River, the Mississippi, and the Gulf of Mexico. A raindrop that falls on the other side once ran east, toward Lake Michigan, and out through the Great Lakes and the Saint Lawrence to the Atlantic.

The line follows a ridge so gentle you can barely feel it, and building has flattened much of it. It's an old beach. Toward the end of the last ice age, some twelve to fourteen thousand years ago, melting glaciers filled a great lake that covered the land to the east, the ancestor of Lake Michigan, known as Lake Chicago. Waves and currents built a long arm of sand and gravel at its edge, called today the Oak Park Spit. It runs from Taylor Park, near Ridgeland Avenue, southwest toward Scoville Park, and then west toward downtown.

The ridge shaped the village. Its high, dry ground drew the first settlers, and its sandy soil grew the oaks that gave the place one of its early names, Oak Ridge.

At least five signs like the one on Chicago Avenue trace the divide through Oak Park, including one on Lake Street downtown, one in Scoville Park and one in Taylor Park. The one you passed was put up in 1999 by the local Rotary Club and the Oak Park River Forest Museum.

There's a modern twist. Since 1900, when Chicago reversed its river to carry the city's sewage away from the lake, even the water from the eastern side of the divide here mostly ends up in the Mississippi too. And about five miles south of here, the divide sinks to its lowest point, at the old Chicago Portage, where travelers once carried their canoes between the two great water systems of the continent.
=== DEEPER The Man Who Set the Fire | legends
Of all the stories told about Taliesin, the hardest to pin down is the one about the man who destroyed it. His name was Julian Carlton, and more than a century later, nearly everything about his reasons is guesswork.

Here's what's documented. Carlton, a man of about thirty, had been hired that June, along with his wife, Gertrude, to work at Taliesin as a cook and servant, with references from a Chicago caterer. On August fifteenth, 1914, while Wright was away in Chicago, Carlton served lunch, then poured gasoline, set the house on fire, and attacked the people who tried to escape. That evening, he was found hiding in the boiler below the house. He had swallowed muriatic acid. It burned his throat so badly that he could hardly eat, and about seven weeks later, he died in custody, of starvation. He pleaded not guilty, and he never explained himself.

What isn't documented is why. The newspapers of 1914 filled the silence with speculation, much of it shaped by the racism of the time, since Carlton was Black. The theories that have lasted are these: that one of the draftsmen, Emil Brodelle, had insulted him with racial slurs; that Carlton and his wife had been told they were losing their jobs; or that he was mentally ill. Each has its supporters, and none has ever been proved. Even his origins are uncertain. He said he came from Barbados, and that's how the story is usually told, though even that has been questioned.

What's certain is the loss. Seven people died: Mamah Borthwick; her children, John and Martha Cheney; the draftsman Emil Brodelle; the gardener David Lindblom; a workman, Thomas Brunker; and young Ernest Weston, the son of the carpenter William Weston, who survived his own wounds. Their story is usually told as part of Wright's, as a tragedy that happened to him. It's worth remembering their names here, at the house where two of those children grew up.
