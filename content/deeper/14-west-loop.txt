=== STOP Where the Great Fire Began
=== DEEPER Why the Engines Were Late | history
When the fire broke out in the O'Leary barn, Chicago had a fire alarm system that other cities envied. Since the 1860s, alarm boxes on street corners had been wired by telegraph to a central office in the courthouse downtown. To prevent false alarms, the boxes were kept locked, and the keys were held by trusted people nearby, often shopkeepers. High in the courthouse tower, a watchman scanned the skyline every night for the glow of a fire. On paper, help should have come quickly. It didn't.

Near the barn, a neighbor ran to a drugstore that had one of the alarm boxes. What happened there is still disputed. The druggist said later that he turned in the alarm, but if he did, the signal never reached the central office.

Up in the tower, the watchman, Mathias Schaffer, did see the glow to the southwest. But in the dark he misjudged the distance, and he had the operator below send out the alarm for box three forty-two, about a mile beyond the real fire. When Schaffer realized his mistake, he asked the operator to correct it. The operator, William Brown, refused, by most accounts because he feared that a new signal would only confuse the engine companies already on their way. So some of the engines headed for the wrong neighborhood, and lost precious minutes.

The department was also small for the job it faced: fewer than two hundred firefighters, with about seventeen horse-drawn steam engines, for a city of some three hundred thousand people. Each engine could throw a strong stream of water, but only as far as its hoses could reach.

By the time enough engines were in place, the fire had spread through the sheds, fences, and cottages around the barn, and the wind was lifting burning embers over the firefighters' heads to start new fires a block or more ahead. Many historians believe that from then on, no fire department of the time could have stopped it.
=== DEEPER The Same Night in the North Woods | nature
On the same night that Chicago burned, a far deadlier fire was racing through the forests of northeastern Wisconsin, a little over two hundred miles north of here. It's remembered by the name of the lumber town at its center, Peshtigo.

The whole upper Midwest was parched that fall. Chicago had seen only a fraction of its normal rain since early summer, and in the north woods, small fires had been smoldering for weeks in the forests and the peat bogs. Logging made things worse. The lumber companies left behind great piles of branches and treetops, called slash, and crews building a new railroad cleared land by burning it.

Then, on the evening of October eighth, 1871, a powerful storm system swept across the region, with strong, gusty winds out of the southwest. The same winds that carried the flames from the O'Leary barn toward downtown Chicago whipped the scattered fires of Wisconsin into a single wall of flame. Survivors described a roar like a train, and whirlwinds of fire that tossed houses and wagons. In Peshtigo, people threw themselves into the river and stayed there for hours, while their town burned around them.

No one knows exactly how many people died. Estimates run from about twelve hundred to well over two thousand, which makes it the deadliest wildfire in American history. It burned more than a million acres. That same night, fires also tore through towns in Michigan, including Holland and Manistee.

Over the years, some writers have suggested that all these fires started at once, set off by fragments of a comet. Scientists reject the idea. Meteorites are cold by the time they reach the ground, and the drought and the wind explain the fires well enough.

The telegraph lines around Peshtigo had burned, so news of the disaster was slow to get out, and by then the world's attention was fixed on Chicago. To this day, far more people know the story of the cow and the lantern than the name of Peshtigo.
=== DEEPER The Suspects | legends
The cow story was a newspaper invention, but people have never stopped looking for the real culprit, and two suspects have drawn the most attention.

The first is the O'Learys' neighbor, Daniel Sullivan, known as Peg Leg because he had a wooden leg. He told the official inquiry that he was sitting across the street that night, saw flames in the barn, and hobbled inside to save the animals. In the 1990s, a Chicago lawyer and amateur historian named Richard Bales went back to the old property records and maps of the block. He argued that from where Sullivan said he was sitting, other buildings would have blocked his view of the barn. Bales suggested that Sullivan may already have been in the barn when the fire started, perhaps by accident, and then told a story to protect himself. It's a careful argument, but it's still a theory. Bales's research did help persuade the city council to clear Catherine O'Leary's name.

The second suspect is a man named Louis Cohn. According to a story published after his death in 1942, Cohn, who had grown wealthy, admitted that as a teenager he'd been shooting dice in the barn that night with some other boys, and that a lantern was knocked over as they scrambled away. Cohn left money to Northwestern University's journalism school, and the story is often told along with his gift. But no one has produced his confession in his own words, so it can't be checked.

The O'Learys' own story took an unexpected turn. Their son James, a child at the time of the fire, grew up to be Big Jim O'Leary, the gambling king of the stockyards district. For decades he ran a saloon and gambling house on South Halsted Street, near the yards. When he died, in 1925, the newspapers remembered him as a man who would bet on anything, and as the son of the woman Chicago had blamed for the fire.
=== STOP Hull House
=== DEEPER The Snare of Preparation | people
Jane Addams didn't come to Hull House by a straight road. She was born in 1860 in Cedarville, a small town in northern Illinois. Her father, John Addams, owned mills, served in the state senate, and had been a friend of Abraham Lincoln. Her mother died when she was two. Jane had a curved spine, probably from a form of tuberculosis, and as a girl she worried that her looks embarrassed her handsome father. She adored him, and took in his sense of duty.

In 1881 she graduated at the top of her class from Rockford Female Seminary, at a time when few women went to college at all. That summer her father died suddenly. She started medical school in Philadelphia, then left it, ill and depressed. For most of the 1880s she drifted, traveling in Europe and living with family, a well-educated woman with no clear purpose, as many women of her class were. She later called those years the snare of preparation: a life spent getting ready for something that never began.

Two scenes in Europe shook her. In London's East End, she watched a late-night auction of spoiled fruit and vegetables, and never forgot the hands of the hungry crowd, reaching up for food. Then, in the spring of 1888, she went to a bullfight in Madrid. Her companions, including her friend Ellen Gates Starr, left in disgust, but Addams stayed and watched for hours as bulls and horses were killed. That evening she felt ashamed, and she wrote later that it made her see all her preparation as a kind of moral failure.

Soon afterward she told Starr about the plan she'd been turning over for months, to rent a house in a poor neighborhood and live there. Starr was eager to join her. The next year, back in Chicago, they found the old Hull mansion on Halsted Street, and on September eighteenth, 1889, they moved in.
=== DEEPER Garbage, Maps, and the Eight-Hour Day | politics
Hull House didn't just serve its neighbors. It studied them, and then it went into politics on their behalf.

One of its residents, Florence Kelley, was hired by the state in 1892 to investigate the sweatshops of Chicago, where whole families, children included, sewed garments for pennies in crowded rooms. The next year, Hull House women and their allies pushed a bill through the Illinois legislature that barred factories from hiring children under fourteen and limited women's work in factories to eight hours a day. Governor John Peter Altgeld made Kelley the state's chief factory inspector, to enforce it. Manufacturers fought back, and in 1895, the Illinois Supreme Court struck down the eight-hour limit, as a violation of the freedom of contract. Two years later, a new governor removed Kelley from her post. She moved to New York, and spent the rest of her life campaigning for labor laws across the country.

Also in 1895, the residents published a book called Hull-House Maps and Papers. Its centerpiece was a set of colored maps of the blocks around Hull House, showing, house by house, where people had come from and how much they earned. It was modeled on a famous survey of poverty in London, and it was one of the first studies of its kind in America.

That same year, Jane Addams took on the garbage. The streets of the ward were heaped with refuse, and complaints about the contractor who was supposed to collect it had gone nowhere. So Addams put in a bid for the contract herself. The bid wasn't accepted, but the mayor appointed her garbage inspector for the ward, a paid city job and the only one she ever held. For a time, she was up before dawn to follow the garbage wagons on their rounds, and the streets got cleaner.

It was politics at street level, and it put Hull House at odds with the ward's powerful alderman, Johnny Powers. More than once, Hull House backed candidates to unseat him, and lost.
=== DEEPER Art for Everyone | culture
Jane Addams believed that her neighbors needed beauty as much as bread, and Hull House was full of art.

The first new building on the property, opened in 1891, was the Butler Art Gallery, paid for by a Chicago businessman, Edward Butler. Its ground floor held a reading room that became a branch of the public library. Upstairs, Hull House hung paintings borrowed from wealthy collectors, so that people who'd never set foot in a museum could see them, free, a few doors from home.

In 1900 came something stranger, which Hull House called the Labor Museum. Addams had noticed that many immigrant children were ashamed of their parents, who seemed old-fashioned and foreign. So the museum invited older women from the neighborhood to demonstrate the skills they'd brought from the old country, spinning wool by hand, weaving at looms, and other crafts, arranged to show how each one had developed over the centuries. Children who had shrugged at their mothers' skills watched them with new respect.

Hull House was a theater, too. A company of neighborhood amateurs, the Hull-House Players, was led from about 1900 by a former professional actress, Laura Dainty Pelham. They staged serious modern plays, by Ibsen, Shaw, and Galsworthy, when Chicago's commercial theaters seldom did, and they're often counted among the founders of the little theater movement, the small, serious playhouses that spread across the country in the 1910s.

There was a music school, founded in 1893 by Eleanor Smith, where neighborhood children studied for years. And from the late 1920s, the Hull-House Kilns turned out pottery, shaped and painted by neighborhood artists, among them Mexican immigrants, which collectors prize today.

None of it was meant as charity. Addams thought of art as something that belonged to everyone, and she wanted the people of Halsted Street to make it as well as look at it.
=== STOP Greektown & Old Saint Patrick's
=== DEEPER Greek Chicago | history
A few Greeks lived in Chicago by the middle of the nineteenth century, but the great wave came between about 1890 and 1920. Most were young men from the villages of the Peloponnese, the mountainous peninsula of southern Greece, many of them from around Sparta and from the region of Arcadia. Hard times pushed them out, including a collapse in the price of currants, the small dried grapes that many villages lived on. Many meant to make some money and go home. Many stayed.

They settled a little south of here, in a triangle of streets formed by Halsted Street, Harrison Street, and Blue Island Avenue. Its shape gave it a name, the Delta, after the triangular Greek letter. There were coffeehouses where men played cards and argued about politics back home, grocery stores selling olives and cheese from barrels, and Greek newspapers. In 1897, Greeks here founded Holy Trinity, the city's first permanent Greek Orthodox church, and in 1908 the parish opened the Socrates School, said to be the first full-time Greek school in the United States. By some counts, Chicago's Greek community was the largest in America until after the Second World War, when New York's overtook it.

Many young Greeks started out as street peddlers, selling fruit and flowers from pushcarts, close to the city's big produce markets. When the city tightened its rules on street vendors, they moved indoors, and opened fruit stores, florists, confectioneries, and lunchrooms. By the 1920s, Greeks ran a large share of the cheap restaurants in Chicago, the coffee shops and lunch counters where working people ate, and for generations after that, a Chicago diner was very often a Greek diner.

Later, many Greek families moved to the North Side and the suburbs, and the Delta was cleared for highways and the university. But Halsted Street stayed the symbolic heart of Greek Chicago. The National Hellenic Museum, which tells the story of Greek immigrants in America, stands at Halsted and Van Buren, and each spring a Greek Independence Day parade marches up the street.
=== DEEPER The Cone That Conquered America | food
The gyro as most Americans know it, a big cone of seasoned ground beef and lamb turning slowly in front of an upright broiler, sliced thin into a warm pita with onion, tomato, and a cucumber and yogurt sauce, is very largely a Chicago creation.

In Greece, the dish called gyros, pronounced roughly yee-ros, from the Greek word for turn, is usually made of slices of pork or chicken stacked on a spit. It's a cousin of the Turkish doner kebab, and Greek refugees from Asia Minor are usually credited with bringing the method to Greece in the 1920s. In America, Greek cooks in Chicago remade it.

Several men claim a share of the story. George Apostolou, a Greek immigrant, said that he started selling gyros at his restaurant here in 1965, building the cones by hand. Hand-built cones took skill and hours of work, and not every lunch counter could manage them. In the early 1970s, a few Chicago companies worked out how to make cones in factories, from ground meat pressed into shape, and to ship them frozen. Now any diner with an upright broiler could sell gyros. One of those companies, Kronos, started in the 1970s by Chris Tomaras, grew into what was, by some accounts, the biggest maker of gyro cones in the world.

From Chicago, the frozen cone spread across the country, to diners, food courts, and hot dog stands. Here at home, the gyro took its place on the menus of countless fast food stands, right next to the hot dogs and the Italian beef.

So if you order a gyro almost anywhere in America, there's a good chance that the cone it was sliced from, or at least the idea of it, came out of a factory in the Chicago area. The one thing Chicago never settled is how to say it. Greeks say yee-ros. Many Americans say it like the first part of gyroscope. And plenty of people just point at the picture on the menu.
=== DEEPER O'Shaughnessy's Windows | look
If Old Saint Patrick's is open, step inside and let your eyes adjust. Much of what glows around you is the work of one man, the Chicago artist Thomas O'Shaughnessy.

O'Shaughnessy was swept up in the Celtic Revival, the movement around 1900 that set out to recover Ireland's ancient art, language, and literature. He's said to have been inspired by an exhibit of Irish art at the 1893 World's Fair, and he studied the illuminated manuscripts of medieval Ireland, above all the Book of Kells, with its endlessly interlaced knots, spirals, and fantastic beasts.

Between about 1912 and 1922, he designed fifteen stained glass windows for this church. Look closely at the borders and the backgrounds, where the patterns weave over and under one another as they do on the pages of the old manuscripts. As the sun moves around the building during the day, the colors change.

The most famous of them is a set of three panels, representing Faith, Hope, and Charity. It's a memorial to Terence MacSwiney, the Lord Mayor of Cork, who died in a London prison in 1920, after a hunger strike of seventy-four days, during Ireland's war of independence. His death made headlines around the world, and in Chicago's Irish parishes he was a martyr. One historian of stained glass has called it arguably the finest window of its kind in the Midwest.

O'Shaughnessy also covered the walls and ceiling with painted Celtic stencils. Over the decades they were lost, and the church declined along with its neighborhood. By the early 1980s, the parish had only a handful of registered members. A new pastor, Father Jack Wall, arrived in 1983 and set out to make it a home for the young people who worked downtown. The parish grew into the thousands. In the 1990s, in time for the parish's hundred and fiftieth anniversary, it restored the windows and recreated the stencils, and the church glows again much as O'Shaughnessy meant it to.
=== STOP Union Station
=== DEEPER The Day of Two Noons | history
Before 1883, there was no such thing as standard time. Every town set its clocks by the sun, so that noon came when the sun stood highest in the sky. Because the sun moves from east to west, noon in Chicago came about eighteen minutes later than noon in Detroit, and about ten minutes earlier than noon in Saint Louis.

For people who walked or rode horses, that hardly mattered. For the railroads, it was chaos. Each railroad ran on its own time, often the local time of its headquarters city, and a junction like Chicago could have several different times showing on the clocks of its stations. Timetables had to be converted from one time to another, and travelers missed their connections.

The railroads fixed the problem themselves. In October 1883, their managers met at the General Time Convention, at the Grand Pacific Hotel in the Loop, and adopted a plan worked out by a railroad official named William F. Allen. The country would be divided into four zones, Eastern, Central, Mountain, and Pacific, each an hour apart, and every railroad would run on the time of its zone.

The change took effect on Sunday, November eighteenth, 1883, which became known as the day of two noons. In places that had to turn their clocks back, the clocks struck noon, and then, a few minutes later, struck it again. Chicago sits a little east of the middle of the Central zone, so here the railroad clocks went back about nine and a half minutes, and most of the city soon followed.

Not everyone liked it. Some people objected to what they called railroad time, and a few insisted that the only true time was God's time, the time of the sun. For years, some towns kept their own local time. It wasn't until 1918 that Congress wrote the time zones into law.

Stand under a departures board here and you can see what those railroad managers achieved: every train, from every direction, runs on the same clock.
=== DEEPER Three Railroads and a Riverbank | companies
Union Station gets its name from the railroads that built it together. Its owners were the Pennsylvania Railroad, the great Eastern line whose trains ran to New York and Washington, and two Western lines: the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy, known as the Burlington, and the Chicago, Milwaukee, Saint Paul and Pacific, known as the Milwaukee Road. The Pennsylvania owned half, and the other two a quarter each. A fourth railroad, the Chicago and Alton, rented space as a tenant.

The same railroads had shared an older and smaller station on this site since the 1880s, and by the early 1900s it was hopelessly crowded. Daniel Burnham's firm planned the new one, but Burnham died in 1912, before construction began, and the partners who carried on his practice, soon renamed Graham, Anderson, Probst and White, saw it through. The work took about twelve years, slowed by the First World War, shortages of materials, and labor troubles, and much of it had to be done while trains kept running.

The site was a hard one. The station is squeezed between Canal Street and the river, and its tracks run below street level, so that the streets above, like Adams and Jackson, cross over them. Most of the tracks don't pass through. They come in from the north and from the south and end at platforms on either side of the middle, an arrangement railroad people call a double stub-end terminal. Milwaukee Road trains used the north side, and the Pennsylvania and the Burlington the south.

In its prime, the station was home to some of the most famous named trains in America: the Pennsylvania's Broadway Limited to New York, the Burlington's sleek, stainless steel Zephyrs to Denver and the West, and the Milwaukee Road's Hiawathas, among the fastest steam-powered trains in the world, racing to Milwaukee and the Twin Cities.

None of those railroads exists anymore. All of them disappeared into mergers. But their station is still here, and still one of the busiest in the country.
=== DEEPER The Great Hall, Up Close | look
Step into the Great Hall, and before you look up, find the two statues on the east wall. They're by the sculptor Henry Hering, and they've been here since the station opened. One figure holds a rooster, the bird of morning, and stands for Day. The other holds an owl, and stands for Night. Together they make a simple point: a great railroad station never sleeps.

Now look up at the skylight. During the Second World War, when the station was handling close to a hundred thousand passengers a day, many of them soldiers and sailors, the glass was blacked out, so that the station would make a less visible target for enemy bombers. The bombers never came, but the blackout coating stayed on for years, and for a long time the Great Hall was a dim, gray place. The old skylight also leaked, and water stained and damaged the plaster.

In 2018, Amtrak finished a restoration that cost about twenty-two million dollars. It built a new skylight of clear glass a few feet above the old one, and the Great Hall now gets about half again as much daylight as before. The plaster was repaired, and the statues of Day and Night were restored and lit.

As you look around, think about what this room was for. In the 1920s, a railroad station was a city's front door, the first thing many visitors saw, and the railroads wanted that first impression to be overwhelming. A traveler who stepped off a train after a night in a sleeping car walked up from the platforms into this room, all marble and light, and knew they had arrived somewhere important.

Over the years there have been many plans for the station's future, from towers above it to a rebuilt concourse. Whatever comes next, the station is protected as a city landmark, and the Great Hall is likely to look much as it does now for a long time to come.
=== STOP Haymarket Square
=== DEEPER The Trial | politics
The trial of the Haymarket defendants opened on June twenty-first, 1886, before Judge Joseph Gary, and from the start it was less a search for the bomb thrower than a trial of anarchism itself.

Choosing the jury took three weeks, and nearly a thousand potential jurors were questioned. Instead of drawing names at random, the court sent a special bailiff out to round up candidates, and the defense charged that he picked men hostile to the accused. Several jurors admitted that they were prejudiced against anarchists, or had already formed an opinion, and the judge seated them anyway, once they said they could be fair.

One defendant, Albert Parsons, had slipped out of Chicago after the bombing and was hiding in Wisconsin. On the first day of the trial, he walked into the courtroom and surrendered, to stand with his comrades. It was a brave gesture, and it cost him his life.

The prosecution didn't need to show who had thrown the bomb. It argued that the defendants had conspired to encourage violence, through their newspapers and their speeches, and so they were all guilty of murdering Officer Mathias Degan, whoever had actually thrown it. In his closing, the state's attorney, Julius Grinnell, told the jurors that the law itself was on trial, and urged them to make examples of these men, hang them, and save the nation's institutions.

On August twentieth, the jury found all eight guilty. Seven were sentenced to death, and the eighth, Oscar Neebe, to fifteen years. The appeals failed. On the day before the executions, the governor commuted the sentences of Samuel Fielden and Michael Schwab to life in prison, and the youngest of the condemned, Louis Lingg, killed himself in his cell with a small explosive that had been smuggled in to him.

On November eleventh, 1887, the remaining four were hanged at the Cook County jail. Today, most historians see the trial as one of the great miscarriages of justice in American history.
=== DEEPER The Governor Who Paid the Price | people
John Peter Altgeld, the governor who pardoned the surviving Haymarket defendants, was an immigrant himself. He was born in 1847 in a village in western Germany, and his parents brought him to a farm in Ohio as a baby. His father, a stern man, saw little use for schooling. Young Altgeld served in the Union army as a teenager, near the end of the Civil War, and came home with a fever that weakened him for the rest of his life.

He taught school, studied law, and worked his way west, and in 1875 he arrived in Chicago with little money. He became a lawyer, a judge, and a very successful real estate investor, and by the end of the 1880s he was a rich man. In 1892, he was elected governor of Illinois, the first Democrat to win the office in decades, and the first governor of the state born abroad.

He came into office believing the Haymarket trial had been a travesty, and he studied the record closely. On June twenty-sixth, 1893, he pardoned Fielden, Neebe, and Schwab. He didn't simply grant mercy. In a long written statement, he argued that the jury had been packed, that the judge had been biased, and that nothing in the evidence connected the men to the bomb. He also blamed the police, whose brutality toward strikers, he suggested, had fueled the anger behind the violence.

The reaction was ferocious. Newspapers across the country called him a friend of anarchy and an alien, and he was burned in effigy. He had expected the backlash, and it cost him. In 1896 he lost his bid for a second term.

Altgeld died in 1902, at fifty-four, after collapsing at the end of a speech in Joliet. A decade later, the poet Vachel Lindsay wrote a tribute to him called The Eagle That Is Forgotten. Chicago didn't forget him entirely. In Lincoln Park stands a statue of him by Gutzon Borglum, the sculptor who went on to carve Mount Rushmore, with a family of working people beside him.
=== DEEPER Two Monuments | history
Two very different monuments have stood for the Haymarket, and both have had eventful lives.

The first was a bronze statue of a Chicago policeman, put up in 1889 in the middle of the square with money raised from the city's business leaders. The sculptor, Johannes Gelert, showed the officer with one hand raised, as if ordering the crowd to disperse. In 1900, the statue was judged a traffic hazard and moved to Union Park, about a mile west. In 1927, on the anniversary of the bombing, a streetcar jumped its track and knocked it off its pedestal. The motorman supposedly said he was tired of seeing that policeman with his arm raised all the time. It's a good story, and it may even be true.

Later the statue came back to Randolph Street, near its old spot. On an October night in 1969, a bomb blew off its legs and scattered pieces onto the expressway below, and the radical Weathermen claimed the attack. The city rebuilt it, and in October 1970 it was blown up again. After that, police guarded it around the clock, until it was moved indoors in 1972. It has stood inside police buildings ever since.

The second monument is in Forest Park, just west of the city, in what was then Waldheim Cemetery, where the five dead defendants were buried. It was dedicated on June twenty-fifth, 1893, the day before the governor pardoned the three survivors. The sculptor, Albert Weinert, made a figure of Justice placing a laurel wreath on the head of a fallen worker. Carved on its base are the last words that August Spies called out from the scaffold: The day will come when our silence will be more powerful than the voices you are strangling today.

Over the years, many radicals asked to be buried close to it, among them the anarchist Emma Goldman. The ground around it is still a place of pilgrimage, and in 1997, the monument was named a National Historic Landmark.
=== STOP Restaurant Row & What Chicago Eats
=== DEEPER The Candy Capital | companies
For much of the twentieth century, Chicago was the candy capital of America. At its peak, the city had well over a hundred candy companies, with some twenty-five thousand workers, and by some estimates it made about a third of all the candy in the country.

The reasons were practical. Chicago sat at the center of the railroad network, so sugar, corn syrup, milk, and nuts could come in cheaply from every direction, and finished candy could go out to every town in the country. The city had plenty of workers, many of them immigrant women, and room for big factories.

Some of the best-known names in American candy grew up here. Emil Brach opened a small candy shop in 1904, and Brach's grew into a giant, with an enormous factory on the West Side. Salvatore Ferrara started in 1908 on Taylor Street, in Little Italy, making sugar-coated almonds, the kind handed out at Italian weddings, and his company went on to make Red Hots and Atomic Fireballs. Otto Schnering founded the Curtiss Candy Company in 1916, naming it, by most accounts, after his mother's family, because Schnering sounded too German during the First World War. Curtiss made Butterfinger. Frank Mars moved his company here from Minneapolis around 1929, and it was in Chicago that Snickers and Three Musketeers were born. Tootsie Roll moved its headquarters and its factory here in the 1960s.

From the 1970s on, many of the factories closed or left. The reasons were complicated, but candy makers often pointed to the price of sugar in the United States, which federal import quotas kept far above the world price. Some production moved to Canada and Mexico, where sugar was cheaper, and Brach's left the city in the 2000s.

Still, Chicago never stopped making candy. Tootsie Rolls still come from a plant on the Southwest Side, and Ferrara, now a much larger company, still makes candy in the Chicago area.
=== DEEPER Who Gets the Credit | legends
Chicago has a habit of inventing famous foods and then arguing about who did it. Two stories show how slippery the history can be.

The first is the Baby Ruth. The Curtiss Candy Company brought out the bar in 1921, just as Babe Ruth was becoming the most famous athlete in America. But the company always insisted that it was named not for the slugger but for Ruth Cleveland, the daughter of President Grover Cleveland, who had been the nation's darling as a baby in the White House. The trouble is that Ruth Cleveland had died of diphtheria in 1904, seventeen years earlier. Many people suspect the story was a way to cash in on the ballplayer's fame without paying him. When Babe Ruth later tried to sell a candy bar under his own name, Curtiss fought it, and won, arguing that buyers would confuse the two.

The second is deep-dish pizza. The usual story says it was invented in 1943 at Pizzeria Uno by its owners, Ike Sewell, a Texan, and Ric Riccardo, a restaurateur. But both were businessmen, not cooks, and others have claimed the credit. Some give it to Rudy Malnati, who managed the restaurant for years, and whose son Lou later started his own pizza chain. Others credit a cook named Alice Mae Redmond, who worked in Uno's kitchen and later at Gino's East. No recipe or record from 1943 survives to settle it.

There's a further twist. Most Chicagoans don't eat deep dish very often. The everyday pizza of the city's neighborhoods is thin and crisp, and cut into small squares instead of wedges, a style known as tavern cut. The story goes that bars served it to keep their customers drinking.

And stuffed pizza, a deep dish with a second layer of crust on top, has its own rival origin stories, from Chicago restaurants that opened in the 1970s. In this city, a good argument about food is part of the meal.
=== DEEPER From Market Stalls to Michelin Stars | food
Notice how wide Randolph Street is here. There's a reason. In 1850, the city built a market hall right in the middle of the street at Des Plaines, and the roadway was laid out broad enough for farmers to pull up their wagons and sell from them. A bird's-eye view of Chicago from 1857 shows the widened street and the market hall, with a bell tower on top. The produce wholesalers stayed long after the farmers were gone, and a few food distributors still work in the district today.

The first restaurants that changed the street came in the early 1990s, when the area was still dark and empty at night. Vivo, an Italian restaurant, opened in 1991, and others followed. In 1997 came Blackbird, a small and sleek restaurant led by the chef Paul Kahan, which drew national attention. Kahan and his partners later opened Avec next door, with long communal tables and small plates meant for sharing, and then the Publican, a couple of blocks north on Fulton Market, a beer hall devoted to pork and oysters.

In 2010, Stephanie Izard, who had won the television cooking competition Top Chef two years before, opened Girl and the Goat on this street, and the lines out the door made Randolph Street a destination for diners from all over the country. Dozens of restaurants opened in the blocks around it over the next decade.

When the Michelin guide began rating Chicago restaurants, at the end of 2010, the West Loop quickly became one of the places where its stars clustered, and some of the city's most ambitious tasting-menu restaurants are within a short walk of here.

Not everything has lasted. Rents climbed with the neighborhood's fame, and several of the restaurants that made the street famous have since closed. Restaurant Row keeps changing, which is nothing new for a street that started out as a market, where every morning brought different crates and different buyers.

=== DEEPER Meat on Fulton | history
Look at the street signs here. They don't say Fulton Street. They say Fulton Market, a name that has stuck to this stretch for more than a century.

The meat trade on Fulton grew up in the 1880s, as an offshoot of the Union Stock Yards, a few miles to the south. At the stockyards, the big packers slaughtered animals by the millions. But someone had to cut the carcasses into the pieces that butchers, hotels, and restaurants wanted, and get them where they were going, and much of that work came to be done here, close to downtown and the railroads. In 1887, a group of small meat dealers joined together to build a shared market on Fulton Street, and over the next four decades the street filled with low brick buildings for meat dealers, poultry houses, and produce merchants. The big packers, like Armour and Swift, opened branch houses here too. Most of the buildings you see went up between the 1880s and the late 1920s.

For most of the twentieth century, this was a working district, busiest in the hours before dawn, when trucks lined the curbs and workers in white coats hauled sides of beef and pork across the loading docks. The street smelled of meat and diesel, and hardly anyone lived nearby.

The trade shrank after the Second World War. The big packers moved their slaughterhouses west, closer to the cattle, and the stockyards closed in 1971. Supermarket chains began buying meat already cut and boxed, so they no longer needed middlemen in the city. Still, some meat and food businesses held on, and a handful are still here, sharing the street with restaurants, offices, and shops.

If you come by early on a weekday morning, you may still see a refrigerated truck backed up to one of the old docks, its driver wheeling boxes inside while the restaurants next door are still dark. It's a small reminder that for most of its life, this street fed the city.
=== DEEPER Thawing the Cold Storage | architecture
The big brick building at the corner of Fulton Market and Morgan Street was built in the 1920s as the Fulton Market Cold Storage warehouse. It was essentially a giant refrigerator, ten stories tall, where meat and other food could be kept frozen until it was sold.

Cold storage buildings like this one were built to keep heat out, which is why the original walls had very few windows. The walls were thick and heavily insulated, and the concrete floors were built to carry enormous loads of frozen goods. For almost ninety years, the building did just what it was built to do, and from the street it looked like what it was, a huge, nearly blank brick box.

In 2012, the Chicago developer Sterling Bay bought it, with a plan to turn it into offices. The first problem was the cold itself. After decades of refrigeration, the building was frozen to its core, and it had to be thawed out slowly, over months, so that the structure wouldn't be damaged as it warmed. Then the builders stripped the inside back to its concrete frame, opened large new windows in the brick walls, and added a new six-story wing alongside. Inside, they left the heavy concrete columns and floors exposed, the kind of industrial look that technology companies like.

Google signed on as the main tenant, and in late 2015 it moved its Chicago offices here, bringing hundreds of employees to a street of meat dealers and warehouses. The building took a new name from its address, 1000 West Fulton Market: One K Fulton.

Look at the walls and you can still read the change. The rows of large windows are cut into what was once nearly solid brick. Google is now moving many of its Chicago workers to the old Thompson Center in the Loop, but it has said it will keep its offices on Fulton Market too.
=== DEEPER Zoning the Boom | politics
The change in Fulton Market didn't just happen. It was helped along, step by step, by decisions at City Hall.

For decades, the city protected this area as an industrial zone, where new offices and homes weren't allowed, so that factories and food businesses wouldn't be priced out by developers. In the 2010s, those protections came apart.

In 2012, a new station opened at Morgan Street on the elevated line along Lake Street, a block south of here, which made the district an easy ride from downtown. In 2014, the city's Plan Commission adopted a plan that named this area the Fulton Market Innovation District, and opened the door to offices for technology and creative companies, while promising to look after the food businesses that were still here. In 2015, the city council made the old market blocks a landmark district, protecting the faces of the historic buildings, while still allowing new construction behind and around them. And in 2017, the city lifted the industrial protections from part of the wider manufacturing corridor around Fulton Market.

The result was one of the fastest real estate booms in the city's history. Office towers and apartment buildings rose on old parking lots and around the warehouses. Companies moved their headquarters here from the Loop and the suburbs, chasing young workers who wanted to be near good restaurants and the train. Hotels and private clubs opened in old factories.

Not everyone was pleased. Many of the remaining meat and produce businesses, facing rising taxes and rents, or simply offered a good price, sold out and moved away, and people who had worked here for decades watched the district they knew disappear. Others argued that those businesses would have left anyway, and that the boom brought jobs and tax money the city needed.

Look along the street and you can often see both sides of the argument at once: an old loading dock across from a glass office lobby.

=== DEEPER The Salesman | people
Ray Kroc was fifty-two, and had been a salesman for most of his working life, when he first saw the McDonald brothers' restaurant.

He left school young. In 1918, still a teenager, he lied about his age to join the Red Cross as an ambulance driver, and he trained in the same company as another teenager who would become famous, Walt Disney. According to Kroc, Disney was considered an odd one, because when the others went out to chase girls, he stayed behind and drew pictures. The war ended before Kroc got overseas.

In the 1920s he played piano in bands and at a radio station, and sold land in Florida during the real estate boom there. Then, for most of the next two decades, he sold paper cups for the Lily-Tulip company, and became one of its best salesmen in the Midwest. One of his customers had invented a machine that could mix five milkshakes at once, the Multimixer, and Kroc went into business selling it across the country.

In 1954, he noticed that one small restaurant in San Bernardino, California, had ordered eight of his machines. He went out to see why. He found a stand selling just a few things, hamburgers, fries, shakes, and soft drinks, cheaply and very fast, to long lines of customers. He talked the brothers, Richard and Maurice McDonald, into letting him sell franchises.

The partnership was never easy. The brothers were cautious, and Kroc wanted to grow fast. In 1961 he bought them out for two point seven million dollars. They kept their original restaurant but lost the right to their own name, and renamed it the Big M. A new McDonald's soon opened nearby, and before long, the Big M was out of business.

Kroc's real genius may have been in what came next. His financial man, Harry Sonneborn, persuaded him that the company should own or lease the land under each restaurant and rent it to the franchisee. McDonald's became, in part, a real estate company, and the steady rent made it rich.
=== DEEPER Hamburger University | companies
Somewhere in this building is one of the most unusual schools in the world: Hamburger University, McDonald's training center for restaurant managers and owners, which moved here with the headquarters in 2018.

It began in 1961, in the basement of a McDonald's restaurant in Elk Grove Village, northwest of the city, with a small class of students. The idea is usually credited to Fred Turner, who had started out a few years earlier as a grill man at one of Ray Kroc's first restaurants, and who would one day run the whole company. Turner was obsessed with doing everything the same way, every time. He helped write the company's first operations manual, which spelled out exactly how to cook a hamburger, how to make the fries, and how long food could sit before it had to be thrown away. Hamburger University's job was to teach that system to everyone who ran a McDonald's, so that a burger in Ohio would taste just like a burger in Oregon.

Graduates receive a degree in what the company calls, with a wink, Hamburgerology. Over the decades, hundreds of thousands of people have earned one, and the school now teaches in many languages, with campuses in other countries as well.

For decades, Hamburger University was part of the company's wooded campus in Oak Brook, in the western suburbs, where McDonald's had moved its headquarters in 1971. Before that, the company had been based in downtown Chicago. When McDonald's decided to come back to the city, it said it wanted to be closer to its customers and to the young workers it hoped to hire, and the West Loop, with its restaurants and trains, was the place.

The move was part of a larger shift. For decades, big companies had left Chicago for office parks in the suburbs. In the 2010s, many of them started coming back, and few moves sent that message more loudly than McDonald's leaving its suburban campus for this block.
=== DEEPER Oprah's Chicago | culture
When Oprah Winfrey took over AM Chicago in January 1984, she was twenty-nine, and she'd been co-hosting a talk show in Baltimore. Chicago was then the home base of the king of daytime talk, Phil Donahue, whose show had been made here for a decade. Within about a month, the newcomer was beating him in the local ratings.

Her style was different. Donahue was an earnest, intelligent interviewer. Winfrey talked about her own life, her weight, her childhood, her mistakes, and she treated her audience as friends. In 1985 she also became a movie star, with an Academy Award nomination for her role in Steven Spielberg's film The Color Purple.

Then came the question of how to take the show national. According to the film critic Roger Ebert, the two of them went out to dinner, and Winfrey told him she was weighing offers to syndicate it. Ebert said he took a napkin and worked out how much she might make, using the numbers from his own syndicated show with Gene Siskel. Whether or not the napkin made the difference, The Oprah Winfrey Show went national on September eighth, 1986, and within a year it was the top talk show in the country. In 1988, her own company took ownership of the show, and bought the old armory on this block to make into her studios.

From here, she shaped the country's tastes. Her book club, begun in 1996, could turn a novel into a bestseller almost overnight. The products she praised on her favorite things shows sold out. In 2004, she gave every member of her studio audience a new car.

She taped her final episodes here in May 2011. That same month, the city gave the block of Carpenter Street beside the studio an honorary name, Oprah Winfrey Way. Three years later, Harpo sold the property, and by the end of 2015, the company had moved its work to California.
