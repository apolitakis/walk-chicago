=== DEEPER The Shriners | culture
The men who built the club with the onion dome belonged to one of the most colorful fraternities in America, and they took their fun seriously.

The Shriners began in New York in the early 1870s, when a doctor named Walter Fleming and an actor named William Florence decided that the Freemasons, the old and solemn fraternal order they both belonged to, needed a livelier branch. According to the order's own story, Florence had been entertained by an Arab diplomat on his travels abroad and came home with the idea. They dressed their new order in an invented Arabian costume: the red fez, leaders called potentates, and local chapters called temples, named for places in the Muslim world. Only Masons could join. The order spread fast, and its members became famous for their parades, their marching bands, and the little cars they drove in tight circles down the street.

Chicago's chapter took the name Medinah, after the holy city in Arabia. In 1912, it built Medinah Temple, a couple of blocks northwest of here at Wabash Avenue and Ohio Street, with domes and horseshoe arches and an auditorium that held more than four thousand people. For decades it was the home of the Shrine Circus, and from around 1970, the Chicago Symphony, under its music director Georg Solti, made many of its famous recordings in that big hall, which recording engineers preferred to the orchestra's own hall downtown. The temple still stands. After the Shriners sold it, it became a furniture store, and more recently a temporary casino.

By the 1920s, the Medinah Shriners wanted a clubhouse to match, and they hired Walter Ahlschlager, an architect who also designed grand movie palaces, to build this tower on the avenue. It didn't last, as you've heard.

The Shriners have far outlived their club. Their best-known work began in 1922, when they opened their first hospital for children, in Shreveport, Louisiana. Today there are Shriners children's hospitals across North America, including one on Chicago's Northwest Side, and for most of their history they treated their young patients without charging their families anything at all.

=== DEEPER Selling the Mile | companies
When Arthur Rubloff gave this street its famous name, he wasn't describing it. He was advertising it.

After the Second World War, North Michigan Avenue looked tired. Hardly anything had been built since the Depression began, and many of the buildings along it were aging and shabby. The man who designed this castle, William Boyington, was one of the busiest architects in early Chicago, and once you know his style, you can spot it around northern Illinois.

He was born in Massachusetts in 1818, trained as a carpenter, and learned architecture in New York before moving to Chicago in 1853, when the city was booming and almost any building was a good business. Over a long career he designed hotels, churches, railroad stations, and the grand Board of Trade building of 1885 at the foot of LaSalle Street, long since replaced.

For the waterworks, he chose a style called castellated Gothic, with battlements and turrets borrowed from medieval castles. He had used it before. He designed the gates of Rosehill Cemetery, on the North Side, in that style, and before that, the state penitentiary at Joliet, a fortress of the same yellow stone that held prisoners until 2002. If you've seen the opening scenes of The Blues Brothers, you've seen it.

The stone itself is a local story. It's a hard, yellowish limestone quarried along the Des Plaines River southwest of the city, around Joliet and a town then called Athens, now Lemont. In the years around the Civil War, it was a favorite building stone in Chicago, used for churches and fine houses all over town. Most of them burned in 1871 or were torn down later, which is one more reason these two buildings are precious.

Now take a closer look. The blocks are rough-faced, left with the marks of the quarry, so they catch light and shadow. Inside all that stone, as you've heard, there was only a pipe, about three feet wide and well over a hundred feet tall.

Boyington lived until 1898, long enough to see his castle become the city's favorite survivor. In his later years he lived up the North Shore in Highland Park, where he served as mayor, and he's buried at Rosehill, behind the gates he designed. === DEEPER The Night the Pumps Stopped | history
In 1871, Chicago fought fires with steam engines, and the steam engines needed water from the city's mains. That's why what happened here, in the small hours of Monday, October ninth, mattered so much.

The fire had started on Sunday evening on the West Side, near DeKoven Street, and around midnight it jumped the South Branch of the river and raced into downtown. A strong, hot wind blew from the southwest, carrying burning boards and shingles over the rooftops, and some time after one in the morning, the fire crossed the main branch into the North Side.

The pumping station's roof was framed in wood. Some time after three in the morning, burning debris carried on the wind landed on it. The roof caught fire, and by about half past three it had fallen in on the engines below. The pumps stopped. All over the city, the hoses went dry, and from then on, there was little anyone could do but run.

And run they did. Tens of thousands of people fled north, carrying what they could, through streets so jammed that wagons couldn't move. Many ended up at the lakeshore, some standing in the water for hours with wet blankets over their heads while the fire roared behind them. Others crowded into the old city cemetery at the south end of what's now Lincoln Park, sheltering among the graves.

The North Side burned all through Monday. Block after block of wooden cottages, and the mansions of the rich, went down together, as far north as Fullerton Avenue. Late that night, it finally began to rain, and by Tuesday morning the fire was dying out.

When people came back, there was almost nothing left in this part of the city. But here at Chicago Avenue, scorched and blackened, the castle and its pumping station were still standing. The engines were repaired, the water flowed again, and Chicagoans adopted the two buildings as proof that their city could survive anything.
=== DEEPER The Tunnel Under the Lake | underfoot
About two miles straight out from here, under the lake, is the far end of one of the great engineering feats of the 1860s, and it started at a shaft near where you're standing.

Ellis Chesbrough's plan, drawn up in 1863, called for a tunnel only five feet across, lined with brick, running more than ten thousand feet through the clay beneath the lake bed. Nothing quite like it had been tried. Work began on the shore in 1864. Crews sank a shaft near the lake and started digging east, by hand, with picks and shovels, by lamplight, hauling the clay back to the shaft in small cars.

Far out in the lake, the city built a crib: a huge, five-sided box of timbers, framed on shore, towed out into open water, and sunk in place by filling its compartments with stone. A shaft went down through the middle of it, and a second crew began digging west, toward the shore, from the middle of the lake.

For most of that year, the two crews worked toward each other in the dark, guided only by surveyors' instruments and arithmetic. In November 1866, they broke through. When they did, the two halves of the tunnel were less than eight inches out of line. Masons followed the diggers, lining the tunnel with two layers of brick, and on March twenty-fifth, 1867, clean water from far out in the lake began flowing through it into the pumps here and out to the city.

It was a sensation. Engineers came to study it, and the crib became one of the sights of Chicago, a lonely little fortress on the horizon.

The first tunnel was soon overwhelmed by the growing city, and others followed, longer and deeper, as Chicago kept reaching farther into the lake. Today the system delivers hundreds of millions of gallons a day. But it all began with two crews of diggers, meeting in the middle, under the lake.

In February 1882, the most talked-about young man in the English-speaking world stepped off a train in Chicago. Oscar Wilde was twenty-seven. Think about that. Fourth Presbyterian Church had about the worst first day a congregation could have.

=== DEEPER The Caissons | underfoot
Under the Hancock, the ground is soft clay for a long way down before you reach solid rock. A tower this heavy couldn't float on that clay the way the buildings of the 1880s did. It needed legs that reached all the way to bedrock.

Those legs are called caissons. Each one is a deep, round shaft drilled down through the clay and filled with concrete, so that it becomes a solid column standing on rock. The tricky part is the clay itself, which is wet and soft and wants to squeeze back into the hole. Builders keep it out with steel casings and careful timing, and if they hurry, the concrete may not fill the shaft completely.

At the Hancock, someone hurried. In 1967, with the steel frame already rising, engineers noticed that part of the building was settling more than it should. When they looked into it, they discovered voids in some of the caissons, weak spots where the concrete hadn't filled the shafts the way it was supposed to. A building that was meant to stand on solid legs was standing, in places, on flawed ones.

Construction stopped. For about four months, crews went back underground to redo five of the caissons, with the partly built tower waiting above them. The delay helped push the developer, Jerry Wolman, out of the project, as you've heard, and the insurance company took over.

The repairs worked, and the tower was finished in 1969. But the episode became a lesson that engineers still talk about: the hardest part of building a skyscraper is often the part no one ever sees, and a building that seems to defy the wind a thousand feet up depends, in the end, on what was done right, or wrong, in the dark under the street.

So as you look up at those great diagonal braces, spare a thought for the columns beneath your feet.

Here's a building to look for later on this walk. A few blocks southeast of here, near the lake at Ontario Street, is a tall, pale concrete tower with giant X shapes running up its sides. It's called the Onterie Center, and it was the last major work of Fazlur Khan, the Hancock's engineer.

=== DEEPER The Lake's Moods | nature
Lake Michigan looks permanent from here, a calm blue line at the edge of the city. But it rises and falls, sometimes over years and sometimes in minutes, and the drive in front of you has felt both.

Over the years, the lake's level goes up and down with the rain, snow, and evaporation across its whole enormous basin. In October 1986, it reached the highest level on record. In January 2013, it hit the lowest, more than six feet below that peak, and Chicago's beaches grew wide. By 2020 it was back up near the record, and that January a winter storm drove waves over the lakefront path and onto Lake Shore Drive, tearing up the trail and closing parts of the road.

Over minutes, the lake can be even more dangerous. On a Saturday morning, June twenty-sixth, 1954, a fast-moving line of storms crossed the southern end of the lake, and the sudden jump in air pressure set off a wave. It came ashore along the North Side, from Wilmette down to North Avenue, as much as ten feet high, and it swept fishermen off the piers and breakwaters at Montrose Harbor and North Avenue. By most counts, eight of them drowned. At the time, it was called a seiche, the sloshing of water from one side of a basin to the other. Scientists now call it a meteotsunami, a tsunami set off by the weather instead of an earthquake.

Now look at the edge of the water. Between 1910 and 1931, Chicago armored much of its shore with big limestone blocks, laid in steps on top of wooden cribs and pilings. Over the decades, the wood rotted, the stones slumped, and the waves chewed at the land behind them. Since the late 1990s, the city, the Park District, and the Army Corps of Engineers have rebuilt more than nine miles of it, mostly in concrete, as broad steps where people sit in summer to watch the water. They're built for the lake at its worst, because sooner or later, the lake comes back.

Plenty of critics in New York didn't know what to make of them. Chicago loved them.

They showed together for only a few years, in Chicago, San Francisco, New York, and Washington, and broke up in 1969. But other young artists showing at Hyde Park worked in the same spirit, among them Roger Brown and Ed Paschke, and a critic, Franz Schulze, gave the whole loose movement a name that stuck: the Chicago Imagists. Several of them went on to long careers. Jim Nutt and Gladys Nilsson, who married young, became two of the most admired painters to come out of the city.

This museum collects their work, and in 2018 the Art Institute gave the Hairy Who a big retrospective. If the galleries are open, look for them. It's some of the most Chicago art there is: a little rude, very funny, and made with a craftsman's care.
=== DEEPER Under the Glass | people
The museum's reputation for provocation was earned early, and two of its most famous moments came from artists who worked with the strangest materials of all: a human body and a whole house.

In April 1975, in the museum's first home on Ontario Street, a young California artist named Chris Burden lay down on the gallery floor beneath a large sheet of glass, propped at an angle against the wall, with a clock hanging nearby. He had set himself a rule and shared it with almost no one: he would stay there until someone interfered. Visitors came and went. The museum closed for the night, and then for a second night, and Burden stayed where he was, without food or water. After about forty-five hours, a member of the museum's staff set a container of water down within his reach. Burden got up at once, and the piece, which he called Doomed, was over. Who, it asked, decides when art ends?

Three years later, the museum bought the townhouse next door to expand into, and before the builders arrived, it handed the old house to Gordon Matta-Clark. The son of the Chilean painter Roberto Matta, he had trained as an architect and made his name cutting buildings open: he sawed a house in New Jersey in half, and carved a great cone through two old buildings in Paris. Here, he cut huge circles through the townhouse's walls and floors, so that visitors, led through by the museum's guards, could look up, down, and across through a spiral of openings. He called it Circus, or The Caribbean Orange. It was open for about two and a half weeks, and then the house was rebuilt into galleries. That summer, Matta-Clark died of cancer, at thirty-five.

At the very same time, in the museum's own galleries next door, a show was running that would look prophetic: the first solo museum exhibition in the United States for a Mexican painter then far less famous than she is today, Frida Kahlo.
=== DEEPER Mrs. Ward's Gift | history
The towers of hospitals and laboratories around you began with a gift from a widow.

Montgomery Ward, the mail-order merchant, died in 1913. His widow, Elizabeth Ward, wanted a memorial to him, and beginning in 1923 she gave Northwestern University a series of gifts that added up to more than eight million dollars. Northwestern had decided to bring its professional schools together on a new campus near downtown, here on the young landfill of Streeterville, and Mrs. Ward's money built the centerpiece: the Montgomery Ward Memorial Building, a Gothic tower for the medical and dental schools.

The architect was James Gamble Rogers, who designed many of the Gothic buildings at Yale. He gave Northwestern a campus stretched upward instead of outward, in limestone, with pointed windows and carved trim. Alongside it, a gift from Rachel Mayer in memory of her husband, the Chicago lawyer Levy Mayer, built a home for the law school, and a third building, Wieboldt Hall, housed the business school. They opened in 1926 and 1927, and you can still see them along Chicago Avenue.

Hospitals gathered around the medical school. Two older ones, Passavant and Wesley Memorial, combined in the 1970s to form Northwestern Memorial Hospital. In 2012, Children's Memorial Hospital moved here from its longtime home in Lincoln Park and took a new name, Lurie Children's.

Not every building has been kept. When the university moved to tear down Prentice Women's Hospital, the city's landmarks commission met in 2012, voted to give it preliminary landmark protection, and then, in the same meeting, voted to take that protection away, clearing the way for the wrecking ball. Preservationists never forgot it.

All of this rose on land that a century and a half ago was open water. The doctors and students hurrying along these sidewalks are standing, just as you are, on Cap Streeter's lake.
=== STOP Ohio Street Beach & the Water Cribs
=== DEEPER Fire on the Lake | history
The cribs on the horizon look peaceful from the beach, but building them was dangerous work, and the worst disaster in their history happened on a January day in 1909.

As the city grew, it kept pushing its intakes farther out, to reach water beyond the pollution near the shore. Every new intake needed a tunnel, and the tunnels were dug the old way, by crews of men working beneath the lake bed in shifts around the clock. To save the long trips back and forth by boat, the contractors housed many of the workers out on the water.

In the winter of 1909, crews were digging a tunnel to bring fresh water to the growing South Side. About a mile out from 71st Street, on a temporary crib, stood a wooden barracks where a hundred or so men ate and slept, above the shaft that led down to their tunnel.

On January twentieth, fire broke out in that building, and it raced through the dry timber. The men had almost nowhere to go. Some were trapped inside, and others ended up in the freezing lake. A fire tug and other boats hurried out from shore, and their crews pulled survivors from the water, but by the end of the day about sixty men were dead, and some counts run higher.

No one ever settled how it started. One newspaper blamed an explosion of dynamite in the shaft below. A rumor blamed a worker who had sprinkled gasoline around the bunks to kill bedbugs.

Photographs taken in the days afterward, now in the collection of the Chicago History Museum, show relatives of the victims stepping off a streetcar, and men picking through the charred wreck during the inquest.

The tunnel was finished, and the new intake went into service. But when you look out at those little towers on the water, remember the men who dug the tunnels beneath them, and what it cost to bring the lake into the city's taps.
=== DEEPER Alewives and Salmon | nature
In the summer of 1967, beaches like this one were covered with dead fish.

They were alewives, small silvery fish about the length of your hand, at home in the Atlantic Ocean. They had found their way into the upper Great Lakes through the canals built for ships, and in Lake Michigan they found a paradise. The lake's big native predator, the lake trout, had been nearly wiped out in the 1940s and 1950s by another invader, the sea lamprey, a parasite that latches onto fish and feeds on their blood. With almost nothing to eat them, the alewives multiplied into the billions.

Alewives are fragile, and they die off in great numbers when conditions in the water change. Every year, some washed up on the beaches. In 1967, they died by the billions, and all along the southern shore of the lake, the beaches were buried in rotting fish. Chicago sent crews with tractors and trucks to scrape them up, swimmers stayed away, and people still remember the smell.

The cure came from Michigan. A state fisheries chief named Howard Tanner had a bold idea: bring in a predator from the Pacific. In the spring of 1966, Michigan released hundreds of thousands of young coho salmon into rivers that flow into the lake, and the next year, Chinook salmon. The salmon fattened on alewives and grew big, and over the following years, the alewives were brought under control, and a whole new sport fishery was born. In summer, charter boats leave Chicago's harbors every morning to fish for salmon.

It's an artificial balance, kept going by stocking, and newer invaders have changed the lake again. Zebra and quagga mussels, which arrived in the ballast water of ships, have filtered the water so clear that in places you can see the bottom many feet down, and they've starved the tiny creatures at the bottom of the food chain. Lake Michigan looks like wilderness from this beach, but it's one of the most rearranged bodies of water on earth.
=== DEEPER Private Olive | people
The park beside you carries the name of a young man from Chicago who lived only eighteen years.

Milton Lee Olive the third was born in the city in November 1946. He joined the Army as a teenager and volunteered to be a paratrooper, and by the fall of 1965 he was a private first class with the hundred and seventy-third Airborne Brigade, one of the first American combat units sent to Vietnam.

On October twenty-second, 1965, his platoon was moving through the jungle near Phu Cuong, pursuing enemy soldiers, when a grenade landed in the middle of a small group of men. Olive grabbed it, pulled it against his body, and fell on it. He was killed, and the men around him survived.

The next April, at a ceremony at the White House, President Lyndon Johnson presented the Medal of Honor to Olive's father and stepmother, with Mayor Richard J. Daley among the guests. In his remarks, the president read aloud from a letter that Olive's father had written to him, a grieving father's plea that his son's sacrifice might help bring Americans of every race and faith together in goodwill. Then the president promised that the nation would never forget Milton Lee Olive the third.

It was the spring of 1966. The civil rights movement was at its height. Martin Luther King Junior had moved into an apartment on Chicago's West Side that winter, and that summer he would lead marches for open housing through white neighborhoods of the city. A young Black man from the city had given his life for the men beside him, and his father had answered with a plea for brotherhood.

Chicago kept the president's promise in its own way. Besides this park, his name is on Olive-Harvey College, one of the City Colleges, on the far South Side, which honors him together with Carmel Harvey, another young Chicagoan who received the Medal of Honor for giving his life in Vietnam. On a summer afternoon, with families on the lawn and the skyline behind them, it's worth remembering whose name this place carries.
=== STOP Navy Pier
=== DEEPER Municipal Pier | history
The pier you're walking on was built to solve a very practical problem.

By the early 1900s, the Chicago River was choked. Lake steamers carrying freight and passengers had to work their way up the narrow river to docks downtown, waiting for bridges to swing open, while the open bridges held up traffic in the streets. The Plan of Chicago called for big piers out in the lake instead, where ships could dock in open water, and the city built one of them here.

Its architect, Charles Sumner Frost, had designed railroad terminals, and he gave the pier the logic of a station: long sheds for freight and passengers, ships tying up alongside, and two levels so that cargo could move on one and people on another. Streetcars ran out along it. At the far end, he put the things that would draw crowds: a domed hall for concerts and dances, places to eat, and open promenades over the water.

It opened in July 1916, and within two years it was pressed into service for war. During the First World War, it held barracks for servicemen and even a jail for men who had dodged the draft. Afterward it became a favorite summer escape, where Chicagoans came out by streetcar to catch the lake breeze, dance, and board excursion boats.

But it never became the shipping hub the planners imagined. Freight moved to trucks and railroads, and the passenger steamers faded away. After its years as a naval training station and a college campus, the pier sat half empty for decades. It hosted trade shows and festivals, among them ChicagoFest, the giant music festival of the late 1970s and early 1980s, while the city argued about what to do with it.

The answer came in the 1990s, when a new public authority took it over and rebuilt it, and the pier that was built for freight became a place built for fun.
=== DEEPER The Plane in the Terminal | people
If you ever fly out of O'Hare Airport, look for an old Navy fighter plane on display in Terminal Two, a stubby Grumman Wildcat. It's painted to honor the man the airport is named for. But the plane itself came out of the lake, off this pier.

During the Second World War, as you heard, Navy pilots learned to land on two converted lake steamers out on Lake Michigan. Not all of them made it, and well over a hundred planes ended up on the lake bottom, where the cold, fresh water preserved many of them remarkably well. This Wildcat went down during training in the 1940s and lay on the lake bed for almost fifty years, until it was raised in 1992 and restored for the airport.

The man it honors, Edward O'Hare, known as Butch, grew up in St. Louis and graduated from the Naval Academy in Annapolis. On February twentieth, 1942, flying from the carrier Lexington in the South Pacific, he found himself nearly alone between his ship and a formation of Japanese bombers. In a few minutes of furious flying, he shot down or damaged several of them and broke up the attack. He became the Navy's first ace of the war and received the Medal of Honor. In November 1943, he disappeared during a night battle near the Gilbert Islands, at twenty-nine. In 1949, Chicago renamed its new airport, Orchard Field, in his honor.

His father's story is darker. Edward O'Hare senior, known as Easy Eddie, was a lawyer from St. Louis who ran dog tracks in partnership with Al Capone's organization, and he quietly helped federal agents build the tax case that sent Capone to prison. In November 1939, a week before Capone was released, Easy Eddie was shot to death in his car.

A popular story says he turned informer to give his son a clean name and a place at Annapolis. It's a moving story, but whether that was really his reason, no one can say for sure. What is certain is that father and son both ended up as part of Chicago's story, one in the history of its gangsters, the other on its busiest airport.
=== DEEPER Two Wheels | look
Look up at the big wheel. It's the second Ferris wheel to stand on Navy Pier, and the story of the two of them says a lot about how this place has changed.

The first one went up in 1995, when the pier reopened. It was about a hundred and fifty feet tall, and it was said to be modeled on the original Ferris wheel from the World's Fair of 1893, which you can hear about on the Hyde Park tour. For twenty years it turned here, and it became one of the most familiar sights on the lakefront, lit up at night against the dark water.

By the time the pier turned a hundred, in 2016, its managers wanted something bigger. They took the old wheel down and sold it, and it was set up again in Branson, Missouri, for a new crowd of riders. In its place went the Centennial Wheel, which opened in May 2016. It's about a hundred and ninety-six feet tall, nearly fifty feet taller than the old one, and its forty-two gondolas are enclosed and climate controlled, with seats, so the wheel can keep turning through a Chicago winter.

From the top, you can see the whole route you've just walked: the Hancock and the Palmolive tower, the beaches and the high-rises of Streeterville, and to the south, the Loop, Grant Park, and the Museum Campus. Out on the water, you may spot the cribs, and on the clearest days, the smokestacks of the Indiana shore, far across the bottom of the lake.
