=== DEEPER The Pioneer | legends
You heard that Pullman turned to railroad cars and made the sleeping car a byword for comfort. The story he told about how it happened is one of the great legends of American business, and he kept telling it until the end of his life.

It goes like this. In 1864 and 1865, Pullman built a car finer than anything yet seen on American rails, and he named it the Pioneer. It was said to have cost about twenty thousand dollars, several times the price of an ordinary car. But it was so tall and so wide, the story goes, that it couldn't clear the bridges and station platforms of the day, and no railroad would run it. Then, in April 1865, Abraham Lincoln was killed. Only the finest car in the land would do for his funeral train, and so the railroads worked day and night, pulling up platforms and widening bridges and cuts, to make way for the Pioneer. Overnight, the whole country knew Pullman's name. Pullman is even said to have called the president's death a piece of good luck for his company.

Historians who have gone back to the records say the story can't bear much weight. Lincoln's coffin rode in a special car built for the government, not in the Pioneer. Mary Lincoln, who in some versions asked for Pullman's car, never rode the funeral train at all. She was too stricken to leave Washington. The Pioneer did join the train for the last leg, from Chicago to Springfield, apparently because a committee of Chicago businessmen, Pullman's friends among them, arranged it. But its part was minor, and the historians who have looked hardest at the company's early years treat the rebuilt bridges and platforms as Pullman's own invention.

What the legend does show is his gift for selling. Pullman understood that a company could sell a feeling, the sense of being part of something grand, and that a good story could do the selling for him.
=== DEEPER Government by Injunction | politics
You heard that federal troops helped crush the strike. Lawyers and judges did the rest, and the legal weapon they used shaped American labor for nearly forty years.

The railroads were organized long before their workers were. The lines running into Chicago belonged to the General Managers' Association, which worked together on wages and labor policy, and when the boycott began, it ran the railroads' side of the fight. It had a friend in Washington. The attorney general, Richard Olney, had spent his career as a railroad lawyer and still sat on railroad boards. At the association's urging, he made a railroad lawyer named Edwin Walker a special federal attorney in Chicago.

The government's case rested on the mail. By many accounts, the railroads made sure of that by hitching Pullman cars to trains that carried mail, so that refusing to move a Pullman car meant holding up the mail. On July second, 1894, two federal judges, William Woods and Peter Grosscup, issued an injunction of extraordinary reach. It forbade anyone to interfere with the mail or with trains crossing state lines, and it covered not just violence but persuasion. Even urging a railroad man to stay off the job could put you in contempt of court. Before the month was out, Debs and other union leaders were under arrest. That December, Judge Woods, without a jury, sentenced Debs to six months, and Debs served them in the county jail in Woodstock, northwest of Chicago.

His lawyer was Clarence Darrow, who had quit a comfortable job as an attorney for the Chicago and North Western railroad to take the case. At the Supreme Court, Darrow was joined by Lyman Trumbull, an old Illinois senator who had helped write the amendment that abolished slavery. On May twenty-seventh, 1895, the justices ruled against them, nine to nothing.

Unions called it government by injunction, and for decades employers used it to break strikes. Not until 1932 did Congress sharply limit the federal courts' power to do it.
=== DEEPER A Modern Lear | culture
Among the people who tried to settle the strike before it spread was Jane Addams, the founder of Hull House. In the spring of 1894, she joined a citizens' committee that tried to bring the company and its workers to arbitration. The workers were willing. The company was not. Its answer, as it was widely reported, was that there was nothing to arbitrate.

Addams kept turning the strike over in her mind, and she wrote an essay about it with a startling title, A Modern Lear. In Shakespeare's play, King Lear is a proud old father who gives his daughters everything, demands their love and gratitude in return, and can't understand why his youngest, Cordelia, won't flatter him. Addams saw Pullman the same way. He had given his workers fine houses, parks, and a church, and he expected them to be grateful, as a father expects gratitude from his children. What he couldn't see, she argued, was that they had grown up. They wanted a voice in their own lives, not presents. His very generosity had cut him off from the simple, equal relationship with his workers that might have spared everyone the tragedy.

She was no partisan. She wrote about the old man with real sympathy, as a tragic figure undone by his own idea of virtue. Her larger point was that the age of the benevolent employer, who decided what was best for his workers, was passing, and that even the kindest boss would have to learn to deal with them as equals.

She read the essay to audiences many times, beginning in 1895, but it wasn't printed until November 1912, in a magazine called The Survey. By then Pullman had been dead for fifteen years.

Look up at the clock tower, which once ruled every working day here, and you can see what she meant.

=== DEEPER The Smoke-Filled Room | politics
You heard that Florence Pullman married a lawyer named Frank Lowden, who became governor of Illinois. In 1920, her husband came within reach of the White House, and the way he lost it gave American politics one of its most famous phrases.

That June, the Republicans held their national convention in Chicago, at the Coliseum on South Wabash Avenue, and Lowden arrived as one of the two front-runners. The other was General Leonard Wood, an old comrade of Theodore Roosevelt's. On the first ballot, Wood led and Lowden was close behind, and neither man could break away.

Lowden's campaign carried a wound. Just before the convention, a Senate committee looking into campaign spending found that his managers had sent money into Missouri, and that two Missouri delegates had each received a check for twenty-five hundred dollars. The delegates said they'd give the money back, but the damage was done. The same investigation hurt Wood, whose backers had spent even more.

After four ballots on Friday, the convention was deadlocked, and it adjourned for the night. That evening, party leaders gathered in a suite at the Blackstone Hotel on Michigan Avenue, and by morning many of them had settled on a compromise, Warren Harding, a little-known senator from Ohio. Harding was nominated on the tenth ballot that Saturday, and in November he won in a landslide. A reporter's description of the scene, a smoke-filled room, entered the language as shorthand for deals made by insiders behind closed doors. Many historians now think that room's role has been exaggerated, but the phrase stuck.

Lowden never got another real chance at the top of the ticket. In 1924, the Republicans nominated him for vice president, and he turned them down. He gave much of the rest of his life to farming and to the cause of the nation's farmers.
=== DEEPER The President's Son | people
You heard that Robert Todd Lincoln led the Pullman Company after its founder died. His life had an uncanny way of crossing paths with history, and some of the crossings happened at railroad stations.

During the Civil War, Robert was on a crowded platform in Jersey City late one night, among passengers buying their places on a sleeping car from the conductor. The train began to move, the crowd pressed him against it, and he slipped into the gap between the car and the platform. A stranger seized him by the collar and pulled him to safety. Robert recognized him. It was Edwin Booth, the most celebrated actor in America, whose brother, John Wilkes Booth, would later kill Robert's father. Robert told the story himself, in a letter he wrote in 1909.

In July 1881, as secretary of war, Robert was at a Washington railroad station to see President James Garfield off on a trip, and Garfield was shot there. Twenty years later, at President William McKinley's invitation, Robert was at the exposition in Buffalo, New York, where McKinley was shot, and he arrived just after it happened. He's said to have turned down a later invitation from a president with the remark that there was a certain fatality about presidential occasions when he was present.

He came to Pullman as the company's lawyer and rose to be its president. By 1915 he was chairman of the board, and he was called before a federal commission on industrial relations to explain the porters' pay. A porter's wage then was about twenty-seven dollars and fifty cents a month, and tips made up much of the rest. Lincoln conceded that the wages were too low, and he said that if tipping were outlawed, the company would raise them at once.

In 1922, he sat in the audience when the Lincoln Memorial was dedicated in Washington. He died four years later and was buried at Arlington National Cemetery, not with his parents and brothers in Springfield.
=== DEEPER The Sleeping Car Empire | companies
The Hotel Florence was built to impress the people who did business with Pullman. What it really advertised was a hotel chain on wheels, and in the decades after George Pullman's death, that chain grew into one of the most complete monopolies in American business.

Pullman had absorbed smaller sleeping car companies one by one. The last big rival was the Wagner Palace Car Company of Buffalo, long tied to the Vanderbilt family's railroads. Around 1900, Pullman bought it, and by one estimate the renamed Pullman Company then controlled nearly nine-tenths of the country's sleeping car business. A railroad that wanted sleepers on its trains had little choice but to run Pullman's, staffed by Pullman's porters. In 1930, its peak year, the company operated close to ten thousand cars.

Then the Depression, the automobile, and the airplane went to work on it, and so did the government. In 1940, the Justice Department sued the company under the antitrust laws, arguing that building sleeping cars and operating them under one roof shut out competition. In 1944, a federal court agreed and ordered a split. The company could keep the factories or the sleeping car service, but not both, and it chose to keep building cars. In 1947, the sleeping car service, with its cars and its porters, was sold for about forty million dollars to a group of more than fifty railroads, which ran it together under the old name.

The end came slowly, then all at once. As travelers moved to planes and interstate highways, the railroads dropped their sleepers line by line, and at midnight on the last day of 1968, the Pullman Company stopped running sleeping cars in the United States. The car builder, known as Pullman-Standard, lasted a little longer. Its last passenger cars were double-deck Superliners for Amtrak, the national passenger railroad, finished around 1981. If you've ever slept on a long-distance train in America, you've traveled on Pullman's idea.

=== DEEPER Un-American | politics
In the mid-1880s, a young economist named Richard Ely came to study the famous model town, and in February 1885 he published what he found in Harper's magazine. Much of it was praise. He admired the town's layout, its variety, its cleanliness, and its beauty. But he ended with a verdict that stung. The idea of Pullman, he wrote, was un-American. He compared it to the paternalism of Bismarck's Germany, which looked after people's welfare while keeping power well out of their hands.

The town had no government of its own. It was one corner of the sprawling Village of Hyde Park, which reached from the edge of Chicago south past Lake Calumet, and Pullman liked it that way. When a movement grew to join Hyde Park to Chicago, he fought it. In 1887, one of the men working against annexation on his behalf was a young Pullman executive named John Hopkins.

The deciding vote came on June twenty-ninth, 1889. Across the village, annexation won, by about five thousand two hundred votes to three thousand one hundred. The old, comfortable settlement of Hyde Park itself, up by the lake, voted no. The yes votes came largely from the working-class districts farther south, very likely including many of Pullman's own tenants, though no separate count survives for the town. From then on, Pullman was part of Chicago. The company still owned the houses, but the people in them now lived under the city's laws and helped elect its aldermen and its mayor.

That's where the story takes a turn. Hopkins left the company and went into business for himself, and in December 1893, after the assassination of Mayor Carter Harrison, he was elected mayor of Chicago, at thirty-five the youngest the city had ever had. When the strike broke out the next spring, Pullman's former man sympathized with the strikers. He helped raise money for them and ordered the police not to interfere.
=== DEEPER Green Stone and Old Pipes | look
Stand where you can see the church's walls up close. You heard that its stone came from Pennsylvania. It's a rock called serpentinite, named for its mottled, snakeskin look, and its green comes from minerals that formed deep underground, when rock from beneath an ancient ocean floor was soaked by hot water and slowly transformed. Southeastern Pennsylvania has a band of it, and nineteenth-century builders prized it for its color. The quarry that supplied this church closed long ago.

The color came at a price. Serpentine is soft and porous. Water seeps in, freezes, and expands, and over the decades the stone flakes and crumbles. Look for the places where the surface has worn or broken away. In 2026, the church began its first major exterior repairs since it opened in 1882, a project of about one point two million dollars to save the crumbling bell tower. Only about a quarter of the original stone is going back on the tower. The rest is being replaced with cast stone made to look like it.

If you can get inside, look up. Above the sanctuary is the church's pipe organ, built in 1882 by Steere and Turner, a Massachusetts firm, and it's believed to have been here from the beginning. It's a tracker organ, which means it works entirely by mechanical linkage. When the organist presses a key, a chain of thin wooden strips and levers opens a valve beneath a pipe, and air from the wind chest makes it sing. There's no electricity between the player's fingers and the pipes. The organ was restored in the late 1960s, and it's still in its place above the pews.

For years after it was built, the church had no congregation, and its organ had no one to play for. Today the green stone and the old pipes are among the oldest things in Pullman still doing the work they were made for.
=== DEEPER The Preacher Who Took Sides | people
You heard that Pullman's one church stood empty for years, too expensive for any congregation. The town's other congregations met wherever they could, and in the spring of 1894, the pastor of one of them became the most talked-about preacher in Chicago.

His name was William Carwardine, and he led the First Methodist Episcopal Church of Pullman. In late May, soon after the walkout, he denounced George Pullman from his pulpit. By one account, he was answering another local minister who had preached against the strikers. Carwardine laid out the wage cuts, the rents, and the way the town was run, and he took the workers' side. It was a remarkable thing for a minister to do in a town where the company owned nearly everything, and newspapers across the country reprinted the sermon.

Later that year, he expanded it into a short book called The Pullman Strike. He wrote as someone who knew the town's families from the inside. After their rent was taken out of their pay, he wrote, men were left with between one and six dollars to live on for two weeks. He told of a man whose pay check, after the rent, came to two cents, and who never cashed it, keeping it instead as a memento.

The book was published by Charles H. Kerr and Company, a Chicago house that later became famous as a socialist press. It made Carwardine a hero to the strikers, and by one account it cost him his place in Pullman soon after it appeared. He spent the rest of his life speaking up for working people.

His book has never quite gone away. In 2024, for the strike's one hundred and thirtieth anniversary, the same publisher, still in business in Chicago, brought it out again. And the church in front of you, built for a congregation that never came, is now, fittingly, a Methodist church.
=== STOP The Workers' Rowhouses
=== DEEPER Two Checks on Payday | history
You heard that the rents here were higher than in the towns around Pullman. The way the company collected them made them harder still to bear.

There's a popular story that Pullman paid his workers in company scrip, money good only at company stores. The National Park Service, which now looks after the town's history, has searched for evidence of that and found none. Pullman paid in real money. But the company made sure a good part of it came straight back. If a man rented one of these houses, his rent was taken out of his earnings before he ever saw them. When an Illinois law challenged automatic deductions, the company found another way. Renters were handed two checks on payday, one for the rent and one for whatever was left, and everyone understood what was expected to become of the first one.

When wages were cut in 1893 and the rents stayed where they were, the second check shrank and shrank. Workers also complained that the company bought water and gas and resold them to its tenants at a steep markup. The company denied it, and insisted that it actually lost money on the water.

George Pullman's answer, given to the federal commission that investigated the strike, was that the town and the factory were separate businesses. The renting of the dwellings and the employment of workmen, he said, were in no way tied together. His houses competed for tenants with those in Kensington, Roseland, and other nearby towns, and about three hundred of his workers owned homes of their own. If the rents seemed too high, the argument went, a man was free to live elsewhere.

Many did. But workers answered that in a depression, with jobs scarce, few of them felt free to cross the company that employed them, even over where they slept. The town that was meant to lift its workers above want had come to feel, to many who lived in it, like a trap.
=== DEEPER The Girls' Union | people
Not everyone who struck at Pullman was a man. Women worked for the company too, and some of them boarded with families in houses like these. One of them gave the speech that helped turn a local strike into a national one.

Her name was Jennie Curtis. Born in 1871, she had worked for about five years as a seamstress in the company's repair shops, sewing for the cars that made Pullman famous. When the company cut wages in 1893, the women's pay fell along with the men's. By her own testimony, even the most experienced seamstresses could then make only about eighty cents a day, and many of the girls averaged forty or fifty cents.

Then her father died. He had worked for the company too, and he left behind a debt of sixty dollars in back rent. Curtis paid it, out of her own small wages. The company later said it had never forced her to, but it didn't deny taking the money.

When the workers organized, the women formed their own local of the American Railway Union, known as the girls' union, and they elected Curtis its president. In June 1894, the union held its national convention in Chicago, with delegates from railroad towns across the country, and the Pullman strikers came to ask for help. Curtis told the delegates her story, the wage cuts, the rents, and her father's debt, and her words are credited with winning them over. On June twenty-first, they voted that unless the company agreed to arbitration, the union's members would stop handling Pullman cars five days later. The company refused.

That August, she told her story again, this time to the commission President Cleveland had appointed to look into the strike, and her words went into the official record. She was about twenty-three. Little is known about the rest of her life. But as you walk these streets, you're passing the homes of people like her, whose pay was cut, whose rent wasn't, and who decided to fight back.
=== DEEPER Beneath the Model Town | underfoot
You heard that Pullman's houses had indoor toilets, a rarity for working families then. Where it all went is one of the town's strangest stories, and it began right under your feet.

Pullman's engineers laid sewers under these streets that carried the waste from every house, by gravity, to a great cistern beneath the town's water tower, which stood near the car works. The tower's basement was about forty feet deep. It held three pumps for the water supply and four for the sewage, along with a reservoir for some three hundred thousand gallons of it. From there, the pumps pushed the sewage through a twenty-inch iron main about three miles south, to a farm the company owned near 130th Street.

On the farm, the town's waste was spread over the fields as fertilizer. The idea was to show that in a well-run town, even sewage could be put to work. The fields grew vegetables, onions, cabbages, and celery among them, and by one account some of the produce came back to the town's market, to be sold to the families who had, in a sense, helped to grow it. Next door, the company ran a dairy, which by 1890 covered about four hundred and twenty acres and kept eighty to a hundred cattle.

A proud description of the town, published in 1893, laid all of this out as one of Pullman's wonders, scientific sanitation for a modern industrial community. Whether the farm ever paid for itself is disputed. And it didn't outlast the company town for long. After the houses were sold off, the farm faded away, by one account in about 1907, and the land around 130th Street, like so much of the Calumet region, filled in with industry.

None of it is left to see. But look down at the street as you walk. Pullman was a town planned from its sewers up, and that was as much a part of the model as the slate roofs and the trim.
=== STOP The Pullman Porters
=== DEEPER Twelve Years to the Day | politics
You heard that the Pullman Company fought the porters' union for twelve years. Here's how that fight was waged, and how the porters won it.

The company had no intention of bargaining with a union of its porters. It had already set up a company union, which it called the Employee Representation Plan, with social benefits and committees it could control, and it presented that as all the voice the men needed. When the Brotherhood appeared in 1925, the company sent spies into its meetings and fired hundreds of porters it suspected of joining. Most of Black Chicago's leaders opposed the union at first. Pullman was one of the biggest employers of Black men in the country, and it had long cultivated the goodwill of churches and community institutions.

Because porters risked their jobs by organizing openly, their wives often did the work instead. In Chicago, a porter's wife named Halena Wilson led the women's council that raised money and spread the union's message, and in 1938 she became president of the Brotherhood's international Ladies' Auxiliary.

The low point came in 1928. The porters voted to strike, and Randolph set a date. But federal mediators declined to step in, and William Green, the president of the American Federation of Labor, urged Randolph to call it off, warning that it would fail. Randolph did, just hours before the strike was to begin. Membership collapsed. By one count, it fell from more than four thousand six hundred to about a thousand in three years.

What saved the union was a change in the law. In 1934, Congress amended the Railway Labor Act to bar company-dominated unions on the railroads, and the next year, under federal supervision, the porters voted on who would represent them. The Brotherhood beat the company's association by more than four to one. On August twenty-fifth, 1937, twelve years to the day after the Brotherhood was founded, the Pullman Company signed its contract, with higher pay and a limit of two hundred and forty hours of work a month.
=== DEEPER The Society of Georges | names
You heard that passengers addressed porters as George, whatever their real names were. The oddest protest against the habit came from men who were named George themselves.

It was called the Society for the Prevention of Calling Sleeping Car Porters George. It was founded in 1914, or perhaps 1916, by George Dulany, a wealthy lumberman, and it seems to have begun at least partly as a joke. Anyone whose first or last name was George could join. The society mailed out tens of thousands of membership cards, claimed some thirty-one thousand members at its height, and boasted a roll of famous Georges: King George the Fifth of Britain, the French statesman Georges Clemenceau, Senator Walter George of Georgia, and the baseball player George Herman Ruth, better known as Babe. Its first president, by the society's own account, was Admiral George Dewey, the hero of Manila Bay.

How much of that was real and how much was publicity is hard to say. But the custom it mocked was no joke to the porters. Calling a grown man by another man's first name echoed the old practice of naming enslaved people after their owners, and it denied him even the courtesy of his own name.

In 1926, the year after the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters was founded, the Pullman Company began placing a small card in each car with the name of the porter on duty. The society claimed the credit, though the union had been demanding the same thing. And by one count, of the roughly twelve thousand porters and waiters then working for the company, just three hundred and sixty-two were actually named George.

In the museum, look closely at the photographs and the uniforms, and look for the names. Every one of these men had one.
=== DEEPER The Porter from Montgomery | people
You heard that Randolph went on to lead the fight for civil rights. One of the porters he inspired helped start that movement's most famous campaign, far to the south of here.

Edgar Daniel Nixon, known as E. D., was born in Alabama in 1899 and went to work for the Pullman Company as a porter in 1923. The job took him all over the country. In Saint Louis, at the local Young Women's Christian Association, he heard Randolph speak, and in 1928 he joined the Brotherhood. Back home in Montgomery, Alabama, he helped build its branch.

The union was a school as well as a union. Its members learned how to run meetings, keep records, raise money, and stand together against a powerful employer. Nixon carried those lessons into the fight for civil rights. He worked to register Black voters in Montgomery, which was hard and dangerous work, and he led the local branch of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People. Its secretary was a seamstress named Rosa Parks.

On December first, 1955, Parks was arrested for refusing to give up her seat on a city bus to a white passenger. Nixon called a white lawyer he trusted, Clifford Durr, and with Durr and his wife, Virginia, he went to the jail and got her out on bond. Then he asked Parks whether she would let her arrest become the test case against the city's segregation law. She agreed.

Nixon spent the next hours on the telephone, calling Montgomery's Black ministers. One of them was the young new pastor of the Dexter Avenue Baptist Church, Martin Luther King Junior. Within days, Black Montgomery had stopped riding the buses, and King had been chosen to lead the boycott. Through it all, Nixon kept working his runs as a porter, and he stayed with the Pullman Company until 1964.

So when you hear the story of Montgomery, remember that it runs back through a sleeping car, and through this union.
