=== DEEPER The Engineer from Dhaka | people
You heard that the tower's engineer was Fazlur Khan. He's one of the most important figures in the history of tall buildings, and his road to this corner was a long one.

Khan was born in 1929 in Dhaka, in what is now Bangladesh, the son of a mathematics teacher. He studied engineering in Calcutta and Dhaka, and in 1952 he crossed the world on a Fulbright scholarship to the University of Illinois, where in three years he earned two master's degrees and a doctorate. In 1955 he joined Skidmore, Owings and Merrill in Chicago, and apart from a short spell back home, he stayed for the rest of his life.

His gift was to see a skyscraper as a single problem in physics. Above a certain height, the real enemy isn't the weight of the floors but the wind, pushing on one side of the building like a hand on a lever. On the Michigan Avenue tour you'll hear how he answered it at the Hancock Center. Here he took the same thinking further, binding several stiff tubes into one.

There's a story, told by people who worked with him, that the bundled tube was first explained with a handful of cigarettes, held upright in a fist and pushed down to different lengths. Most versions give the cigarettes to the architect, Bruce Graham. Whether it happened just that way, nobody can say, but it's a fair picture of the tower above you.

Khan never thought engineering was only engineering. In 1971, when war broke out in what was then East Pakistan, he worked from Chicago to raise money and lobby Washington for the new country of Bangladesh. Later he engineered the Hajj Terminal at the airport in Jeddah, in Saudi Arabia, a vast field of white fabric tents that shelters pilgrims on their way to Mecca. A line of his is often quoted: the technical man must not be lost in his own technology; he must be able to appreciate life, and life is art, drama, music, and most importantly, people.

He died of a heart attack on a trip to Jeddah in 1982, at fifty-two. He's buried in Graceland Cemetery on the North Side, near many of the architects you'll hear about today, and there's a sculpture in his honor here in the tower.
=== DEEPER The World's Largest Store | companies
You heard how Sears grew from a watch business into the catalog that sold everything. Before this tower, the company ran that empire from the West Side, from a complex of more than forty acres at Homan Avenue and Arthington Street that opened in 1906, with its own power plant and rail yards. For most of the twentieth century, the catalog's orders were filled there, and the company's first retail store opened on the grounds in 1925. The old Sears tower on that campus, fourteen stories tall, still stands, in a neighborhood now called Homan Square.

In 1924, Sears started a radio station to reach farm families and named it after itself: W L S, for World's Largest Store. === DEEPER Tallest, Until It Wasn't | history
Ground was broken here in August 1970, and the last beam went up in May 1973. The frame took about seventy-six thousand tons of steel. You heard that the tower held the world's height record for almost twenty-five years. How it lost the title is a very Chicago grievance.

In 1996, the Council on Tall Buildings and Urban Habitat, the group of engineers and architects that keeps the official records, ruled that the Petronas Towers, going up in Kuala Lumpur, would be the tallest buildings in the world. The Malaysian towers had decorative spires, and the council counted them as part of the architecture. This tower had higher floors, a higher roof and far taller antennas, but antennas didn't count. Chicagoans felt robbed, and the argument pushed the council to start keeping several kinds of height records instead of one. These days, as it happens, the council has its headquarters in Chicago.

Then came Taipei 101 in 2004 and the Burj Khalifa in Dubai in 2010. In 2014, New York's One World Trade Center took the title of tallest building in the country, with the help of a spire of its own. Its roof is lower than this one.

The tower has had its adventures. On Memorial Day in 1981, a young man named Dan Goodwin, dressed as Spider-Man, climbed the outside of the building with suction cups and hooks while firefighters spent hours trying to stop him. He made it to the roof, was arrested, and was fined thirty-five dollars.

The name changed in 2009, and in 2015 the building was sold to the investment firm Blackstone, which spent hundreds of millions of dollars remaking its base. The shops and restaurants that opened at the bottom in 2022 are called Catalog, a nod to the book that paid for all of this.
=== STOP Chicago Board of Trade
=== DEEPER Bushels into Paper | history
You heard that the Board invented standard grades of grain. That idea came out of a machine. In the 1850s, Chicago built huge steam-powered grain elevators along the river. Grain arrived loose in railroad cars, rode belts of buckets up to the top of the elevator, poured into tall bins, and later slid down chutes into the holds of ships. In a system like that, nobody could keep each farmer's sacks apart. Grain from hundreds of farms ran together in the same bin, so it had to be sorted by grade, and the farmer got back not his own wheat but a receipt for so many bushels of a certain grade.

That receipt was the revolution. A piece of paper could change hands far faster than grain could move, and merchants were soon trading contracts for grain that hadn't been harvested yet. During the Civil War, with the Union army to feed, the trading boomed, and in 1865 the Board wrote formal rules for these futures contracts, including the deposit, called margin, that traders still put up today.

Not everyone thought it was honest. Farm groups called it gambling. And all over the country, storefront parlors called bucket shops let ordinary people bet on the Board's prices, which came in over the telegraph, without buying any grain at all. The Board fought them for decades, insisting that its prices were its own property. The case reached the Supreme Court, and in 1905 the Board won. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote the opinion, and he defended speculation itself. Speculation of this kind by competent men, he wrote, is the self-adjustment of society to the probable.

That's still the idea behind every futures market in the world: that a crowd of people betting on tomorrow's price will find it faster than anyone else can. Whether the price of bread should hang on that crowd is an argument that has never quite ended.
=== DEEPER From Pork Bellies to Money | people
The Board had a younger rival a few blocks away, the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, which started out in 1898 as a market for butter and eggs. For decades it was the scrappier exchange, trading onions until Congress banned onion futures in 1958, a ban that's still the law, and then frozen pork bellies, the raw material of bacon. The man who changed its fortunes came to America as a child refugee.

Leo Melamed was born in Bialystok, Poland, in 1932. When war came, his family fled to Lithuania, where they got two of the transit visas that the Japanese diplomat Chiune Sugihara was writing for Jewish refugees, against his government's orders. They crossed the Soviet Union on the Trans-Siberian Railway, sailed from Japan, and landed in Seattle in 1941.

In Chicago, as he tells the story, he was a law student who answered a newspaper ad from Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner and Beane, thinking it was a law firm. It was a brokerage, and the job was running orders on the floor of the Merc. By 1969 he was the exchange's chairman.

He saw that once the world gave up fixed exchange rates, which began when President Nixon cut the dollar's link to gold in 1971, the price of money itself would rise and fall like the price of wheat. So why not trade it? To win over the skeptics, he paid the University of Chicago economist Milton Friedman seven thousand five hundred dollars to write a paper on why the world needed a futures market in currencies. The Merc opened one in May 1972.

The Board answered in April 1973 with an exchange for stock options, opened in what had been its members' smoking lounge, which traded nine hundred and eleven contracts on sixteen stocks on its first day. That same spring, the economists Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published their formula for pricing options, in a University of Chicago journal. Chicago had turned the trading pit from a market for crops into a market for risk. In 2007, the younger exchange bought the older one.

The goddess on the roof was made by John Storrs, a Chicago-born sculptor who had studied in Paris with Auguste Rodin and spent much of his life in France. His Ceres is pure Art Deco, a figure reduced to smooth planes and folds, cast in aluminum, a modern material for a statue in 1930. Storrs stayed in France through the Second World War. === DEEPER Too Big to Fail | companies
LaSalle Street, stretching north from here, has been Chicago's money street for well over a century. Look up the street at the block just in front of you, where two classical buildings with tall columns face each other. On the left, on the west side, is the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago. On the right is the old home of the Continental Illinois bank, and its story is one of the great financial crises of the twentieth century.

In the early 1980s, Continental Illinois was the seventh largest bank in the country, and it had grown fast by lending aggressively, especially to oil and gas companies. Many of those loans came from a small bank in a shopping center in Oklahoma City called Penn Square, which collapsed in 1982 when the oil boom went bust. Continental's losses piled up. In May 1984, rumors spread through markets overseas that the bank was failing, and big depositors around the world began pulling out their money by wire. In about ten days, the bank lost close to a third of its funding.

Regulators faced a choice nobody had faced on this scale. If Continental failed, hundreds of smaller banks that kept their money in it might fall too. So the government stepped in. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation promised to protect every depositor, even those far above the insured limit, and that summer it put in a billion dollars and took over eighty percent of the company. It was the biggest bank failure in American history until 2008.

That September, at a hearing in Congress, the comptroller of the currency admitted that regulators had no workable way to let one of the very largest banks fail. A congressman from Connecticut, Stewart McKinney, put it bluntly. We have a new kind of bank, he said. It is called too big to fail. The phrase has come back with every financial crisis since.
=== STOP The Rookery
=== DEEPER John Root | people
John Wellborn Root was born in Georgia in 1850. During the Civil War, his father made money in blockade running, slipping cotton past the Union navy to England, and after Atlanta fell in 1864, he sent his teenage son across the Atlantic to school near Liverpool. Root came home to study engineering at New York University, and after the Great Fire, like so many young architects, he came to Chicago, where he met Daniel Burnham in a draftsman's office. They were partners by 1873.

They worked because they were opposites. Burnham was the salesman and organizer, the one who could walk into a boardroom and come out with a commission. Root was the artist: a gifted pianist and organist, a writer and critic, quick, funny and charming. Burnham handled the clients, and Root designed the buildings, this one included.

His family life was touched by loss. His first wife died young, not long after their wedding. His second wife, Dora Louise Monroe, was the sister of Harriet Monroe, the poet who later founded Poetry magazine here in Chicago, and who wrote the first biography of her brother-in-law.

In 1890, Burnham and Root were put in charge of the architecture of the coming World's Fair. In January 1891, the leading architects of New York and Boston came to Chicago to begin planning it with them. That same week, Root came down with pneumonia, and within days he was dead.

You heard that Burnham carried on alone. People have wondered ever since what the fair, and American architecture, would have looked like if Root had lived. He admired the bold, plain work being done in Chicago, and some historians believe he might have steered the fair away from the white classical style it finally took, the style that Burnham and the eastern architects chose and that shaped American public buildings for a generation. Nobody can know. What we have instead are the buildings he finished in his short career, and you're standing in front of one of the best.
=== DEEPER The Lobby's Three Lives | architecture
The light court has been remade three times, and you can still read the layers.

Root's original court, in 1888, was a showpiece of ornamental iron, dark and intricate, under a roof of glass. In 1905, the building's manager, Edward Waller, who had been one of Frank Lloyd Wright's early patrons, hired Wright to brighten it up, and you heard what he did with white marble and gold.

In 1931 came another remodeling, this time by William Drummond, who had once worked in Wright's studio in Oak Park. He put in new elevators and added Art Deco touches, and to squeeze in more rentable space, he took out a pair of stairways and divided up part of the lobby.

Then came the dim years. To stop the leaks and save on upkeep, the great glass roof over the court was covered with waterproofing and paint, and a room that Root had designed to be full of daylight was lit by electric bulbs for decades. By the 1980s, the building was shabby and its future uncertain.

The restoration, finished in the early 1990s, brought the daylight back. The architects, from a firm called McClier, chose to return the court to Wright's design of 1905 rather than Root's, and they protected the old glass roof by building a new skylight above it. So when you look up, you're seeing an idea from 1888 through a renovation from 1905, under a rescue from the 1990s.

The Rookery is still a working office building, so be respectful of the people at work here. The Frank Lloyd Wright Trust runs tours that start in the lobby, and they can take you into parts of the building that are otherwise closed to visitors.
=== DEEPER The Windy City | legends
In the late 1880s, Burnham and Root moved their offices to the top floor of the Rookery, and much of their early work on the 1893 World's Fair was done up there. The fair is tied to the most famous story about Chicago's nickname.

Chicago had to beat New York, Washington and St. Louis for the right to hold the fair, and the fight was nasty. The story goes that Charles Dana, the editor of the New York Sun, told his readers to pay no attention to the nonsensical claims of that windy city. He meant not the weather but the city's boastful politicians and promoters, full of hot air. It's a great story, repeated in countless guidebooks.

But nobody has ever found it in the Sun. People who've combed through old newspapers found Chicago being called the windy city years earlier, in the 1870s, especially in Cincinnati, a rival for everything from pork packing to baseball. In May 1876, the Cincinnati Enquirer ran the headline That Windy City over a story about a tornado that had struck Chicago. Rival papers used the phrase both ways, for the gusts off the lake and for the hot air of Chicago's boosters. The link to Dana doesn't show up in print until a Chicago Tribune article in 1933, decades after he died.

So the nickname may really be about the wind after all, at least partly. And yet Chicago isn't especially windy. By the weather records, several big American cities have higher average wind speeds, Boston among them. What Chicago has is a lake that makes its own weather, and downtown streets that act like canyons. Stand on a corner here in January, when the wind comes off the lake and funnels between the towers, and you'll understand why the name has never needed a better explanation.

=== DEEPER Down the Mississippi | history
The bronze reliefs over the doors show the journey that made Marquette and Jolliet famous, so here's the journey.

In May 1673, the two men set out from the mission at Saint Ignace, at the top of Lake Michigan, with five French voyageurs in two birch bark canoes. Jolliet was a fur trader born in Quebec, sent by the governor of New France to find out where the great river the Native people spoke of really went. Marquette was a Jesuit priest who spoke several Native languages. They paddled down Green Bay, up the Fox River, carried their canoes across to the Wisconsin River, and in the middle of June they floated out onto the Mississippi.

They went south for a month, past the muddy rush of the Missouri and the mouth of the Ohio, to a village of the Quapaw people near the mouth of the Arkansas River. There they learned that the river ran to the Gulf of Mexico, not the Pacific, and that farther down were Spanish soldiers who might well arrest them. They turned back, and on the way home, the Illinois people showed them the shortcut through Chicago.

Then the expedition's records nearly vanished. In 1674, almost home, Jolliet's canoe overturned in the Lachine Rapids near Montreal. He was barely rescued, but his companions drowned, among them a young Illinois boy who had been given to him, and his journal and maps went to the bottom. He wrote his report from memory, and most of what we know comes from the account credited to Marquette.

Marquette had promised to return to the Illinois people, and he kept the promise though he was already ill, which is why he had to spend that winter camped by the river here. He reached them in the spring, but heading north again along the lake, he died in May 1675, at thirty-seven, near what is now Ludington, Michigan, though the exact place is still argued over. Two years later, according to the Jesuits, a party of Native converts found his grave, cleaned his bones, and carried them by canoe, in a box of birch bark, back to Saint Ignace.
=== DEEPER The MacArthurs | people
You heard that the MacArthur Foundation makes its home here. The man behind it, John D. MacArthur, was one of the richest men in America and one of the least showy.

He was the son of a traveling evangelist preacher. He sold insurance, and in 1935, in the depths of the Depression, he bought a small, struggling Chicago company, Bankers Life and Casualty, and built it into a giant by selling inexpensive policies by mail. He moved on into real estate, especially in Florida, where in his last years he ran his businesses from a table in the coffee shop of a hotel he owned in Palm Beach Shores.

The foundation moved in here, and in 2001 it began a long, careful restoration that included re-creating the building's cornice, the heavy crown along the roofline that had been stripped off decades before.

The foundation is best known for its fellowships, the so-called genius grants, which began in 1981. You can't apply for one. The foundation itself avoids the word genius.

The family had another famous name. John's older brother Charles MacArthur was a Chicago newspaper reporter, and with his friend Ben Hecht he wrote The Front Page, a comedy set in the press room of the Chicago criminal courts, about reporters who'll do anything for a story. === DEEPER Who the Streets Are Named For | names
Chicago's downtown street names are a short course in early American history. Walk south from Washington Street, and you pass through the presidents almost in order: Madison, Monroe, Adams, Jackson, Van Buren, and then, past Ida B. Wells Drive, Harrison, Polk and Taylor.

The streets running north and south mostly honor the frontier. Dearborn Street takes its name from Fort Dearborn, which was named for Henry Dearborn, Thomas Jefferson's secretary of war. Clark Street is named for George Rogers Clark, who led the Revolutionary War campaign that won the Illinois country from the British. LaSalle Street is named for the French explorer René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle, who came through the Chicago portage in the 1680s on his way to claim the whole Mississippi valley for France.

Wells Street has the strangest story. William Wells was captured as a boy in Kentucky by the Miami people, raised as one of them, and married a daughter of the Miami war chief Little Turtle. He fought against the Americans and later for them, as a scout and interpreter. In August 1812, he hurried to Fort Dearborn to help lead its garrison to safety, and he was killed in the attack on the evacuation. According to survivors, the warriors who killed him ate his heart, to take in his courage.

For more than forty years, though, Wells Street wasn't Wells Street. By 1870 it had become a strip of saloons and brothels, and the name seemed an insult to the hero, so the city renamed it Fifth Avenue. It got its old name back in the 1910s.

Even the grid itself tells a story. The streets of the original town were laid out in 1830 by the commissioners planning the Illinois and Michigan Canal, who were selling lots to pay for it. Chicago began, in other words, as a real estate development, and it has been one ever since.

=== DEEPER Mies | people
Ludwig Mies van der Rohe was born plain Ludwig Mies, in 1886, in the old German city of Aachen, the son of a stonemason. He never went to architecture school. He learned to draw in his father's stone-carving shop and worked his way up in the offices of Berlin architects, including Peter Behrens, where Walter Gropius and Le Corbusier also worked for a time.

In the early 1920s, he remade his name. Mies, in German, sounds like a slang word for lousy or miserable. So he added his mother's family name, Rohe, with a Dutch-sounding van der in front, which sounded distinguished without claiming the von of the German nobility.

His most famous early building stood for less than a year. For the 1929 world's fair in Barcelona, he designed the German Pavilion, a low building of marble, onyx, glass and chrome, with walls that seemed to slide past each other under a roof that seemed to float. It was taken down after the fair and rebuilt on the same spot in the 1980s. The steel and leather chair he designed for it, the Barcelona chair, is still made today.

In 1930 he became director of the Bauhaus, the famous design school, and in 1933, under pressure from the Nazis, he and the faculty closed it for good. You heard that he came to Chicago a few years later. He never became comfortable in English, and he let his buildings do most of his talking.

In Chicago he never lived in one of his glass boxes. From the early 1940s until his death, he rented an apartment on East Pearson Street, a short walk from his famous towers on the lake, in a conventional building from 1916, hung with art by his old Bauhaus colleague Paul Klee. He died in Chicago in 1969, before this plaza was finished.

One line is often credited to him, though nobody has traced where he first said it: God is in the details. Look at the corners of these buildings, where steel meets glass, and decide for yourself.
=== DEEPER Inside the Conspiracy Trial | politics
You heard about the chaos in the courtroom here. The trial ran for nearly five months, from September 1969 into February 1970, and the defendants were a cross-section of the antiwar movement: Tom Hayden and Rennie Davis, organizers from the student left; the pacifist David Dellinger; Abbie Hoffman and Jerry Rubin, founders of the Yippies, who treated politics as theater; and two academics, John Froines and Lee Weiner. === DEEPER Calder's Circus | culture
The Flamingo's maker came from a family of sculptors. Alexander Calder's grandfather made the giant statue of William Penn on top of Philadelphia's City Hall, and his father made the Swann Memorial Fountain a few blocks away. Calder himself trained as a mechanical engineer, and you can see it in everything he built: the balance, the joints, the careful way the weight comes down to the ground.

In Paris in the late 1920s, he became famous for a circus. He built it himself, in miniature, from wire, cloth, cork and string, with acrobats, a lion tamer and a sword swallower, and he performed it on the floor for audiences of artists and friends, working every figure by hand. That's why Chicago welcomed him in 1974 with a circus parade.

His sculptures that moved got their name from Marcel Duchamp, who called them mobiles. When Calder began making big sculptures that stood still, his friend Jean Arp suggested that those must be stabiles, and the name stuck. The Flamingo is a stabile.

It was commissioned by the federal government, under a program that sets aside a small share of the cost of new federal buildings for art. Calder worked out the design in a small model, and the full-size sculpture was cut and bolted together at Segre's Iron Works in Waterbury, Connecticut, which built many of his big stabiles in the 1960s and 1970s.

Walk underneath it. Look at how the curving plates are stiffened with ribs and joined with bolts, more like a bridge than a statue, and at how lightly the whole thing seems to step on the plaza, landing on just a few narrow feet. From each side it's a different creature. Calder died in New York in 1976, two years after the Flamingo went up, and it remains one of his largest works in any public square.
=== STOP Monadnock Building
=== DEEPER Root's Last Brick Tower | architecture
You heard that the owners wanted no ornament. Getting to that plain wall took years of argument. Root's early sketches for this site, in the mid-1880s, dressed the building in an Egyptian style, with carved lotus flowers and flaring columns. The Brooks brothers, writing from Boston, wouldn't have it. Ornament, they believed, only collected dirt and cost money to repair.

The story goes that Root resisted at first, then came back one day and announced that the heavy lines of the Egyptian pyramids had caught his imagination, and that he would put the building up without a single ornament. Many people see a trace of Egypt in it anyway. That hollow flare at the top of the wall is close to the curved cornice that crowns an ancient Egyptian temple.

Brick had a hard limit, and this building found it. When the walls carry the weight, every story added on top means thicker walls at the bottom, and here that thick base swallowed floor space on the most valuable floors, where shops paid the highest rents. The weight was a problem too. Under downtown Chicago lies soft, wet clay, and bedrock was too deep to reach with the methods of the day. So the building stands on what engineers call a floating raft: a broad pad of concrete laced with iron rails and steel beams, spread out to share the load across the clay. The engineers expected the building to settle about eight inches, and planned for it. Over the years it sank about twenty.

Inside is another experiment. The famous staircase is made with cast aluminum, at a time when aluminum was still a costly, almost precious metal, and it's often described as one of the first uses of aluminum in any building.

Root never saw the building finished. He died in January 1891, while the walls were still rising, so in a way the Monadnock is his last word: the old way of building, pushed as far as it would go, and made beautiful by taking things away.
=== DEEPER Four Mountains | names
You heard that the building is named for a mountain in New Hampshire. The name is much older than the building. Monadnock comes from the Abenaki or a related Algonquian language of New England, and it's usually translated as something like the mountain that stands alone, though scholars argue over the exact meaning. The mountain fits it. It rises by itself above the rolling hills of southern New Hampshire, and it became a favorite of New England writers. Ralph Waldo Emerson wrote a long poem about it, and Henry David Thoreau climbed it several times and camped on its bare rocky top. Geologists borrowed the word too. In their language, a monadnock is any isolated hill of hard rock left standing after the land around it has worn away.

The block you're facing was planned as four buildings in a row, each with its own entrance and its own mountain. From north to south, they were the Monadnock, the Kearsarge, the Katahdin and the Wachusett: mountains in New Hampshire, Maine and Massachusetts, all with Native American names. Burnham and Root's half held the first two, and the steel-framed half the other two. Only the first name stuck, and the whole block has been the Monadnock for generations.

The Brooks brothers and their manager liked names like these. Their earlier tower by Burnham and Root, the ten-story Montauk Block, built in 1882 a couple of blocks north of here, is often said to be the first building people called a skyscraper. It was torn down in 1902. Their Pontiac Building of 1891, by Holabird and Roche, still stands a few blocks south on Dearborn, named for the Odawa leader who led a war against the British in the 1760s. And you've already seen their Marquette Building.

There's an irony in all of it. By the time Boston money was naming Chicago towers in Native languages, the Potawatomi, who had lived here when the city began, had been pushed out of the region by treaty half a century before.
=== DEEPER Saved Room by Room | history
By the 1970s, the Monadnock looked like a building without a future. The Loop was losing offices to new towers and to the suburbs, and old buildings all over downtown were coming down for parking garages and plazas. Over the decades, owners had modernized the Monadnock the cheap way. Its hallways had once been lined with marble, and with office walls of oak and textured glass that let daylight from the offices spill into the corridors. Most of that glass and oak had been ripped out or covered over, along with much of the marble, and lowered ceilings hid the old plaster.

In 1979, the building was bought by William Donnell, who made a bet that sounded either visionary or foolish: that people would pay to work in a nineteenth-century office building if it really felt like one. Instead of modernizing further, he set out to bring the Monadnock back to the way it looked in the 1890s, and to make that pay.

Working with the Chicago preservation architects Vinci Hamp, his team restored the building a floor at a time, through most of the 1980s. They brought back the marble along the corridor walls, rebuilt the partitions of oak and glass, opened the hallways back up to their full height, and tucked the modern wiring and ductwork above lowered ceilings inside the offices, where nobody walking the halls would see it. By one account, the work cost about a million dollars a floor.

In 1987, the National Trust for Historic Preservation named it one of the best restoration projects in the country, and the bet paid off. The Monadnock has stayed popular with the kind of tenants it was built for: small firms that like an office behind an oak door, off a marble hallway, in a building with a story.

If the building is open, walk through the ground-floor corridor and look at the glass and the oak and the marble. This is about as close as you can get, anywhere downtown, to the feeling of a Chicago office building when it was new.
=== STOP Sullivan Center
=== DEEPER The Man Who Numbered Chicago | names
You heard that every address in the city counts from this corner. Before 1909, that wasn't so. Chicago had grown by swallowing its neighbors, and each town it annexed brought its own street names and its own house numbers, counted from its own starting points. By the 1890s, the city had dozens of streets sharing the same names, and the same house number could mean very different distances in different parts of town. Deliveries went astray, and visitors were hopelessly lost.

The fix came from a private citizen with no official job at all. His name was Edward P. Brennan, and in 1901 he proposed a single system for the whole city. Take State and Madison as the center. Count every address outward from there. Put eight hundred numbers to the mile, so that a hundred numbers make a standard block and the main streets fall every half mile, on round numbers. Put even numbers on the north and west sides of every street, and odd numbers on the south and east. And give every street a single name from one end to the other.

The city council adopted his plan in 1908. The new numbers took effect across the city in 1909 and in the Loop in 1911, and hundreds of streets were renamed to get rid of the duplicates. Brennan lived until 1942, and today honorary street signs here at State and Madison carry his name.

Here's how to use his gift. Any Chicago address is also a map. Wrigley Field is at ten sixty West Addison, and Addison Street is thirty-six hundred north. So the ballpark is four and a half miles north of where you're standing, and a little more than a mile west. Once you know the trick, you can work out where almost anything in the city is without looking at a map, which is exactly what Brennan had in mind.
=== DEEPER Sullivan's Fall | people
You heard that Sullivan's career collapsed after this store. First, here's how high he had climbed.

Louis Sullivan was born in Boston in 1856, the son of an Irish dancing master. He spent a single year studying architecture at MIT, grew impatient with it, worked briefly for the architect Frank Furness in Philadelphia, and came to Chicago in 1873, to the boom after the fire, where he drafted for William Le Baron Jenney. Then he spent a year at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris, came back, and joined the office of Dankmar Adler, a gifted engineer and a master of acoustics. As Adler and Sullivan, they became one of the most celebrated firms in America.

The turning point came at the World's Fair of 1893. Sullivan designed the Transportation Building, one of the few big buildings at the fair that wasn't white: a long hall in reds and oranges and gold, with an enormous arched entrance called the Golden Door. A French arts society awarded it medals. But the fair's white classical palaces won over the public, and Sullivan never forgave them. Decades later he wrote that the damage wrought by the World's Fair would last for half a century from its date, if not longer.

That same year, a financial panic dried up building across the country. In 1895, Adler left the partnership for a job with an elevator company, and Sullivan, proud, prickly and drinking hard, drove away many of the clients who were left.

What he built in his last decades were small banks in small Midwestern towns, beginning with one in Owatonna, Minnesota, in 1908, which is still a bank. People call them jewel boxes. In his last years, living in a cheap hotel on the South Side, he wrote The Autobiography of an Idea, the story of his life told in the third person, and drew a set of plates called A System of Architectural Ornament. He lived just long enough to see both books in print, and died in April 1924.
=== DEEPER Under State Street | underfoot
Beneath your feet is a hidden city, built in layers.

The deepest layer is about forty feet down. Starting in 1899, a company with a franchise to lay telephone cables dug a network of tunnels under the Loop, far bigger than any telephone cable needed. Soon it was running a narrow-gauge railroad through them, on tracks two feet apart, with little electric locomotives. Its trains carried coal to the basements of office buildings and department stores, hauled away their ashes, and moved freight to and from the railroad stations, with branches running right into store basements along State Street. At their peak, the tunnels ran for close to sixty miles. Trucks finally beat them, the railroad shut down in 1959, and the tunnels were simply left behind.

They came back to life in April 1992. The autumn before, a crew replacing the wooden pilings that protect a bridge on the Chicago River at Kinzie Street had driven a new piling too close to an old tunnel running under the riverbed, and cracked it. An inspector found the leak and estimated a repair at about ten thousand dollars, but the job was still waiting on bids when, on April 13, the crack gave way. River water poured into the tunnels and from there into basements all over the Loop. One of the first signs was fish swimming in the basement of the Merchandise Mart. Downtown was evacuated, the power was cut, and parts of the subway were closed for weeks. Estimates of the total cost ran as high as two billion dollars, and Mayor Richard M. Daley forced out the acting transportation commissioner and fired other city officials.

The middle layer is the subway. The State Street subway opened in October 1943, in the middle of the Second World War, and under this stretch of State its stations share one continuous platform more than half a mile long, often called the longest subway platform in the world. And alongside it runs the Pedway, a network of walkways under and between downtown buildings. It began in 1951 with a tunnel between two subway stations, and it now runs for about five miles. In winter, some downtown workers barely come up at all.
=== STOP Reliance Building
=== DEEPER A Skyscraper Built Under Its Tenants | architecture
The Reliance went up in one of the strangest ways any skyscraper ever has: from the bottom, underneath people who were still at work upstairs.

The man behind it was William Ellery Hale, who had made a fortune building hydraulic elevators, the machines that made tall buildings practical in the first place. In the late 1880s he owned an older building on this corner and wanted something much taller. The trouble was the tenants on the upper floors, whose leases ran until 1894 and couldn't be broken.

So in 1890, Hale's builders propped up the top floors of the old building while they tore out the bottom and built a new base underneath it: the stone-faced ground floor and the basement that John Root designed. Upstairs, people went on working. When the last leases finally ran out in 1894, the old upper floors came down, and Charles Atwood's tower rose on Root's base. Its steel frame went up in a matter of weeks.

Hale's son, George Ellery Hale, grew up to be one of the great astronomers of his age. He built his first observatory at the family home on the South Side, then spent his life persuading rich men to pay for bigger and bigger telescopes. You'll hear about one of those men at the Chicago Theatre, at the end of this tour.

Atwood's own story is a sad one. He was one of the most gifted designers of his time, and the man behind some of the finest buildings at the 1893 fair. But in December 1895, just after this tower was finished, he left Burnham's firm amid stories of illness and erratic behavior that have never been fully explained, and nine days later he was dead, at forty-six.

The building lost its crowning cornice in the 1940s and spent decades in decline. In 1994 the city bought it to save it. A new cornice of cast aluminum went up in place of the old one, and in 1999 the building opened as a hotel.
=== DEEPER Give the Lady What She Wants | companies
Marshall Field was a farm boy from Conway, Massachusetts, who came to Chicago in 1856, at twenty-one, to clerk in a dry goods house. He was quiet, careful and famously thrifty, and he was very good at the one thing that mattered most in his business: knowing what customers would buy. In 1865 he went into partnership with Potter Palmer and Levi Leiter. Palmer soon left to build up State Street, and in 1881 Field bought out Leiter, and the store became Marshall Field and Company.

There's a famous story about where the store's motto came from. Field was walking the floor one day and found a manager arguing with a woman over a complaint. What are you doing, Field asked. Settling a complaint, said the manager. No, you're not, Field told him. Give the lady what she wants. Nobody can prove it happened, but the line became the store's slogan.

The man who did the most to turn that idea into a show was Harry Gordon Selfridge, who started at Field's as a stock boy in 1879 and rose to run the retail store. He filled the windows with light, brought goods out where shoppers could touch them, opened a bargain basement, and is sometimes credited with the phrase the customer is always right. In 1904, he left to buy a rival, Schlesinger and Mayer, in Sullivan's new building at State and Madison. After about two months, he sold it to Carson Pirie Scott, which kept the store there until 2007.

Then Selfridge went to London, where in 1909 he opened the department store Selfridges on Oxford Street, and London had never seen anything like it. He lived grandly and gambled heavily, lost control of the store in 1941, and died with little left.

Field died in 1906, the richest man in Chicago. His store kept his name for a century, passing in 1982 to BATUS, the American arm of British American Tobacco, and then through a series of other owners before it became Macy's.
=== DEEPER Under the Clock | culture
Not many department store clocks have made the cover of a national magazine. In November 1945, just after the war, the Saturday Evening Post ran a cover by Norman Rockwell inspired by the Field's clocks: a repairman on a ladder beside the giant clock face, pocket watch in hand, setting the time. It became one of the best-loved pictures of the store.

The mints have a stranger history than you might guess. Frango Mints didn't start in Chicago at all. They came from Frederick and Nelson, a department store in Seattle, which Marshall Field's bought in 1929. Field's began making them here, in a candy kitchen on the thirteenth floor of the store across the street, and kept making them there for about seventy years.

Why Frango? Nobody is quite sure. A popular story says the candy was first called Franco, and that the name was quietly changed when Francisco Franco seized power in Spain. It's a good story, but there's no solid evidence for it. Since 2017, the mints have been made by Garrett, the Chicago popcorn company, and they still come in the familiar green box.

Christmas was the store's other great tradition. Field's turned its State Street windows into elaborate holiday scenes, and generations of children were brought downtown to see them, then taken upstairs for lunch in the Walnut Room, beside the Great Tree, a towering decorated Christmas tree that has stood in the restaurant every holiday season since the early 1900s. For many Chicago families, it was a ritual repeated every December.

When Macy's took over, it kept the windows, the tree and the mints. It may be the main reason some Chicagoans have started to forgive it. Step across the street and look up at the nearest clock, and you'll see why people chose it as a meeting place: you can see it from a block away, it always tells the time, and everybody knows exactly where it is.
=== STOP Daley Plaza & the Picasso
=== DEEPER How Chicago Got Its Picasso | culture
The idea came from the architects of the new Civic Center, as the Daley Center was first called. They wanted a great sculpture for the plaza, and William Hartmann, a partner at Skidmore, Owings and Merrill, set out to get one from the most famous artist alive. In 1963, he went to see Picasso at his home in the south of France, armed with photographs of Chicago and its buildings, and he made return visits. Picasso, by then in his eighties, had never been to America and never would go. But he agreed, and in 1965 he finished a model about forty-two inches tall, which you can now see at the Art Institute.

The money came from three Chicago charitable foundations: the Woods Charitable Fund, the Field Foundation of Illinois, and the Chauncey and Marion Deering McCormick Foundation. The sculpture itself was built at the American Bridge plant of United States Steel in Gary, and trucked to Chicago in pieces.

On August 15, 1967, Mayor Daley pulled the cord that dropped the covering, and told the crowd that what is strange to us today will be familiar tomorrow. Not everyone wanted to wait for tomorrow. An alderman named John Hoellen proposed sending the thing back and putting up a statue of the Cubs star Ernie Banks instead. Banks did eventually get his statue, outside Wrigley Field, in 2008.

Then came a twist that would have amused Picasso. The Public Building Commission, which ran the Civic Center, had tried to copyright the design, so that it could control and sell the souvenirs. But the model and early photographs had been shown in public without a copyright notice, and in 1970 a federal judge ruled that the design had fallen into the public domain. You heard that Picasso gave his design to the people of Chicago. A court made sure of it. Anyone can put the Chicago Picasso on a T-shirt or a coffee mug, and over the years, just about everyone has.
=== DEEPER Miró, Dubuffet and Chagall | look
Daley Plaza is the starting point of one of the best outdoor sculpture walks in America, and most of it is within a few minutes of where you're standing.

You heard about Miró's figure across Washington Street. Its real title is simply Chicago. It stands thirty-nine feet tall, made of concrete, steel, bronze and ceramic tile, and it was unveiled in 1981, when Miró was in his late eighties.

A block north, at Clark and Randolph, in front of the curved glass state office building designed by Helmut Jahn, stands Jean Dubuffet's Monument with Standing Beast, unveiled in 1984: a twenty-nine-foot tangle of white fiberglass outlined in black that you can walk right into. Chicagoans nicknamed it Snoopy in a Blender. The building behind it, long known as the Thompson Center, was bought by Google in 2022.

A few blocks south, at Dearborn and Monroe, is Marc Chagall's Four Seasons, a freestanding wall seventy feet long, fourteen feet high and ten feet wide, covered on all four sides in a mosaic of stone and glass in a great range of colors, with scenes of the seasons and of Chicago. Chagall came to the city for its dedication in 1974. A glass canopy was added in 1994 to protect it from the weather.

Chagall loved Chicago back. A few years later he made the America Windows for the Art Institute, a row of deep blue stained glass celebrating American freedom and the arts, and dedicated them to the memory of Mayor Daley. They were unveiled on May 15, 1977, which would have been Daley's seventy-fifth birthday.

Two more are worth the walk. At Madison and Wells is Louise Nevelson's Dawn Shadows, from 1983, a thicket of black steel that looks different from every side. And just across the river, at six hundred West Madison, Claes Oldenburg's Batcolumn, from 1977, is a lattice baseball bat a hundred feet tall, standing in front of a federal office building. Oldenburg liked to take ordinary things and make them enormous, and in a city of tall columns, he gave Chicago a bat.
=== DEEPER Operation Greylord | politics
The tower behind the Picasso holds the courtrooms of Cook County, and in the early 1980s it was the scene of the biggest investigation of judicial corruption in American history.

For years, lawyers had known that some of the county's judges were for sale. Cases could be fixed for a price, with the money passed through court clerks, police officers or lawyers who acted as bagmen. In 1980, the FBI and the United States attorney's office began an undercover operation to prove it. They called it Greylord, after the curled gray wigs worn by English judges.

The key undercover agent was a young prosecutor named Terrence Hake, who pretended to be a corrupt prosecutor and later a crooked defense lawyer, and spent years paying bribes while wearing a hidden recorder. FBI agents posed as defendants in fake cases invented just for the investigation, so that there would be something to fix. A judge from downstate Illinois, Brocton Lockwood, who was assigned to traffic court here, agreed to wear a wire as well. And for the first time, the FBI won permission to plant a bug in a judge's chambers.

When the investigation became public in 1983, it was a sensation. In all, ninety-two people were indicted, among them seventeen judges and forty-eight lawyers, along with police officers, deputy sheriffs and court clerks, and nearly all of them were convicted. A later case was even darker: in 1994, a former judge named Thomas Maloney was sent to prison for taking bribes to fix murder trials.

Corruption didn't stop at the courthouse. By the count of Dick Simpson, a former alderman who became a political scientist, more than thirty Chicago aldermen have been convicted of crimes since the 1970s. The most famous is Ed Burke, who served on the city council for fifty-four years, longer than anyone in its history. In December 2023, a federal jury convicted him of racketeering, bribery and attempted extortion, and in 2024 he was sentenced to two years in prison. Chicagoans have a saying for all this, borrowed from an old alderman: Chicago ain't ready for reform.
=== STOP Chicago Theatre
=== DEEPER Balaban and Katz | companies
You heard that Balaban and Katz turned the movies into an event. They started on the West Side, in a family of Jewish immigrants who ran a small grocery. The Balaban brothers went into the picture business when it was still nickelodeons, and they teamed up with a sharp young businessman named Sam Katz.

Their first big hit was the Central Park Theatre on Roosevelt Road, which opened in 1917 and is often called one of the first movie theatres anywhere with mechanical cooling. Barney Balaban had worked for a cold storage company, and he knew how refrigeration worked. Then came the Riviera, the Tivoli, and this theatre in 1921, each grander than the last.

They had a formula, and it worked. Build next to the L or a streetcar line, so customers could ride to the door. Make the building a palace, so that a factory worker could feel like a king for the price of a ticket. Give a full show: a feature film, live stage acts and an orchestra. And hire ushers in uniform, drilled like soldiers to be polite to every customer, whoever they were.

The music was part of the draw. In the 1920s, the organist Jesse Crawford, known as the Poet of the Organ, played the theatre's mighty Wurlitzer and became a national star through records and radio. The organ is still here.

In 1925, Balaban and Katz joined forces with Paramount, the Hollywood studio. Sam Katz went to New York to run Paramount's theatres, and Barney Balaban later became president of Paramount Pictures, a job he held from 1936 to 1964. The family that started in a West Side grocery ended up running one of the biggest studios in Hollywood.

And when the restored theatre reopened in September 1986, the star of the opening night gala was Frank Sinatra, singing on the stage where the Balabans' stage shows had once played between the pictures.
=== DEEPER The Traction King | people
Charles Tyson Yerkes came to Chicago with a prison record. He'd been a successful young financier in Philadelphia, handling the city's bonds, until the Chicago Fire of 1871 set off a panic in the markets. Yerkes couldn't cover the city money he'd been using in his own speculations. He was convicted and sentenced to two years and nine months in prison, and was pardoned after seven months.

In the early 1880s he moved to Chicago and set out to control its streetcars. He took over the main lines on the North and West Sides, replaced the horses first with cable cars and then with electric trolleys, and ran them for profit above all else. The cars were packed, and when riders complained, he's often quoted as saying that it's the strap-hangers who pay the dividends.

His great gamble came in 1897. Yerkes got the state legislature to pass a law allowing Chicago to grant streetcar franchises for as long as fifty years, and then he asked the city council for exactly that. Mayor Carter Harrison Junior fought him, the newspapers fought him, and in December 1898, with angry crowds around City Hall, some of them reportedly carrying nooses, the council turned him down. The law was repealed the next year, and Yerkes sold out and left for London.

He left something better behind. In 1892, the young astronomer George Ellery Hale and the president of the new University of Chicago, William Rainey Harper, persuaded Yerkes to pay for the biggest telescope in the world. The Yerkes Observatory opened in 1897 on Geneva Lake in Wisconsin, with a lens forty inches across. It's still the largest refracting telescope ever used for astronomy. The university closed the observatory in 2018, and it reopened under a new foundation in 2022.

The novelist Theodore Dreiser turned Yerkes into Frank Cowperwood, the hero of three novels: The Financier, The Titan and The Stoic. Yerkes himself died in New York at the end of 1905, before most of his London lines had opened.
=== DEEPER The Loop Above Your Head | history
Chicago's first L didn't run over a street at all. In 1892, the South Side line opened, running above the alleys behind the buildings, which is why people called it the Alley L. Under Illinois law, a railway built over a street needed the consent of most of the property owners along it. An alley needed nobody's permission.

More lines followed, but they all stopped at the edge of downtown, because downtown property owners didn't want trains roaring past their windows. Riders had to get off and walk, or change to a streetcar. The answer was to build a ring of track around the business district and tie all the lines together, and in 1897 the Union Loop opened.

To see what the stations looked like then, visit Quincy station, on Wells Street near where this tour began. It opened in 1897 and was restored in the late 1980s to look much as it did in the 1890s.

The Loop has had its dark days. On the evening of February 4, 1977, at the sharp curve at Lake and Wabash, one block east of here, a train ran into the back of another, and cars tumbled off the structure to the street below. Eleven people were killed and more than a hundred and eighty were hurt, the deadliest accident in the system's history. Federal investigators blamed the motorman of the second train.

By then, many planners wanted the elevated Loop torn down altogether. In the 1970s, the city drew up plans to replace it with new subways, including one under Franklin Street. But costs climbed, the plans were fought over for years, and in 1979 the city and state dropped the Franklin Street subway and moved the money to other projects. The old Loop survived.

Today it's hard to imagine downtown without it: the shade and the rumble, the sparks at the corners, and the long view down Wabash or Wells, under the steel, with the sunlight coming through in stripes. More than a century after Yerkes, it's still the thing that holds the city's train lines together.
