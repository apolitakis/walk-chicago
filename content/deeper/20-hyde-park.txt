=== DEEPER Missiles on the Point | history
For almost twenty years, this peaceful meadow was part of the nation's defense against nuclear attack.

In the early 1950s, as the Cold War deepened, the Army began ringing American cities with Nike missile batteries, meant to shoot down Soviet bombers before they could reach their targets. Chicago and its industrial suburbs were surrounded by them, and one of them was right here. Each battery came in two parts: a launch area, where the missiles were kept and fired, and a control area, with radar to find the bombers and steer the missiles to them. In Hyde Park, the launchers went into a meadow in Jackson Park, south of here near the 63rd Street beach, and the radar went onto the Point.

Picture it: behind fences and barbed wire, south of the field house, a cluster of radar towers, the tallest about a hundred and fifty feet high, sweeping the sky over the lake. In time, the first Nike Ajax missiles gave way to the bigger Nike Hercules, which could carry nuclear warheads.

Hyde Park did not take it quietly. In 1955, members of the Hyde Park-Kenwood Community Conference protested the radar center on the Point. In 1961, a local branch of the Committee for a Sane Nuclear Policy protested plans to enlarge it. During the Vietnam War the protests grew louder, and the neighborhood never stopped treating the base as an intruder in its favorite park.

By then, long-range rockets had replaced bombers as the real danger, and the batteries were growing obsolete. The Army gave up its lease, and in 1971 the towers came down. Hyde Parkers celebrated with a victory rally.

A few traces may linger underground. In recent years, Army engineers have dug at the Point, searching for fuel tanks the base might have left behind.
=== DEEPER The Parrots of Hyde Park | nature
Listen for loud, raucous chatter in the trees, and look for a flash of bright green. Those are monk parakeets, small parrots from South America, and they've been living wild in Hyde Park for about half a century.

They come from the temperate south of that continent, from Argentina and its neighbors, where winters can turn cold. That's part of the reason they can survive here, where most escaped tropical birds die in their first winter. The other reason is their building skill. Monk parakeets are the only parrots that build their own nests, out of sticks, and they build them together, as big, bulky communal nests with separate chambers for different pairs. They often put them on light poles and power equipment, which helps keep them warm, and doesn't make them popular with the electric company.

Nobody knows exactly how they got here. The first ones were noticed in Hyde Park in 1973. One story says a shipment of them escaped at O'Hare Airport. Another says they were part of a university experiment. Most likely, they were pets that got loose or were set free.

Their most famous neighbor was Harold Washington, Chicago's first Black mayor, whose story is told on the South Loop tour. He lived across the street from the best-known colony, near 53rd Street and the lake, and he was fond of the birds. He called them his good luck talisman.

That friendship came to matter. In 1988, a few months after Washington died in office, the United States Department of Agriculture announced plans to destroy the colony, because in South America monk parakeets are a serious pest of crops. Hyde Parkers rose up in the birds' defense and threatened to sue, and the plan was dropped.

By the 1990s, the colony had grown to more than two hundred birds. Their numbers here have fallen since, as monk parakeets have spread across the city and into the suburbs. But Hyde Park still thinks of them as its own.
=== DEEPER A Fountain for Men and Beasts | look
As you come or go through the 55th Street underpass, look for a small fountain beside the Lakefront Trail, just east of the tunnel. A bronze fawn rests on top of a polished marble base. This is the David Wallach Memorial Fountain, and it took almost half a century to arrive.

Very little is known about David Wallach. When he died in 1894, he left the city money for a fountain for men and beasts. In the Chicago of his day, the beasts were mostly horses, the many thousands of animals that pulled the city's wagons and carriages and needed water on hot days. Fountains with troughs for horses stood all over American cities.

But Wallach's fountain wasn't built. Years passed, then decades, and the horses gave way to automobiles. In the 1930s, his heirs pressed the city to finally spend the money. The new Chicago Park District, formed in 1934 by merging the city's many separate park boards, set up a committee with the family and held a design competition.

The winners were a husband and wife, Frederick Hibbard and Elisabeth Haseltine Hibbard. He designed the marble fountain. She sculpted the fawn, and she modeled it on a young deer she had watched at the Lincoln Park Zoo. The fountain was dedicated in 1939, the same year the benches and boulders were set in place out on the Point.

Look low on the fountain, and you'll see that the beasts weren't forgotten. The Hibbards included places for birds and dogs to drink, so the fountain still serves men and beasts, just smaller ones than Wallach had in mind.

The fawn has had an adventure of its own. In 1981, it was stolen. It turned up in a warehouse on the West Side after an anonymous tip to the local alderman, and it went back on its perch. And if you're ever in Davenport, Iowa, you can visit its twin: an exact copy of Elisabeth Hibbard's fawn rests in a cemetery there.

=== DEEPER The Capture | history
You heard that the U-505 was captured by the Navy in 1944. How it happened is one of the great sea stories of the Second World War.

The man behind it was Captain Daniel Gallery, a Chicago native, who commanded the escort carrier Guadalcanal and a group of small warships hunting U-boats in the Atlantic. Gallery had noticed that the crews of crippled submarines tended to abandon ship in a hurry, sometimes leaving their boats afloat for a few minutes. So he trained boarding parties, in case his ships ever got the chance to grab one.

On June fourth, 1944, off the coast of West Africa, his group found the U-505 and hammered her with depth charges. The submarine surfaced, and her crew, under fire, jumped into the sea. They left her circling, with water pouring in through a valve they had opened to sink her.

Eight men from the destroyer escort Pillsbury, led by Lieutenant Junior Grade Albert David, leapt aboard, with no idea whether she was rigged to blow up. They found the leak and stopped it, and they gathered up codebooks and an Enigma cipher machine. David received the Medal of Honor. It was the first time the United States Navy had captured an enemy warship on the high seas since the War of 1812.

Then came the hard part, keeping it secret. If Germany learned its codes had been taken, it would change them. The prize was towed to Bermuda under an American cover name, Nemo, and the code material went on to the British codebreakers at Bletchley Park. The fifty-eight German survivors were held apart, at a camp near Ruston, Louisiana, so that word wouldn't get back to Germany.

After the war, Gallery's brother, a Chicago priest named Father John Gallery, came up with the idea of bringing the boat here. Chicagoans raised about a quarter of a million dollars to tow it through the Great Lakes and haul it across Lake Shore Drive, and on September twenty-fifth, 1954, it became part of the museum.
=== DEEPER The Zephyr | companies
In an underground hall in front of this museum stands a silver train that changed American railroading in a single day.

It's the Pioneer Zephyr, built for the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad, known to everyone as the Burlington. In the early 1930s, the Depression and the automobile were draining passengers from the rails, and the Burlington's president, Ralph Budd, wanted something new: a light, fast, streamlined train that would win them back.

He found the right partners. The Budd Company of Philadelphia, founded by Edward Budd, no relation, built the body of stainless steel, a metal many engineers thought couldn't be joined strongly enough for a railroad car. Budd's engineers solved that with a patented electric welding method they called shotwelding. General Motors supplied the power, a compact new diesel engine of six hundred horsepower. The result was the first diesel-powered streamliner: three short cars joined together, with a gleaming, fluted skin and a rounded nose.

Ralph Budd named it. He had been reading Chaucer's Canterbury Tales, the story goes, and he chose Zephyr, after Zephyrus, the ancient Greek god of the west wind.

Then he showed it off. On May twenty-sixth, 1934, the Zephyr left Denver at dawn and ran without a stop to Chicago, one thousand and fifteen miles, in about thirteen hours and five minutes. It averaged seventy-seven miles an hour and touched a hundred and twelve, taking roughly half the time of the regular trains. That evening it rolled onto the stage of a transportation pageant at the Century of Progress fair, and later that year it played the title role in a Hollywood thriller, The Silver Streak.

Streamliners soon spread across the country, and within about twenty-five years diesels had driven steam from America's main lines. The Zephyr itself carried passengers for twenty-six years and some three million miles before the Burlington gave it to the museum in 1960. It stood outdoors for decades, and in 1998, freshly restored, it was lowered into its home underground.
=== DEEPER The Perfect Crime | people
You heard that Clarence Darrow kept Nathan Leopold and Richard Loeb from the gallows. Here is how they were caught, and what became of them.

They were nineteen and eighteen, sons of millionaires, and both had already finished college. Leopold was a prodigy, said to speak many languages, and an expert on birds. The two believed they were superior beings, above the rules that bound everyone else, and they planned the crime for months.

On May twenty-first, 1924, they lured Bobby Franks into a rented car and killed him. They hid his body in a culvert near Wolf Lake, at the far southeastern edge of the city, and sent his father a typed note demanding ten thousand dollars.

Their perfect crime came apart within days. Near the culvert, the police found a pair of horn-rimmed glasses. They were ordinary, except for an unusual hinge. Only one Chicago firm, the opticians Almer Coe and Company, sold frames with that hinge, and just three pairs had been sold. One belonged to Nathan Leopold.

Leopold said he must have dropped them while bird-watching, and the pair claimed they had spent that evening driving around in Leopold's car with two girls. Then the Leopold family's chauffeur told police the car had been in the garage all day. The ransom note was traced to a portable typewriter Leopold had used. The two had pried off its keys and thrown it from a bridge into a lagoon here in Jackson Park, and divers brought it up. Both young men confessed.

Judge John Caverly sentenced each of them to life in prison for the murder, plus ninety-nine years for the kidnapping. In 1936, Loeb was slashed to death with a razor by another inmate at Stateville prison. Leopold served thirty-three years. He reorganized the prison library, taught other inmates, and volunteered to be infected with malaria for a wartime medical study. Paroled in 1958, he moved to Puerto Rico, worked at a church-run hospital in the mountains, married, and died there in 1971.

=== DEEPER The Electric Fair | companies
At night in 1893, people said, this park blazed with more electric light than anyone had ever seen in one place. Behind that glow was one of the great business battles of the age, and this fair was its turning point.

Two systems were fighting to electrify America. Thomas Edison's companies sold direct current, which worked well over short distances. George Westinghouse, a Pittsburgh inventor and manufacturer, backed alternating current, which could be sent long distances far more cheaply, using motors and a system patented by the brilliant Serbian-American inventor Nikola Tesla. Edison's side warned that alternating current was deadly, and the fight got ugly. People called it the War of the Currents.

The fair needed more electric power than any event in history. General Electric, the giant newly formed out of Edison's company and a rival, bid for the contract. Westinghouse bid far lower, at a loss by some accounts, and won.

Edison's side struck back by refusing to let Westinghouse use the Edison light bulb, which was protected by patent. So, by the usual account, Westinghouse's engineers designed a different bulb, in two pieces sealed with a glass stopper, and his factories turned out the many thousands the fair needed in a matter of months.

On May first, 1893, President Grover Cleveland pressed a golden telegraph key, and the fair's engines and fountains came to life. After dark, the buildings were outlined in lights, and searchlights swept the sky. In the Electricity Building, the Westinghouse exhibit showed off Tesla's system, including a copper egg that spun and stood up on its end, driven by an invisible rotating magnetic field. It was called the Egg of Columbus, after the old story of Columbus making an egg stand on end.

The fair was a triumph for alternating current. Westinghouse went on to build the great power plant at Niagara Falls on Tesla's system, and in 1896 its electricity began flowing to the city of Buffalo. The power in your home today is alternating current.
=== DEEPER The White City Burns | history
You heard that the White City didn't survive the fair for long. The way much of it ended was one last spectacle.

The buildings were never meant to last. Under their white skins of staff were frames of wood and iron. When the fair closed at the end of October 1893, the country was sliding into one of the worst depressions in its history, and there was neither the money nor a plan to keep the palaces in use. They stood empty through the winter.

On January eighth, 1894, fire broke out at the east end of the Court of Honor. It destroyed the Peristyle, the great colonnade along the lake, and the buildings beside it. The heat, it was said, melted the ice on the Grand Basin, yet the golden Republic, standing in the basin, came through almost untouched.

The worst fire came that summer. On July fifth, 1894, Chicago was in turmoil. The Pullman strike had stopped much of the nation's railroad traffic, and federal troops had just arrived in the city. That evening, flames swept through what was left of the Court of Honor. The Manufactures and Liberal Arts Building, said to be the largest building in the world when it went up, burned along with others around the basin, while crowds watched from the lakeshore. Many suspected arson, and some blamed the strikers, but nobody ever proved who set it.

The Republic outlasted nearly everything around her. But she was made of staff too, under her gold leaf, and she was falling apart. On August twenty-eighth, 1896, by order of the park commissioners, she was pulled down and set on fire. The colossus that had stood over the fair was gone in about thirty-six minutes.

That's why the statue you see today is bronze, and smaller. Her gold wears thin over the years. She was last regilded in 1993, for the fair's hundredth anniversary, and in 2026 restorers began covering her again, applying the gold leaf by hand, piece by piece.
=== DEEPER The Senator's Sanctuary | names
Officially, the southern part of this island has another name. Since 1977, the area south of the Japanese garden has been the Paul H. Douglas Nature Sanctuary.

It honors one of the most remarkable people ever to live in Hyde Park. Paul Douglas came to the University of Chicago in 1920 to teach economics. With a mathematician named Charles Cobb, he worked out a formula for how labor and capital combine to make goods, the Cobb-Douglas function, which economics students still learn.

He was a reformer, too. In 1939 he was elected alderman for the Hyde Park ward, and on the City Council he was one of the few voices against the Kelly-Nash machine. Then came Pearl Harbor. Douglas, a Quaker who had long opposed war, decided that this one had to be fought. He fought on Peleliu and Okinawa, where machine-gun fire tore into his left arm, and he never regained its full use.

In 1948, Illinois elected him to the United States Senate, where he served eighteen years. The sanctuary came from his neighbors. In 1974, a group of local birders began leading regular walks on the island, partly to reclaim it from the gangs that had made people afraid to come. A year after Douglas died, they persuaded the Park District to set this end of the island aside for wildlife, and to name it for the senator who loved wild places.

The walks are still going, half a century later, and there's plenty to see. Jackson Park lies on a great migration route along the lakeshore, and birders have reported more than three hundred species here. On one day in May, a single birder counted thirty-four kinds of warblers.

=== DEEPER The Tower and Its Words | architecture
The Obama Presidential Center opened to the public on June nineteenth, 2026, after years of delay. Here's a closer look at what the architects built.

It isn't one building but a campus of several, spread across about nineteen acres at the western edge of Jackson Park, joined by gardens, a plaza, and paths leading toward the lagoons. The tallest building is the museum. Its walls are a rough, mottled granite called Tapestry, from a quarry in New Hampshire, and the former president chose the stone himself.

Look up at the top of the tower. The stone gives way to huge letters, cast in concrete, each about five feet tall, wrapping around the corner. They spell out a passage from the speech Obama gave in 2015 at Selma, Alabama, on the fiftieth anniversary of the march across the Edmund Pettus Bridge, the passage that begins with the words, You are America. From the ground the letters are hard to read, and the designers expected that. The words are meant to work two ways: as a text, if you take the time, and as a pattern, if you don't.

Near the top is a room called the Sky Room, with views across the South Side, and the museum inside includes a replica of the Oval Office. The campus also has spaces meant for the neighborhood as much as for visitors: a full-size basketball court called the Home Court, and a branch of the Chicago Public Library.

What it doesn't have is an archive. Every presidential library since Franklin Roosevelt's has held the president's papers in a building run by the National Archives. Not this one. Most of the Obama administration's records were digital from the start, and the foundation paid to digitize the rest, so researchers read them online. The paper records and the artifacts stay with the National Archives near Washington, which lends some of them for display here. The Archives calls it the first all-digital presidential library.
=== DEEPER See Your Food | food
A mile north of here, on 53rd Street, is a plain cafeteria with a sign out front that gives an order: See Your Food. This is Valois, and it has been feeding Hyde Park since 1921.

The idea is right there in the slogan. You take a tray, move down the line, point at what you want, and watch it land on your plate. Prices are low, portions are generous, and the room is loud. It's one of the few places where the whole neighborhood eats together: professors and laborers, students and retirees, old-timers and newcomers.

In the 1980s, a sociology graduate student spent four years at its tables, getting to know a group of Black working men who gathered there every day around a regular called Slim. His book, Slim's Table, came out in the early 1990s and became a classic. It argued that men like these, hardworking, proud, and deeply respectable in the old-fashioned sense, were missing from the picture most Americans had of Black men in the city, a picture drawn from crime stories and studies of the poorest blocks.

Barack Obama ate here for years, from his days as a community organizer on. After he became president, the manager said Valois had long been one of his favorite places, and a board on the wall listed the president's usual orders. When he won in 2008, Hyde Parkers celebrated with what were called victory breakfasts, and in 2014, back in town for a fundraiser, the president came in for breakfast himself.

The food hasn't changed much over the decades, and neither has the sign. In a neighborhood that has argued about almost everything, the instruction over the door may be the one thing everybody follows.
=== DEEPER The Race He Lost | politics
You heard about Obama's wins in 1996 and 2004. In between came the only election he ever lost, and it was fought right here on the South Side.

In 1999, Obama, then a little-known state senator, decided to challenge this district's congressman, Bobby Rush, in the Democratic primary. Rush had helped found the Black Panther Party in Illinois, then turned to electoral politics, served on the City Council, and won his seat in Congress in 1992. Early in 1999 he had run for mayor against Richard M. Daley and lost badly, and Obama thought he looked beatable.

It went wrong almost from the start. That October, Rush's son Huey was shot and killed, and sympathy gathered around the grieving father. In December, Obama stayed in Hawaii with his sick baby daughter and missed a close vote in Springfield on a gun-control bill, and he was harshly criticized for it.

Rush's campaign had a simple message about the Harvard-trained law lecturer from Hyde Park: he isn't one of us. Rush mocked Obama's elite schooling, and the attack worked. On March twenty-first, 2000, Rush beat him by about two to one.

Obama later wrote about how low he felt. That summer, he flew to Los Angeles for the Democratic convention, only to have his credit card turned down at the rental car counter, and he couldn't get a pass to the convention floor. He flew home early, in debt and wondering whether to quit politics.

He didn't quit. He learned to campaign in churches and barbershops as well as lecture halls, and to build support across the city. When a United States Senate seat opened four years later, he ran a far better campaign.

The two men made their peace. Rush later backed Obama for president and liked to joke about having beaten him. He stayed in Congress for thirty years, until 2023.

=== DEEPER The Hoochie Coochie | culture
The man who made the Midway pay was twenty-three years old. His name was Sol Bloom.

Bloom had grown up poor in San Francisco and worked his way into theater management. At the Paris world's fair of 1889, he saw a troupe of performers from Algeria and bought the rights to show them. When the Chicago fair's planners found that the Midway wasn't working as a dignified museum of the world's peoples, they put Bloom in charge, and he turned it into a mile of shows.

The most notorious of them was a dance. In the Algerian village, the Persian palace, and above all the Street in Cairo, women performed dances from North Africa and the Middle East, with movements of the hips and stomach that American audiences had never seen in public. It was billed as the belly dance. Respectable Chicago was scandalized, and so, of course, everyone went.

Bloom knew how to get attention. As he told it, when he was asked to describe the dancing at the Chicago Press Club, he sat down at a piano and picked out a little melody to go with it. You know it, even if you don't know its name: it's the snake charmer's tune from countless cartoons, the one children still sing in schoolyards with silly words. Bloom never copyrighted it, and whether he made it up or borrowed an old folk tune is still argued over. The dance came to be called the hoochie coochie, and within a few years it was in every carnival sideshow in the country.

And Little Egypt? Several dancers later claimed to be the original, and historians still can't say for sure which woman, if any, danced under that name on the Midway in 1893. The name grew famous afterward, carried from carnival to carnival.

As for Bloom, he went into music publishing, moved to New York, and served in Congress for twenty-six years, ending as chairman of the House Foreign Affairs Committee. In 1945, he was one of the American delegates at the conference that founded the United Nations.
=== DEEPER A Ladder of Peoples | history
Walking this mile in 1893 was meant to teach a lesson, and not a pretty one.

The fair's department of ethnology was run by Frederic Ward Putnam, a Harvard archaeologist. He imagined the Midway as a living outdoor museum, where visitors could see the peoples of the world and measure humanity's progress toward the civilization on display in the White City. In the end, most of the villages were commercial shows run by promoters for profit, but the layout told Putnam's story anyway. Nearest the White City, at the eastern end, stood the German and Irish villages. Farther west came the bazaars and villages of the Middle East and Asia, and near the far end, the village of Dahomey, presented as the most primitive of all. Historians have called it a sliding scale of humanity.

The people inside the exhibits were real, and their lives were more complicated than the signs. The performers from Dahomey, a West African kingdom then at war with France, were mocked mercilessly in the press. In the Eskimo village, Inuit families from Labrador were reportedly made to wear heavy furs in the summer heat. Some of them went to court over their treatment and set up a village of their own outside the gates.

One of those families had a baby girl in January 1893, before the fair even opened. She was named Nancy Columbia, partly for the fair and partly for Bertha Palmer, its leading hostess, and she spent her childhood on display at one world's fair after another. She grew up to be a performer and a writer, and she died in Los Angeles in 1959.

Here's the twist. Putnam's chief assistant at the fair was a young German immigrant named Franz Boas. Boas became the most influential anthropologist in America, and he spent his career demolishing the very idea of a ladder of races. Cultures, he argued, had to be understood on their own terms, and differences between peoples came from history and circumstance, not biology.
=== DEEPER The Murder Castle | legends
You heard that during the fair, a con man named H. H. Holmes was killing people in Englewood. His story has grown into Chicago's darkest legend, so it's worth separating what's documented from what isn't.

His real name was Herman Webster Mudgett. He had trained in medicine in Michigan, but his real trade was fraud. In the 1880s he came to Englewood, took over a drugstore at 63rd and Wallace Streets, and put up a three-story building with shops below and rooms above, which he rented to visitors during the fair. Neighbors called it the Castle.

What's documented is bad enough. Holmes was a bigamist, a forger, and an insurance swindler. After his arrest in 1894, investigators found that he had murdered his business partner, Benjamin Pitezel, in Philadelphia, and then three of Pitezel's children, whom he had taken on a long, wandering journey. A Philadelphia detective, Frank Geyer, tracked their remains to Indianapolis and Toronto. Several women who had known Holmes in Chicago had vanished, and when police searched the Castle in 1895, they reported finding human bones.

Holmes was convicted of Pitezel's murder and hanged in Philadelphia in May 1896. Before he died, he sold the newspapers a confession to twenty-seven murders. Some of the people he named turned out to be alive.

The rest is legend. The secret passages, gas chambers, and chutes for bodies were largely the creation of the newspapers of 1895, which competed to make the Castle as lurid as possible. Claims that he killed two hundred people, many of them fairgoers, have nothing behind them. Historians can tie him with confidence to fewer than ten deaths, and it isn't clear that more than one of his victims came to Chicago for the fair.

The legend got its biggest boost in 2003, from Erik Larson's bestseller The Devil in the White City. The Castle itself stood as an ordinary commercial building until 1938, when it was torn down. A post office occupies the site today.

=== DEEPER The City as a Laboratory | history
You heard that the university's sociologists studied Chicago itself. For a generation, they more or less invented the way the world studies cities, and the city they used was this one.

The central figure was Robert Park, who joined the faculty in 1914, at about fifty. He was no ordinary professor. He had been a newspaper reporter for years, and then a writer and publicist for Booker T. Washington at the Tuskegee Institute in Alabama. He wanted his students out of the library. Go sit in the lobbies of the luxury hotels and on the doorsteps of the flophouses, he is said to have told them, and get the seat of your pants dirty in real research.

They did. Park's colleague Ernest Burgess drew a famous diagram of the city as a set of rings: the business district at the center, then a zone of rooming houses and factories, then workers' homes, and finally the commuters' suburbs, with each new wave of arrivals pushing outward. It was based on Chicago, and it shaped the study of cities for decades.

Their students fanned out. Nels Anderson, who had ridden the rails himself, wrote The Hobo, about the homeless men of West Madison Street. Frederic Thrasher spent years tracking down one thousand three hundred and thirteen youth gangs for his book The Gang. Louis Wirth wrote about the old Jewish quarter around Maxwell Street, and others studied taxi-dance halls, delinquent boys, and the Gold Coast and the slum beside it.

The Chicago school had blind spots. It tended to treat neighborhoods like natural ecosystems, with groups competing for space the way plants do, and it said too little about the deliberate choices, by landlords, banks, real estate agents, and the city itself, that kept Black Chicagoans penned into a narrow strip of the South Side. Later scholars, some trained here, put that at the center of their work.

But the habit Park taught, of walking the streets and listening to people, became the heart of modern sociology.
=== DEEPER The Chicago Boys | politics
You heard that the university's economists, the Chicago School, transformed economic policy. Nowhere did they do it more directly, or more controversially, than in Chile.

It began in the mid-1950s with a program paid for by the United States government, meant to steer Latin American economics away from state planning and toward free markets. The university's economics department signed an agreement with the Catholic University of Chile, in Santiago, and over the next two decades, dozens of young Chilean economists came here to study under Milton Friedman, Arnold Harberger, and their colleagues. At home they became known as the Chicago Boys.

For years their ideas went nowhere. Then, in September 1973, the military overthrew Chile's elected socialist president, Salvador Allende, who died during the coup. The new dictator, General Augusto Pinochet, took over an economy in chaos, with prices rising by hundreds of percent a year. The Chicago-trained economists had a plan ready, a thick document nicknamed the brick, and Pinochet soon put them in charge of the economy.

In March 1975, Friedman himself visited Chile, gave lectures, and met Pinochet. Afterward he wrote to the general recommending what he called shock treatment, deep and fast cuts in government spending to kill inflation. The Chicago Boys carried it out, and went on to slash tariffs and sell off state companies.

The results are still argued over. Inflation came down, and from the mid-1980s Chile's economy grew faster than almost any other in Latin America. But unemployment soared, a banking collapse in 1982 was devastating, and all of it happened under a regime that killed thousands of its opponents and tortured many thousands more.

When Friedman won the Nobel Prize in economics in 1976, there were protests, and at the ceremony in Stockholm a heckler shouted for freedom for Chile. Friedman replied that he had offered similar advice to communist governments without complaint, and that economic freedom would bring political freedom in time. In 1990, Chile did return to democracy. How much credit, or blame, the Chicago Boys deserve is still fiercely debated.
=== DEEPER Scav and the Great Debate | culture
The University of Chicago has a reputation for seriousness. A joke T-shirt sold near campus for years put it bluntly: the University of Chicago, where fun goes to die. The students have spent decades proving it wrong, in their own peculiar ways.

The biggest is Scav, the university's scavenger hunt, which began in 1987. Every year, over four days around Mother's Day, teams race to complete a list of hundreds of items written by a panel of judges. Some items are jokes. Others are nearly impossible: build a working machine, stage a performance, produce an object nobody should be able to find. For a few years, Guinness listed it as the largest scavenger hunt in the world.

The most famous item came in 1999. The list asked for a breeder reactor, built in a shed, as a joke about a teenager in Michigan who had tried to build one in his mother's backyard. Two physics students took it seriously. In a dorm room, with a little thorium and a great deal of care, they built a tabletop device that, by their measurements, turned a few atoms of the thorium into uranium. The item was worth five hundred points, and it made them campus legends.

The oldest tradition is even sillier. Since 1946, the university has held the Latke-Hamantash Debate, in which distinguished professors argue, at length and with straight faces, over which is the superior food: the latke, the fried potato pancake of Hanukkah, or the hamantash, the three-cornered pastry of Purim. Nobel Prize winners have taken part, and so have university presidents. Speakers have attacked the question through physics, economics, philosophy, and literary theory, and for more than twenty-five years the moderator was a philosophy professor named Ted Cohen.

The debate has never been settled, and that's the point.
=== STOP Robie House
=== DEEPER The Excelsior | companies
You heard that Frederick Robie was in the bicycle business. That business has a story of its own, and it ends with a name you probably know.

It was the Excelsior Supply Company, founded in Chicago in 1876 by Robie's father, George Robie. It started out selling parts for sewing machines. Then, as the bicycle craze swept the country in the 1880s and 1890s, it moved into bicycles and bicycle parts. Chicago was one of the capitals of that boom, home to dozens of bicycle makers.

When the craze faded, the Robies looked to the next new thing: motors. In 1907, the family formed a new company, the Excelsior Motor Manufacturing and Supply Company, with Frederick as its president, to build motorcycles. The first Excelsiors were simple machines, essentially a sturdy bicycle frame with a single-cylinder engine.

That's the business that paid for this house, and it helps explain the client Wright had: a practical, mechanical man who knew what he wanted. As Robie told it decades later, other architects kept offering him conventional houses, and Wright was the first one who understood him.

But the money ran out. After George Robie's death left the family business deep in debt, Frederick couldn't keep it afloat. In November 1911, the Excelsior companies were sold to Ignaz Schwinn, the German-born Chicago bicycle maker. About a month later, Robie sold his house.

Under Schwinn, Excelsior became one of the great American motorcycle brands. In 1912, an Excelsior became the first motorcycle officially timed at a hundred miles an hour. Schwinn later bought the Henderson company too, and for a time Excelsior-Henderson ranked with Harley-Davidson and Indian among the big three of American motorcycles. The Great Depression ended it. Schwinn stopped making motorcycles in 1931 and went back to bicycles, which kept the Schwinn name famous for another half century and more.
=== DEEPER The Portfolio | culture
This house changed architecture in Europe as well as in America, and the reason is a book.

In the fall of 1909, before the Robie House was finished, Wright left Chicago for Europe. One purpose of the trip was to prepare a publication of his work for a Berlin publisher, Ernst Wasmuth. In 1910 and 1911, Wasmuth brought out two volumes: a large portfolio of a hundred lithographs of Wright's buildings and designs, and a smaller book of photographs. It was the first time Wright's work had been published as a whole, and it happened in Germany.

The Robie House was in it, with its long horizontal lines, its roofs floating over open space, and its rooms flowing into one another. To young European architects, struggling to escape the heavy historical styles of the nineteenth century, it looked like the future.

There's a well-known story that when the portfolio reached the Berlin office of Peter Behrens, where Walter Gropius and Ludwig Mies van der Rohe were then working, everyone stopped to pore over it. Nobody has ever proved that. But Mies himself wrote, years later, that Wright's work, shown in Berlin, had given his whole generation a powerful jolt. Gropius went on to found the Bauhaus, and Mies, as you may know, ended up in Chicago.

In the Netherlands the influence was even more direct. A young Dutch architect visited America in 1914, saw Wright's buildings, and went home to design a concrete villa in the Prairie manner, often counted among the first buildings of the Dutch movement called De Stijl. And the long, low brick town hall that Willem Dudok designed for Hilversum shows the same horizontal lines.

So when you look at this house, you're looking at one of the roots of modern architecture in Europe. The irony is that Wright, who insisted he owed nothing to anyone, had little good to say about what the Europeans made of his ideas.
=== DEEPER The Developer's Headquarters | politics
You heard that the Robie House was nearly torn down in 1957. Its rescuer was a New York real estate developer, and the reason he was in Hyde Park is one of the most important, and painful, stories in the neighborhood's history.

After the Second World War, Hyde Park was changing fast. Its housing was aging, many big apartments had been cut up into tiny units, and white families were moving out as Black families, shut out of most of the city, moved in. The University of Chicago feared it would be surrounded by decline, and it set out to remake the neighborhood. In 1952 it helped create the South East Chicago Commission, and through it, the university pushed for one of the first large urban renewal plans in the country.

The city approved the Hyde Park-Kenwood plan in 1958. Over the following years, more than six hundred buildings came down, and some four thousand families were displaced, most of them Black. Along 55th Street and Lake Park Avenue, whole rows of bars, music clubs, and small shops disappeared.

One of the developers chosen to rebuild was William Zeckendorf's firm, Webb and Knapp. Its architect was I. M. Pei, who designed the pair of ten-story apartment towers that stand in the middle of 55th Street, and rows of townhouses nearby.

In 1958, Zeckendorf's company bought the Robie House from the seminary for a hundred and twenty-five thousand dollars and made it its Midwest headquarters while the rebuilding went on. A few years later, he gave the house to the University of Chicago.

Supporters of urban renewal said it saved Hyde Park from emptying out, and made it one of the few places in Chicago where Black and white middle-class families lived side by side. Critics said that integration was bought by pushing out people who couldn't afford to stay. Historians tend to say both are right. Either way, Wright's masterpiece owes its survival, in part, to the bulldozers.
=== STOP Nuclear Energy & the First Reactor
=== DEEPER The People in the Squash Court | people
You heard about Fermi and the man with the axe. Some fifty people crowded into the squash court that December day, and a few of their stories are worth knowing.

The youngest member of the team, and the only woman there, was Leona Woods, who was twenty-three. She had built the instruments that counted the neutrons, and as the pile came to life, she helped keep watch on them. Fermi relied on her.

Above the pile, on a platform, stood three young men known as the suicide squad. They held buckets of cadmium solution, ready to flood the pile if everything else failed. They never had to.

On the floor beside the pile, a physicist named George Weil handled the last control rod. On Fermi's orders, he drew it out a little at a time, while Fermi checked the instruments and worked his calculations on a slide rule. Fermi was so calm about it all that late in the morning, with the pile nearly there, he stopped everything and announced that it was time for lunch.

When the reaction finally sustained itself, at about twenty-five minutes past three in the afternoon, nobody cheered. Fermi let it run for a few minutes, then shut it down. Eugene Wigner, a Hungarian physicist, brought out a bottle of Chianti that he had bought in advance and kept out of sight. Everyone drank from paper cups, in silence. Most of them signed the bottle, and it's now kept by the National Archives in Chicago.

One person who didn't know about any of this was the university's president, Robert Maynard Hutchins. Arthur Compton, who ran the project in Chicago, decided on his own to go ahead with the experiment in the middle of a crowded city, without telling Hutchins or the mayor. Compton was confident it could be controlled, and he judged that Hutchins was in no position to weigh the risks. Fortunately, he was right.
=== DEEPER The Petition | politics
You heard that many of the scientists were troubled by what they had made. Some of them tried to keep the bomb from being used at all, and the center of that effort was here.

By the spring of 1945, the Chicago laboratory's main work was done, and the bomb itself was being finished at Los Alamos, in New Mexico. That gave the scientists here time to think about what would come next. In May, Germany surrendered. The bomb would be used against Japan.

In June, a committee led by the physicist James Franck, a Nobel Prize winner who had fled Nazi Germany, sent a report to Washington. The Franck Report warned that dropping the bomb on Japan without warning would set off a nuclear arms race and cost the United States its moral standing. It proposed a demonstration instead, on a desert or a barren island, in front of observers from around the world. The report was delivered, but the decision to use the bomb had already been made.

Then Leo Szilard went further. Szilard, a Hungarian physicist, had first imagined a nuclear chain reaction in 1933, while waiting to cross a street in London. In 1939, he drafted the letter, signed by Albert Einstein, that warned President Roosevelt a bomb might be possible. Now he wanted to stop it. In July 1945, he wrote a petition to President Truman, urging him not to use the bomb unless Japan had first been told the terms of surrender and had refused them. Seventy scientists here signed it.

It never reached Truman. The petition went up the chain of command to General Leslie Groves, the head of the Manhattan Project, who was in no hurry to pass it along, and it was filed away as secret. It wasn't made public until 1961.

The petition failed, but its signers kept going. After the war, many of them campaigned for civilian control of atomic energy, and they started the magazine you heard about, the one with the clock.
=== DEEPER The Buried Reactor | history
The pile that came to life under Stagg Field didn't stay here long. What's left of it now lies buried in a forest southwest of the city, under a stone that tells you not to dig.

Running a nuclear reactor beside a football stadium in a crowded neighborhood was too risky to continue, even in wartime. So early in 1943, the pile was taken apart, block by block, and trucked about twenty miles southwest, to a wooded spot in the Cook County forest preserves near Willow Springs, then known as the Argonne Forest. The project called it Site A. There the pile was rebuilt, larger, and this time with a thick shield around it, as Chicago Pile Two. Nearby, the scientists built Chicago Pile Three, the world's first reactor to use heavy water.

The work done in those woods grew into Argonne National Laboratory, the country's first national laboratory, which took its name from the forest. Within a few years, Argonne moved to a much larger site in DuPage County, where it still is.

That left the question of what to do with the old reactors. In 1956, the Atomic Energy Commission buried them right where they stood. The reactors were entombed at Site A, and nearby, at a spot called Plot M, the commission buried radioactive waste from the early years of research.

Two stone markers went up later. The one at Plot M warns you, in capital letters, Caution, do not dig, and explains that radioactive material from nuclear research done there between 1943 and 1949 is buried below. The other marks Site A, where the world's first nuclear reactor was rebuilt.

Today the area is part of Red Gate Woods, open to hikers and cyclists. The fence around Site A came down in 1998. The government still tests the groundwater and the nearby wells, and it says the surface is safe for recreation. So you can walk in the woods where the atomic age went after it left Hyde Park, as long as you take the stone's advice.
