=== DEEPER The Campus Grid | architecture
When Mies took over the architecture school here in 1938, the Armour Institute was a cluster of old buildings in a crowded, run-down part of the South Side, and its leaders wanted to rebuild on a far larger site, to a plan by their new director. By 1941 Mies had laid out the entire campus on one grid.

Everything follows a single measure: a module twenty-four feet square and twelve feet high. The spacing of the columns, the heights of the walls, and the placement of the buildings and the walks between them are all multiples of it, so that buildings put up decades apart would still line up with one another. Mies kept the materials just as strict: a frame of steel painted black, panels of buff brick, and big sheets of glass.

Look at a corner of one of the older brick buildings nearby. Fire codes required the steel columns to be wrapped in concrete, which hid the very frame Mies wanted you to see. So he wrapped the concrete in steel plate and fastened steel beams to the outside, to show what was holding the building up. The steel you see does no structural work at all. It's there to tell the truth about the steel inside, a kind of honest ornament, and architects still argue about whether that's a contradiction.

The smallest building on the campus may be the most surprising. A short walk north is the Carr Memorial Chapel, finished in 1952, the only religious building Mies ever designed. It's a plain brick box with a glass front, and generations of students have called it the God Box.

Crown Hall was the climax. Its main floor is a single room about a hundred and twenty feet wide and two hundred and twenty feet long, raised a few feet above the lawn, with workshops and classrooms tucked into a half-sunken level below. Mies reportedly called it the clearest structure he had ever built. In 2001 it was named a National Historic Landmark.
=== DEEPER The Strangest Place in Chicago | history
The Mecca was designed by the architects Willoughby Edbrooke and Franklin Burnham, no relation to the famous Daniel, and it was meant at first as a hotel for the crowds coming to the world's fair of 1893. After the fair, it became an apartment house for prosperous white families, with nearly a hundred apartments. Their doors opened onto long balconies with ornate iron railings, stacked one above another around the inside of the building, so that the whole place faced inward, like a village built around its own indoor street.

The Depression hit it hard. Apartments were cut up, repairs stopped, and by the 1940s well over a thousand people were crowded into the building.

Around 1940, the institute bought it, planning to tear it down for the new campus. The tenants fought back. They organized, went to court, and pressed the politicians, and for more than a decade they held off the wreckers.

In December 1950, Harper's Magazine published a long article about the building by the journalist John Bartlow Martin, under the title The Strangest Place in Chicago. Martin walked the balconies and talked with the people who lived there. He found a crumbling, dangerously overcrowded building that was also a real community, full of working people, many of them from the South, who didn't want to lose their homes. A Life magazine photographer, Wallace Kirkland, took pictures there too, and his photographs of tenants leaning on the iron railings, looking down into the courts, are still the best-known images of the building. Martin went on to write speeches for the presidential candidate Adlai Stevenson and to serve as ambassador to the Dominican Republic.

None of it saved the Mecca. The tenants were moved out, and the wreckers finally came. On the ground where all those families had lived, Mies built his great glass room, a building that opens itself to the street on every side, where once there had been a building that turned its whole life inward.
=== DEEPER Gwendolyn Brooks | people
Gwendolyn Brooks was born in Topeka, Kansas, in 1917, and her family brought her to Chicago when she was a baby. She grew up in a small house on the South Side, filling notebooks with poems. By the family's account, her mother predicted she would be a lady Paul Laurence Dunbar, a woman to rival the celebrated Black poet. She published her first poem in a children's magazine at thirteen, and as a teenager she sent verses to the Defender, which printed dozens of them.

She knew the Mecca from the inside. By her own account, after college she worked there for a few months as the assistant to a self-styled spiritual adviser who sold charms and potions, and part of her job was delivering them to his customers in the building. Decades later, that memory became her long poem In the Mecca, published in 1968. It follows a mother searching the building for her missing little girl, meeting the neighbors one by one along the way. It was a finalist for the National Book Award.

In between came her first book, A Street in Bronzeville, in 1945, and in 1960 the poem most people know, We Real Cool, about seven pool players at a place called the Golden Shovel, with lines that end, again and again, on the word we.

Then, in 1967, she went to a writers' conference at Fisk University in Nashville and met a new generation of young Black poets, who wanted to write for Black readers first. By her own account, she came home transformed. She began leading poetry workshops for young people in Chicago, including members of a South Side gang called the Blackstone Rangers, and she moved her work from a big New York publisher to small Black presses.

In 1968, she succeeded Carl Sandburg as poet laureate of Illinois, and she held the title until her death in 2000. In 1985, she became the first Black woman appointed poetry consultant to the Library of Congress, the post now called poet laureate of the United States.

=== DEEPER Georgia Tom | people
Thomas Dorsey was born in Georgia in 1899, the son of a preacher and a mother who played the church organ, and he learned both kinds of music early, the hymns of Sunday morning and the blues of Saturday night. Around the end of the First World War, he came north to Chicago, where he played piano at rent parties and in speakeasies and studied composing and arranging.

In the mid-1920s, he led the band behind Ma Rainey, the Mother of the Blues, on her tours. Then, in 1928, he teamed up with the guitarist Tampa Red, and as Tampa Red and Georgia Tom they recorded a sly, bawdy blues called It's Tight Like That, which became a huge hit.

But Dorsey was torn between the clubs and the church, and he suffered at least one long breakdown. He began writing religious songs with the bounce and the blue notes of the music he played in the clubs. Many ministers and church musicians were horrified at first, and some kept his songs out of their churches.

In 1931, at Ebenezer Baptist Church, farther south in Bronzeville, he and the singer Theodore Frye organized what's often called the first gospel chorus. Soon afterward, with the singer Sallie Martin, he helped found the National Convention of Gospel Choirs and Choruses, which carried the new music to churches across the country. He also set up his own company to publish and sell his sheet music, one of the first of its kind, and for a while, it's said, people called any gospel song a Dorsey.

He wrote hundreds of songs. One of them, Peace in the Valley, was later sung by Elvis Presley on national television. But none was loved like Precious Lord. On the evening of April fourth, 1968, in Memphis, Martin Luther King Junior leaned over a motel balcony and asked the musician Ben Branch to play it at a rally that night, and to play it real pretty. Moments later, King was shot.

Dorsey died in 1993, at ninety-three.
=== DEEPER Move On Up a Little Higher | culture
Mahalia Jackson grew up in New Orleans, singing in a Baptist church and soaking up the records of the blues singer Bessie Smith. In 1927, at sixteen, she came to Chicago, where she worked as a laundress and a maid and sang with a group called the Johnson Singers and in the choir at Greater Salem Baptist Church. Later, to support herself, she trained as a beautician and ran a beauty shop and a flower shop.

She turned down offers to sing the blues in nightclubs. Blues are the songs of despair, she liked to say, but gospel songs are the songs of hope.

In 1947, she recorded Move On Up a Little Higher, a song by the Reverend William Herbert Brewster, for a small label called Apollo. It sold in the millions, perhaps more than any gospel record before it. On October fourth, 1950, she sang at Carnegie Hall in New York, in one of the first gospel concerts ever held there, and in the 1950s she signed with Columbia Records and became a star of radio and television.

She was a friend and supporter of Martin Luther King Junior from the days of the Montgomery bus boycott. At the March on Washington, in August 1963, she sang an old spiritual, I Been 'Buked and I Been Scorned, shortly before King spoke. By several accounts, as he worked through his prepared text, she called out to him: Tell them about the dream, Martin. He set his notes aside, and the speech that followed is the one the world remembers.

When King was killed in 1968, she sang Precious Lord at his funeral in Atlanta. She died in 1972, and by most accounts tens of thousands of people filed past her coffin in Chicago.
=== DEEPER When the Temples Became Churches | history
You heard that this building began as a synagogue. It wasn't the only one. In the late nineteenth century, the avenues south of downtown, from Michigan and Indiana over to Grand Boulevard, now King Drive, were home to many of Chicago's prosperous Jewish families, most of them from Germany, while poorer Jewish immigrants from Eastern Europe crowded the West Side. Their congregations built some of the city's finest temples.

Then, around the time of the First World War, the Black Belt pushed south and east, and the Jewish families moved on, toward Hyde Park, Kenwood, and South Shore. Their congregations followed them and sold the old temples, one by one, to Black churches, many of them founded by migrants from the South.

About a mile and a half south of here, at 45th Street and Vincennes Avenue, stands Ebenezer Missionary Baptist Church. It was built in 1899 as Isaiah Temple, one of the last buildings by Dankmar Adler, who had designed this one with Louis Sullivan. Ebenezer bought it in 1921, and the gospel chorus it started in 1931 was among the first anywhere. It's now on the National Register.

A little farther south, at 46th Street and King Drive, is the former Sinai Temple, built in 1912 for Chicago's first Reform congregation and designed by Alfred Alschuler. Sinai stayed until 1944. The building then housed a Catholic high school, Corpus Christi, and in the early 1960s it became Mount Pisgah Missionary Baptist Church. In 2020, the city made it a landmark.

These buildings suited their new owners well. Reform temples of that era were laid out much like big Protestant churches inside, with pews facing a raised platform, a pipe organ, and a choir loft. A Baptist congregation could move in and start singing.

And the sound that Adler designed for organ and choir served gospel just as well. Look at the old stone walls in front of you. They were raised by one community of newcomers, and filled with the music of another.

=== DEEPER Binga's Bank | companies
A block north of here, at the corner of 35th and State, stood the bank of Jesse Binga, for a time the most celebrated Black businessman in Chicago.

Binga was born in Detroit in 1865, and after years of odd jobs out West, he reached Chicago in the early 1890s with very little money. He started out peddling fruit and then went into real estate. His method made him rich. He leased run-down buildings on the edge of the Black Belt, fixed them up, and rented them to Black families who couldn't find housing anywhere else.

In 1908, he opened a private bank in a storefront on State Street. He married Eudora Johnson, an heiress to a South Side gambling fortune, and in 1921 his bank won a state charter as the Binga State Bank. In 1929, he opened the Binga Arcade, a five-story office building at 35th and State.

Not everyone welcomed his success. He bought a house on what is now King Drive, at 59th Street, in a white neighborhood, and in 1919 and 1920 it was bombed again and again, at least five times. He refused to move.

The end came with the Depression. In the summer of 1930, state examiners closed the Binga State Bank, and thousands of depositors lost their savings. It was one of the first in a wave of failures that wiped out most of Bronzeville's banks, including, two years later, the Overton bank you heard about. Binga was convicted of embezzlement and sentenced to ten years in prison. He was released after about five.

He spent his last years working as a handyman and usher at Saint Anselm, a Catholic church on the South Side, and he died in 1950. His bank had been built on the savings of people shut out of white banks, and it fell when the whole economy collapsed around them.
=== DEEPER Queen Bess | people
In 1915, a young woman named Bessie Coleman came up from Texas to join her brothers in Chicago. She was one of thirteen children in a family that picked cotton, and she had managed one term of college in Oklahoma before the money ran out. In Chicago, she went to beauty school and found work as a manicurist in a South Side barbershop.

Her brothers came home from the First World War full of stories about France, and one of them teased her that French women could fly airplanes and she never would. She decided she would. No flight school in America would take a Black woman, so Robert Abbott, the publisher of the Defender, advised her to go to France, and Abbott and the banker Jesse Binga helped pay for her training.

She studied French at night, sailed for France in November 1920, and learned to fly in a fragile biplane at a flying school on the French coast. On June fifteenth, 1921, she earned her license from the international aviation federation, the first Black woman to earn a pilot's license.

Back in America, she became a stunt flier, thrilling crowds at air shows with loops and figure eights, and the press called her Queen Bess. She dreamed of opening a flying school for Black students, and she is said to have refused to perform where Black spectators had to come in through a separate gate.

On April thirtieth, 1926, in Jacksonville, Florida, she was rehearsing for an air show, with her mechanic at the controls, when the plane went into a dive and then a spin. She wasn't wearing a seat belt, it's said, because she had been leaning out to study the field below, and she fell to her death. She was thirty-four. The mechanic died in the crash, and investigators reportedly found that a loose wrench had jammed the controls.

Some ten thousand people came to her funeral in Chicago, and she's buried at Lincoln Cemetery, just southwest of the city. Every year since 1931, Black pilots have flown over her grave in salute.
=== DEEPER The Second Ward | politics
Black Southerners who came to Chicago gained something they had been denied at home: the right to vote. And because segregation packed them into a few wards on the South Side, their votes added up to real power.

The first to wield it was Oscar De Priest, a house painter turned real estate man, born in Alabama. In 1915, voters in the Second Ward, which took in much of the Black Belt, made him the first Black member of the Chicago City Council. Two years later, he was indicted, accused of taking money to protect gambling houses. The famous lawyer Clarence Darrow won his acquittal, but under pressure from his own Republican Party, De Priest had already given up his seat.

His great ally was the Republican mayor, Big Bill Thompson, who won the Black wards by enormous margins and rewarded them with jobs. In 1928, when the district's longtime congressman, Martin Madden, died, Thompson's organization picked De Priest to take his place on the ballot. He won, becoming the first Black member of Congress in twenty-eight years, and the first ever elected from a Northern state. When the First Lady, Lou Hoover, invited his wife, Jessie, to a White House tea in 1929, Southern newspapers and politicians were outraged.

Then the Depression changed everything. Black voters had been loyal to the party of Lincoln since emancipation, but the New Deal drew them to the Democrats. In 1934, a Democrat named Arthur Mitchell beat De Priest by about three thousand votes and became the first Black Democrat ever elected to Congress.

The man who built the Democrats' Black organization here was William Dawson, a lawyer from Georgia and a veteran of the First World War. He started out as a Republican alderman, switched parties in 1939, and in 1942 won a seat in Congress, which he held until his death in 1970. In 1949, he became the first Black chairman of a standing committee of the House. To his critics, Dawson was a boss who put the party ahead of civil rights. To his admirers, he made sure Black Chicago got its share of the jobs and favors.

=== DEEPER The Woman at the Piano | people
When Louis Armstrong came up from New Orleans in 1922 to join King Oliver's band, the piano player was a young woman from Memphis named Lillian Hardin. She had studied music at Fisk University, moved to Chicago with her mother around the end of the First World War, and found a job demonstrating sheet music in a music store on State Street, playing whatever song a customer wanted to hear. From there she moved into the bands, where she was billed as Hot Miss Lil.

By her own account, she wasn't impressed at first by the young man from New Orleans, with his country clothes and his old-fashioned bangs. But she soon heard what he could do. They married on February fifth, 1924, and she set about changing his career. As she told it later, she warned him that as long as he played second cornet to Oliver, he would never be a star. That fall, he left Oliver's band and went to New York to play with Fletcher Henderson's orchestra.

When he came back to Chicago at the end of 1925, she had him booked with her own band at the Dreamland Café, and, as the story goes, she had the club bill him as the world's greatest trumpet player. She also played piano on his Hot Five records, and she wrote or co-wrote several of the tunes they recorded.

The marriage didn't last. They separated in the early 1930s and divorced in 1938, but she kept his name, and they stayed on friendly terms. She went on working as a pianist, bandleader, and songwriter for the rest of her life.

Louis Armstrong died in July 1971. A few weeks later, on August twenty-seventh, Lil was playing at a memorial concert for him in Chicago when she collapsed at the piano, in the middle of the Saint Louis Blues. She died soon afterward. The woman who had pushed Louis Armstrong toward stardom went out playing in his honor.
=== DEEPER Heebie Jeebies | legends
On February twenty-sixth, 1926, Louis Armstrong and his Hot Five went into a recording studio in Chicago to cut a novelty song called Heebie Jeebies. Partway through, Armstrong stopped singing the words and broke into a stream of nonsense syllables, bouncing and swinging like a horn solo. It became one of the most famous scat vocals ever put on a record.

Armstrong told the story of how it happened for years afterward. He had the lyrics on a sheet of paper, he said, and in the middle of the take, he dropped it. There was no time to stop and start over, so he kept going, making up sounds to fill the space until he could pick up the words again. In his telling, the record company liked what it heard and released the take just as it was.

Many jazz historians doubt it. The scat passage fits a little too neatly into the arrangement, and singers had been scatting on records since the days of ragtime. But whatever happened in that studio, the record was a national hit. According to musicians who were young in Chicago at the time, friends took to greeting each other in Armstrong's nonsense syllables.

The title was brand new slang. The cartoonist Billy DeBeck had coined the phrase heebie-jeebies in his comic strip Barney Google in 1923, for a bad case of the jitters, and within a few years the whole country was using it.

Armstrong didn't invent scat, but he made it famous, and he made it an art. After him came Cab Calloway's hi-de-ho, Ella Fitzgerald's dazzling flights of invention, and generations of singers who used their voices like instruments. And in the story Armstrong chose to tell, it all began with a sheet of paper fluttering to the floor of a Chicago studio.
=== DEEPER The Grand Terrace | culture
The pianist who played beside Armstrong here at the Sunset went on to rule the South Side's nightlife for a decade. Earl Hines grew up near Pittsburgh and came to Chicago in the mid-1920s. He played with a bright, hard attack, punching out melodies in octaves with his right hand so that they cut through a big band like a trumpet. People called it trumpet-style piano.

In December 1928, he opened at a new club called the Grand Terrace, at 39th Street and what is now King Drive, about half a mile south of here, leading his own big band. He stayed for about twelve years. The club belonged, in part, to the mob. Al Capone's organization reportedly held a quarter share.

What made the Grand Terrace famous was radio. Its late-night shows went out live over a national network, and by the mid-1930s Hines's band was said to be on the air more than any other band in the country. A radio announcer gave Hines his nickname, as the story goes, when he introduced him one night as Fatha Hines, and it stuck. Later on, the club moved here, into the Sunset's old space on 35th Street.

Hines's bands were also a school. The singer Billy Eckstine joined him in the late 1930s, and in the early 1940s his band included the young Dizzy Gillespie, Charlie Parker, and Sarah Vaughan, the musicians who would soon invent bebop. Because the musicians' union had banned recording in those years, almost none of that band's music was ever recorded, and it's remembered as one of the great lost bands of jazz.

Hines kept playing into his seventies, and he died in 1983.

=== DEEPER The Black Devils | history
The regiment this monument honors is older than the First World War. It grew out of a Black militia battalion of the 1890s, and when war with Spain broke out in 1898, it became the Eighth Illinois Volunteer Infantry, with Black officers from its commander, Colonel John R. Marshall, on down, at a time when nearly all Black troops were led by white officers. It was sent to Cuba that summer, to relieve white troops who had been struck down by tropical fevers, and it stayed into the next year as part of the occupying force.

Back home, the regiment got something no Black unit in the country had: an armory built for it. The Eighth Regiment Armory went up in 1914 and 1915, a few blocks west of here, at 35th Street and Giles Avenue. It was the first armory in the United States built for a Black regiment.

In 1917, the Eighth went to war again, under Colonel Franklin Denison, a Chicago lawyer. In France, it became the 370th Infantry, and like the other Black regiments of its division, it was lent to the French army, largely because American commanders didn't want Black combat troops fighting beside white ones. The men fought in French helmets, with French rifles. In July 1918, Denison was relieved of command, officially because of illness, and a white colonel took his place.

That fall, the regiment fought its way across northern France as the German army fell back, and its men won many French and American medals for bravery. According to regimental lore, the Germans called them the Black Devils.

The street where the armory stands is named for one of the regiment's dead. In 1922, the city renamed Forest Avenue for Lieutenant George Giles, the regiment's highest-ranking officer killed in the war. Since 1999, the old armory has housed a public high school, the Chicago Military Academy.
=== DEEPER Who Was Bud Billiken | names
You heard that each August the Bud Billiken Parade comes down King Drive. The name behind it began as a fad and a newspaper gimmick.

In 1908, a Kansas City art teacher named Florence Pretz patented a little figure she called the Billiken, a grinning, pointy-headed elf with a round belly, sitting with its feet stuck out in front. She billed it as the god of things as they ought to be. For a few years, Billikens were everywhere, as dolls, charms, banks, and postcards. The fad faded, but the sports teams at Saint Louis University are still called the Billikens.

In 1923, the Chicago Defender started a page for children, and it needed a mascot. The paper's founder, Robert Abbott, and his editor Lucius Harper are usually credited with the idea of Bud Billiken. By one account, Abbott had seen a Billiken figure in a Chinese restaurant, and the paper presented the Billiken as a Chinese guardian of children, though it was really an American invention.

Bud Billiken became the make-believe editor of the children's page, and young readers all over the country joined Bud Billiken clubs. For Black children far from Chicago, the page was a window onto a bigger world, and a place to see their own names in print.

The parade began in 1929, as a thank-you to the newsboys who sold the Defender on street corners. It grew into one of the biggest days of the year on the South Side, with marching bands, drill teams, floats, and dancers. Politicians of every kind have marched in it, because there are few better places in Chicago to be seen.

These days it heads south on King Drive toward Washington Park, where the day ends with family picnics on the grass. More than a century after the fad, the little god of things as they ought to be still has his day on the South Side.
=== DEEPER The Open Casket | politics
In August 1955, a fourteen-year-old Chicago boy named Emmett Till went to visit relatives in the Mississippi Delta. In a country store in the small town of Money, he was accused of whistling at or flirting with the white woman behind the counter. A few nights later, her husband and his half-brother came for him at his great-uncle's house. They beat him, shot him, and threw his body into the Tallahatchie River, weighted down with a heavy metal fan from a cotton gin.

When the body was sent home to Chicago, his mother, Mamie Till Bradley, made a decision that changed American history. She insisted on an open casket. Let the world see what I've seen, she said.

The funeral was held on September third, 1955, at Roberts Temple Church of God in Christ, at 4021 South State Street, about a mile southwest of here. Over four days, the lines stretched around the block, and estimates of how many people filed past the casket run from fifty thousand to more than a hundred thousand.

Then Jet, the pocket-sized weekly published by John Johnson's company, printed photographs of Emmett's face in the casket, taken by its photographer David Jackson, in its issue of September fifteenth. Most white newspapers wouldn't run them. The issue sold out, and copies were passed from hand to hand across the country.

That same month, an all-white jury in Mississippi acquitted the two men after deliberating for about an hour. A few months later, safe from being tried again, they admitted the killing to a magazine writer, who paid them for their story.

For a generation of Black Americans, those pictures were a turning point. Rosa Parks is reported to have said that Emmett Till was on her mind that December, when she refused to give up her seat on a bus in Montgomery.

In 2023, Roberts Temple became part of a new national monument named for Emmett Till and for his mother, who spent the rest of her life telling his story.
=== STOP Ida B. Wells
=== DEEPER The Reason Why | history
Ida B. Wells first came to Chicago for the world's fair of 1893, and she came to protest it. The fair's organizers had shut Black Americans out of almost any real part in planning it, and out of most of its exhibits. So Wells joined forces with Frederick Douglass, then in his seventies, to put out a pamphlet called The Reason Why the Colored American Is Not in the World's Columbian Exposition.

Douglass wrote the introduction. Wells wrote about lynching and about the convict lease system, under which Southern states rented out prisoners, most of them Black, as forced laborers. The lawyer Ferdinand Barnett, her future husband, and the educator Irvine Garland Penn wrote the rest. They handed out copies at the fair's Haitian pavilion, where Douglass, a former American minister to Haiti, served as Haiti's representative.

The fair did make one gesture. It set aside August twenty-fifth as Colored American Day. Wells called for a boycott, dismissing the day as meaningless. Douglass went anyway and gave a powerful speech, and Wells is said to have told him afterward that she had been too hasty.

She stayed in Chicago, and over the next four decades she built a life of activism here. She helped found one of the first clubs for Black women in the city, which was later named for her. In 1910, she and Barnett opened the Negro Fellowship League on State Street, a reading room, social center, and employment office for young men arriving from the South. To keep it open, she took a job as an adult probation officer from 1913 to 1916, and she put her salary into the league. It closed in 1920.

She never left politics. In 1930, she ran for the Illinois Senate as an independent, and lost. She died the next spring, in March 1931. Her autobiography, Crusade for Justice, edited by her daughter Alfreda Duster, wasn't published until 1970, and it brought her story to a new generation.
=== DEEPER Native Son | culture
Richard Wright came to Chicago in 1927, at nineteen, after growing up poor in Mississippi and Memphis. He found the South Side crowded and hard. He washed dishes, worked as a porter and a busboy, swept streets, and sorted mail as a clerk at the main post office, and when the Depression came, he lost that job and went on relief.

He also found his first readers. Around 1933, he joined the John Reed Club, a writers' group sponsored by the Communist Party, and soon the party itself, whose magazines published his early poems. He helped start the South Side Writers Group, a circle that included the poet Margaret Walker, and he worked for the Federal Writers' Project, helping to write a guide to Illinois. He also worked at a boys' club on the South Side, and he wrote later that the restless, angry young men he met there were part of the model for his most famous character, Bigger Thomas.

Wright moved to New York in 1937, but Chicago stayed in his book. In 1938, while he was writing Native Son, Chicago police arrested a young Black man named Robert Nixon for the killing of a white woman, who had been beaten with a brick during a burglary. The Chicago Tribune covered the case in openly racist language. Friends in Chicago sent Wright the clippings, and in his essay How Bigger Was Born, he admitted borrowing details from the coverage. Nixon was executed in 1939.

Native Son came out in March 1940. It was the first novel by a Black author chosen as a main selection of the Book-of-the-Month Club, and it became a best seller. The next year, Wright won the Spingarn Medal, the highest honor of the civil rights organization that Wells had helped to found.

In 1951, a film of the novel, shot in Argentina, was released, with Wright himself, then in his early forties, playing Bigger. By then he had moved to Paris, where he died in 1960, at fifty-two.
=== DEEPER Hansberry versus Lee | politics
You heard that Carl Hansberry's fight ended at the Supreme Court. The case, Hansberry versus Lee, is still taught in law schools, though not for the reason you might expect.

The house is at 6140 South Rhodes Avenue, about three miles south of here. In the late 1920s, white property owners in the surrounding neighborhood had signed a restrictive covenant, a private agreement never to sell or rent to Black people. It was to take effect only if the owners of ninety-five percent of the frontage signed it. In an earlier lawsuit, in 1934, both sides simply agreed that enough owners had signed, and the court enforced the covenant.

In 1937, Hansberry, a successful real estate broker, bought the house and moved in with his family. His daughter Lorraine was seven. She later recalled the mob outside and a brick that came crashing through a window, and she wrote that her mother patrolled the house at night with a loaded pistol while her father fought in court.

A neighbor named Anna Lee sued to undo the sale. In court, Hansberry's lawyers, led by Earl Dickerson, showed that only about fifty-four percent of the owners had actually signed. The Illinois courts ruled against the family anyway. The question, they said, had already been settled by the earlier case, for everyone in the neighborhood.

In November 1940, the Supreme Court unanimously reversed. The justices didn't strike down racial covenants. They ruled that the Hansberrys couldn't be bound by a lawsuit in which nobody had stood up for their interests, as a matter of due process. That's why law students still study it, as a landmark in the law of class actions. Courts kept enforcing covenants until 1948, when the Supreme Court ruled in Shelley versus Kraemer that they could no longer do so.

The fight took its toll. Carl Hansberry began planning to move his family to Mexico, and he died there in 1946. His daughter came to believe that American racism had helped kill him. In 2010, the city made the house a landmark.

=== DEEPER By the Light of His Effigies | politics
The law that made Douglas famous, and nearly ruined him, was the Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854. As chairman of the Senate's committee on the territories, he wanted to organize the vast prairie west of Missouri and Iowa, partly, many historians think, to clear the way for a Pacific railroad running west from Chicago.

To win Southern votes, the bill repealed the Missouri Compromise of 1820, which had banned slavery in those lands, and left the question to the settlers. Douglas called it popular sovereignty. The North erupted. He was burned in effigy so often, he is supposed to have said, that he could have traveled from Boston to Chicago by the light of his own effigies.

That September, he came home to defend the law. A huge crowd jeered and shouted him down for hours. By one account, sometime after midnight he lost his temper and told the crowd that it was now Sunday morning, that he was going to church, and that they could go to hell. The fury gave birth to the Republican Party, and it drew a Springfield lawyer named Abraham Lincoln back into politics.

Douglas famously said that he didn't care whether slavery was voted up or voted down. What fewer voters knew was how much of his own family's income came from it. In 1847, he married Martha Martin, the daughter of a North Carolina planter. When her father died the next year, he left her a cotton plantation on the Pearl River in Mississippi, with about a hundred enslaved people, and Douglas managed it, for a share of the profits. After Martha died in 1853, he kept drawing income from it as guardian of their two young sons, while insisting that he had never owned a slave, which was true only on paper.

Chicago is still reckoning with that legacy. In 2020, after a campaign started by Chicago schoolchildren, the Park District took his name off Douglas Park, on the West Side, and renamed it for the abolitionists Frederick and Anna Murray Douglass. The sound of the name stayed. Its meaning changed completely.
=== DEEPER The Plot to Free the Prisoners | legends
By 1864, Camp Douglas, just west of here, held thousands of Confederate prisoners, and Confederate agents working out of Canada saw an opportunity. Freed and armed, those men might set the Midwest on fire.

The plan depended on the Sons of Liberty, a secret society of Northern Democrats who opposed the war. In August 1864, when the Democratic National Convention brought huge crowds to Chicago, a young Kentucky cavalry captain named Thomas Hines slipped into the city with a few dozen Confederate soldiers in civilian clothes, expecting the Sons of Liberty to supply hundreds of armed men. Too few came, and the attack was called off.

They tried again in November, with the rising set for election day, when Lincoln was running for a second term. This time the camp's commander, Colonel Benjamin Sweet, was ready. He had informers, among them a Confederate prisoner named John Shanks, who escaped with Sweet's blessing and kept him posted on the plotters' plans. In the early hours of November seventh, the day before the election, Sweet's soldiers swept through the city, arresting suspected conspirators and seizing hidden weapons, including dozens of double-barreled shotguns.

Among those arrested was an English soldier of fortune, George Grenfell, who had ridden with Confederate cavalry. Sentenced to hang, he was sent instead to a remote island prison off Florida, and he vanished in 1868 trying to escape in a small boat.

Hines got away, and here the story turns into legend. As he and his friends told it later, soldiers surrounded the house where he was hiding in the middle of the night. He crawled inside a mattress, and the woman of the house, pretending to be gravely ill, lay on top of it while the soldiers searched. They never found him. Later, the story goes, he slipped out among visitors carrying umbrellas in the rain.

How dangerous was the plot, really? Historians still argue. Some think Sweet and the Republican newspapers inflated it, to make the Democrats look treasonous just before the vote. Hines went home to Kentucky after the war and ended up as chief justice of the state's highest court.
=== DEEPER The Column | look
Look up at the monument. It took fifteen years to finish. The cornerstone was laid on September sixth, 1866, by President Andrew Johnson, who had come west on a speaking tour with General Ulysses Grant in his party. The tour, which the newspapers called the Swing Around the Circle, turned into a disaster for Johnson, who traded insults with hecklers from town to town. Less than two years later, the House impeached him.

Douglas's body was moved into the tomb in 1868, but the monument above it rose slowly, and it wasn't finished until 1881, at a cost of about ninety thousand dollars.

The whole monument stands about ninety-six feet high. Douglas looks out from the top of the granite column, in a statue nearly ten feet tall. Around the base sit four figures of women, who stand for Illinois, History, Justice, and Eloquence. Eloquence is a fitting choice. Douglas was one of the great stump speakers of his day, a short man with a deep, booming voice that seemed far too big for his body.

Inside the base is the crypt, where Douglas lies in a sarcophagus of Vermont marble, from the state where he was born. It's carved with what are said to be his last words, a message for his sons: Tell my children to obey the laws and uphold the Constitution. They suit a man who spent his career trying to hold the Union together by compromise, and who spent his last weeks rallying the country to save it.

Walk around the base and look up at the figure on top once more. The man who argued that each territory should decide the question of slavery for itself has stood here through everything that came after: the war, emancipation, the Great Migration, and the rise of the Black Metropolis all around him. The tomb is now a state historic site and a Chicago landmark.
