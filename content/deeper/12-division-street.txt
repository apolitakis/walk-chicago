=== DEEPER Studs Terkel | people
The man who put Division Street into the title of a famous book was born Louis Terkel, in New York City, in 1912, the son of Russian Jewish immigrants. He came to Chicago as a boy, and his parents ran a rooming house, where he grew up among the workers, drifters, and talkers who passed through it.

He earned a law degree at the University of Chicago, but the law never took. During the Depression he worked for the New Deal's writers' project and acted in radio soap operas, where he was often cast as a gangster. A director gave him his nickname, after Studs Lonigan, the hero of James T. Farrell's novels of the Irish South Side, which Terkel happened to be reading at the time.

In the 1950s, his left-wing politics got him blacklisted from television, and he found a home at a small Chicago classical music station, WFMT. His daily interview program ran there from 1952 to 1997. He talked with just about everyone, from Bob Dylan to Mahalia Jackson, and with countless people nobody had ever heard of.

Those ordinary people became his life's work. In 1967 he published Division Street: America, the first of his books of oral history, and he went on to do the same for the Great Depression in Hard Times, for jobs in Working, and for the Second World War in The Good War, which won the Pulitzer Prize in 1985. His method was simple. He turned on a tape recorder, asked a question, and listened, for as long as it took. He believed that people who were never asked about their lives had the most to say about them, and his books proved him right.

He kept at it into his nineties, and he died in Chicago in 2008, at the age of ninety-six. His recordings, thousands of hours of them, have been preserved, a portrait of an American century in the voices of the people who lived it.
=== DEEPER Inside the Shvitz | culture
In the early 1900s, few of the crowded flats around Division Street had a bathtub, and a trip to the bathhouse was part of the weekly routine. For immigrants from Russia, Poland, and Ukraine, it was also a piece of home. Across Eastern Europe, the steam bath was an old institution, a place to get clean, to sweat out a cold or a hangover, and to talk. Jewish customers called it the shvitz, from the Yiddish word for sweat, and for observant men, a visit on Friday, before the Sabbath, was an old custom.

A Russian bath works with wet heat. At its heart is a huge oven packed with rocks and heated for hours. When an attendant throws water onto the rocks, a blast of steam fills the room, so hot that most people stay on the low benches, and only the brave climb to the top. Then comes a plunge into ice-cold water, and then you go back in and do it all again. The Turkish half of the name meant a room of hot, dry air, for those who preferred to bake rather than steam.

These baths had famous regulars. Saul Bellow was one of them, and Nelson Algren wrote about the place too. When an Italian American family, the Coluccis, took over the business in the 1970s, the regulars were still mostly Eastern Europeans, and the family ran it for decades, through good times and lean ones, as the old neighborhood around it changed.

The baths closed in 2010, and many people assumed that another piece of old Chicago was gone for good. But they soon reopened under new owners, first as Red Square, and later as the Chicago Bath House, and more than a century after they opened, the steam is still rising here. One thing has changed. The old baths were a world of men. The bathhouse welcomes women now too, and it has done better for it.
=== DEEPER Riot or Rebellion | politics
What should you call what happened at this corner in June 1966? For years, newspapers and official reports called it the Division Street riot. Many people in the neighborhood call it the Division Street rebellion, or the uprising, and the choice of word is itself an argument. A riot sounds like senseless destruction. A rebellion says the anger had causes, and that the people in the street had reasons.

Those reasons got a public airing that summer. About a month after the riot, the city's Commission on Human Relations held open hearings, and Puerto Rican and other Spanish-speaking residents had an official forum, perhaps for the first time, to describe how they were treated by the police and by the city.

New organizations grew out of those days. The Spanish Action Committee of Chicago organized Puerto Rican residents to press the city for change. Before the year was out, Puerto Rican and Mexican activists founded the Latin American Defense Organization, an early effort to bring the two communities together. Over the next few years came housing groups, education programs, and cultural centers, and in Lincoln Park, the Young Lords, whom you'll hear about later on this tour.

The riot also showed how little had changed for many people, and how easily violence could return. Eleven years later, after police killed two young men in Humboldt Park, the neighborhood saw a second uprising.

Over the decades, historians and community groups collected the stories of the people who lived through those three nights, and a younger generation came to see them as the moment Puerto Rican Chicago stood up and demanded to be heard. In June 2026, sixty years on, the community marked the anniversary, and many called it a rebellion.

=== DEEPER Why They Became Orthodox | history
Back home in the Carpathian Mountains, most Rusyns weren't Orthodox. They were Greek Catholics. Their churches looked Orthodox, with icons, chant, and the old Eastern liturgy, but since the 1600s they had been in union with the Pope in Rome. One thing set their clergy apart from the Roman Catholic priests most Americans knew. Their priests could marry.

In America, that became a problem. Most of the Catholic bishops here were Irish American, and many of them saw the Eastern rite, and above all its married priests, as foreign, confusing, and a scandal to their own flocks.

The breaking point came in Minneapolis. In late 1889, a Greek Catholic priest named Alexis Toth arrived to serve a small Rusyn parish there. He was a widower who had taught church law back home. When he presented his papers to the archbishop, John Ireland, the archbishop asked whether he had been married, and on hearing that he had, refused to recognize him as a priest at all. In Toth's telling, the meeting was bitter, and it ended without any compromise in sight.

Toth found another way. In 1891, he and several hundred of his parishioners were received into the Russian Orthodox Church, whose American diocese was then based in San Francisco. As Toth saw it, he was leading his people home to the faith of their ancestors, before their union with Rome. He spent the rest of his life traveling to Rusyn communities across the country, and by the time he died, in 1909, many thousands had followed his example.

Holy Trinity, founded in Chicago just a year after his parish made its move, grew out of the same current, and the Russian Church, backed by the tsar's government, sent priests and money to help. Rome's own policies kept the movement going. In 1929, a Vatican decree barred married Eastern Catholic priests from serving in America, and another wave of parishes left. Today Toth is honored as a saint by the Orthodox Church in America, and many American Orthodox parishes trace their roots to his quarrel with the archbishop.
=== DEEPER Rusyn, Ruthenian, Russian | names
The people who founded this parish went by many names, and the confusion tells you a lot about where they came from.

They called themselves Rusyns. Their homeland was a string of mountain valleys and villages along the Carpathians, where today's Poland, Slovakia, Ukraine, Hungary, and Romania come together, and they had never had a country of their own. The Habsburg officials who ruled them called them Ruthenians, an old Latin form of the same name. Depending on the valley, they might also call themselves Rusnaks, or Lemkos.

In America, the names multiplied. Many of those who joined the Russian Orthodox Church began calling themselves Russians, which wasn't quite right, but which made a certain sense in a church whose bishops and money came from Russia. Others came to think of themselves as Slovak Americans, or Ukrainian Americans, and within a generation, cousins from the same village could disagree about what they were.

The twentieth century made it harder still. After the Second World War, much of the Rusyn homeland was annexed to Soviet Ukraine, and the Soviet authorities simply declared that Rusyns were Ukrainians. Only after communism fell did a Rusyn revival take hold, with its own schools, newspapers, and festivals, and today Rusyns are recognized as a national minority in several countries of central Europe.

You've almost certainly heard of one American with Rusyn roots. Andy Warhol's parents, Ondrej and Julia Warhola, came from Miková, a Rusyn village in what is now eastern Slovakia, and Warhol grew up in Pittsburgh going to a Byzantine Catholic church. He never made much of his background in public. But a short drive from his parents' village, there's now a museum of his work, and to many Rusyns he's a point of pride: the most famous son of a people much of the world has never heard of.

So if you hear this church called Russian, Rusyn, or simply Orthodox, all of those names are true in their way.
=== DEEPER Saint John of Chicago | people
The two saints connected with this church each spent about a decade in America, and both left marks that outlasted them.

John Kochurov came first. Born in 1871 in a village in central Russia, he finished his studies at the theological academy in Saint Petersburg and volunteered for the church's American mission. In 1895 he arrived in Chicago, a young priest, to serve a small, poor congregation that didn't yet have a proper church.

For the next twelve years, he was its pastor, organizer, and fundraiser. He raised money wherever he could, including in Russia, he worked with Louis Sullivan on the design, and he saw the church through to its completion. Then, in 1907, he was called home. He taught religion in the schools of Narva, in what is now Estonia, and later served at Tsarskoye Selo, pronounced roughly tsar-skoy-eh seh-lo, the imperial town outside Saint Petersburg, where his life ended in the first days of Bolshevik rule. Here at Holy Trinity, he's remembered simply as Saint John of Chicago.

His bishop, Tikhon, had come to America in 1898 to lead the Russian Church's North American diocese, then based in San Francisco. He was a tireless traveler, crossing the continent to visit scattered parishes from Alaska to Pennsylvania, and he saw that the church's future here would be American. In 1903 he came to Chicago to consecrate this church. Two years later he moved his headquarters to New York, and that same year he founded a seminary in Minneapolis and a monastery in Pennsylvania. He encouraged worship in English, and he even proposed that the American church should one day govern itself.

In 1907, Tikhon too was called home, and ten years later, in the turmoil of revolution, he became patriarch, as you heard at the stop. The idea he had planted here took longer to grow. In 1970, the church that grew out of his American diocese was granted its independence by the church in Moscow, as the Orthodox Church in America, much as he had imagined.

=== DEEPER The Displaced Persons | history
When the Second World War ended, millions of people in Europe found themselves far from home, and a great many Ukrainians were among them. The war had carried them west: as forced laborers taken to Germany, as prisoners of war, or as refugees fleeing the returning Soviet army.

The Allies had agreed that Soviet citizens should be sent home, and many were, some of them by force. For Ukrainians who remembered the famine and the purges of the 1930s, going home could mean prison or worse, and many refused. So they waited, sometimes for years, in camps for displaced persons in Germany and Austria, and they turned those camps into small Ukrainian towns, with schools, churches, choirs, newspapers, and theaters.

In 1948, Congress passed the Displaced Persons Act, and America's doors opened. Tens of thousands of Ukrainians came, and many of them settled here, around Saint Nicholas and along Chicago Avenue. They were different from the farmhands and laborers of the first migration. Among them were engineers, teachers, doctors, and former soldiers, though many at first took whatever work they could find.

Then they built. In 1951, ten of them gathered in a borrowed room on Chicago Avenue with a few hundred dollars between them, three hundred and fifty-nine, by the institution's own count, and founded a credit union they called Selfreliance. For its first couple of years, volunteers ran it. In the 1950s and 1960s, it lent money to more than twelve hundred Ukrainian families to buy homes, helping the neighborhood stay Ukrainian while others around it changed. Today it's the largest Ukrainian American credit union in the country, and it celebrated its seventy-fifth anniversary in 2026, still headquartered a few blocks from here.

The newcomers founded much more: Saturday schools to teach their children Ukrainian, scouting groups, dance troupes, choirs, and the museum down the street. Some of the older immigrants found them proud and political, and the two generations didn't always see eye to eye.
=== DEEPER Domes and Mosaics | architecture
Saint Nicholas parish was founded in 1905 or 1906 by a few dozen Ukrainian laborers, and within a decade they had built this. The architects were Worthmann and Steinbach, a Chicago firm that designed many of the city's Catholic churches, among them Saint Mary of the Angels, the great domed Polish church you may have seen from the 606.

Their model, as you heard, was Saint Sophia in Kyiv, built in the eleventh century, when Kyiv was the capital of the great medieval state called Rus. Byzantine builders liked to cover a church with domes so that from inside, you look up into a series of heavens. Here, the domes rise toward the largest one at the center, and their copper was recently restored, work that won a national award from the copper industry in 2025.

Step inside if you can. Almost every surface is covered with images, and much of what you see dates not from 1915, but from the 1970s, when the parish hired Boris Makarenko, an émigré icon painter, to redo the interior in the Ukrainian Byzantine style. In that tradition, the main dome is reserved for Christ, ruler of the universe, looking down on the congregation, and the curved wall behind the altar for the Virgin Mary.

The great mosaic over the entrance was the work of three émigré artists, Makarenko among them, along with Sviatoslav Hordynsky, one of the best-known Ukrainian painters in exile. Cut off from a homeland they couldn't visit, they used the art of medieval Kyiv to build a bridge back to it.

In 1961, Saint Nicholas became the cathedral of a new diocese for Ukrainian Catholics in the western half of the United States. Not bad for a church built on the wages of factory workers.

As you walk on, compare it with Saints Volodymyr and Olha, down the block, a 1970s take on the same tradition.
=== DEEPER The Monster in Chains | legends
The decorated eggs called pysanky, which you heard about at the stop, are made with a technique that's simple to explain and very hard to master.

The artist starts with a raw egg and a small stylus, a tiny funnel on a handle, filled with melted beeswax. Wherever the wax goes, the color underneath is sealed in. The egg is dipped in dye, the lightest color first, usually yellow, then darker and darker, orange, red, and finally black, with more wax added at each stage. At the end, the artist holds the egg beside a candle flame and wipes away the wax, and the whole design appears at once, in brilliant color, for the very first time.

The patterns carry meanings. Wheat stands for a good harvest, a sun or a star for life and good fortune, and a line that runs around the egg without end stands for eternity. Many of the symbols are thought to be far older than Christianity, from a time when the egg itself was a sign of spring's return, and the church adopted them for Easter.

And then there's the legend, told in the villages of the Hutsuls, the highlanders of the Carpathian Mountains. Somewhere, it's said, a monster lies chained to a cliff. Every year, his servants go out across the world to count how many pysanky have been made. If there are few, the chains loosen, and evil spreads across the earth. If there are many, the chains are pulled tight, and love and goodness hold the monster fast for another year. So as long as people keep making pysanky, the world will be safe.

Other old beliefs held that a pysanka kept in the house protected it from fire and lightning, and some were buried in the fields to bring a good harvest.

Far from home, the tradition became something more: a way of keeping a culture alive. Every spring, Ukrainian families in Chicago still gather around kitchen tables with candles, beeswax, and jars of dye, and every spring, somebody tells the children about the monster in chains.

=== DEEPER A Flag Once Banned | history
The flag arching over the street was born in exile, in New York. On December twenty-second, 1895, a group of Puerto Ricans living in Manhattan met in a hall called Chimney Corner. They belonged to the Cuban Revolutionary Party, which was plotting to free both Cuba and Puerto Rico from Spain, and they adopted a flag for their island modeled on Cuba's, with the colors reversed: red and white stripes, and a white star on a blue triangle. Exactly who designed it is still argued about.

Three years later, the United States took Puerto Rico from Spain in the Spanish-American War, and the flag of one revolution became the emblem of another cause: independence from the United States. To the island's government, that made it dangerous. In 1948, as the Nationalist Party grew more militant, Puerto Rico's legislature passed what people called the gag law. It made it a crime to display the Puerto Rican flag, to sing patriotic songs, or to speak or write in favor of independence, with penalties of up to ten years in prison. Many people were arrested under it, especially after the Nationalist uprisings of 1950.

Then history took a turn. In 1952, Puerto Rico became a commonwealth, with its own constitution, and the new government adopted that very flag as its official banner. The gag law was repealed a few years later. The flag of rebellion had become the flag of the state, and ever since, it has been flown by Puerto Ricans of every political stripe.

Look closely at the blue, though. Many independence supporters prefer a light, sky blue, which they consider the original color of 1895, while the commonwealth's flag has usually been a darker navy, much like the blue of the American flag. Which shade you fly can be a statement.

So can no color at all. In 2016, after Congress put Puerto Rico's finances under a federal oversight board, artists in Old San Juan painted the flag in black and white, as a sign of mourning and resistance. You may spot that version in this neighborhood too.
=== DEEPER Steel for the Steelworkers | look
Stand under one of the flags and look up. It's built of steel pipe, bent and welded so that the stripes seem to ripple, and the whole flag looks as if it's snapping in a stiff wind off the lake. The flags were designed by a Chicago architecture firm, DeStefano and Partners, and put up in 1995, with the backing of the local alderman, Billy Ocasio, Congressman Luis Gutiérrez, and Mayor Richard M. Daley.

Why steel? The flags' backers have said that it was a tribute to the first generation of Puerto Rican migrants, many of whom came to the Chicago area after the Second World War to work in steel mills and foundries, and as welders.

Some of those first migrants arrived in 1946, recruited on the island by a private employment agency called Castle, Barton and Associates. The agency brought men to work as laborers in foundries, and women to work as maids in homes in Chicago and its suburbs. Many found the pay and the conditions far worse than they had been promised. That November, a group of the women picketed the agency's Chicago office, and with the help of graduate students at the University of Chicago and other reformers, they pressed Puerto Rico's government to protect migrant workers. The island's government soon set up an office to look after migrants, and it eventually had representatives in Chicago too. It was one of the first times Puerto Ricans in Chicago organized to defend themselves, and it wouldn't be the last.

In 2022, the city made the two flags official Chicago landmarks, which means they're protected for the future, like the mansions of Wicker Park and the skyscrapers of the Loop. For a community that had been pushed from one neighborhood to the next, that mattered. The flags were meant to say that Puerto Rican Chicago wasn't going anywhere, and now the law says so too.

Come back after dark, when the flags are lit, and you'll see why people here call them the gateways to the neighborhood.
=== DEEPER Roberto Clemente | people
Roberto Clemente was born in 1934 in Carolina, Puerto Rico, the son of a foreman on a sugar plantation. He joined the Pittsburgh Pirates in 1955 and played his whole major league career there, eighteen seasons, as one of the most complete players the game has ever seen. He won four batting titles and twelve Gold Gloves for his fielding, and he had a right fielder's throwing arm that base runners soon learned not to test. He was the National League's Most Valuable Player in 1966, and when the Pirates won the World Series in 1971, he was the most valuable player of the Series too.

It wasn't easy. Clemente was Black and Puerto Rican, and he arrived in an America that was still segregated in many places, in a sport whose writers often mocked his accent by quoting him in phonetic English. He answered by speaking out, about racism, about the respect owed to Latin American players, and about the poor.

On the last day of September 1972, he got his three thousandth hit, a double. It was the last regular-season hit of his life. That December, an earthquake devastated Managua, the capital of Nicaragua, and Clemente organized relief flights from Puerto Rico. When he heard that supplies were being stolen before they reached the victims, he decided to fly along himself, to make sure they got through. On New Year's Eve, the overloaded plane crashed into the sea just after takeoff. His body was never found.

Baseball waived its usual waiting period, and in 1973 he became the first Latin American player elected to the Hall of Fame. Every year, the major leagues give an award in his name for service to the community.

The high school on the corner took his name the next year. It had been Tuley High School, where Saul Bellow studied, and when a new building opened across the street in 1974, its students, many of them Puerto Rican, pressed for it to be named for Clemente.

=== DEEPER Don Pedro | people
Pedro Albizu Campos was born in 1891 near Ponce, on Puerto Rico's southern coast. His mother was of African descent, and he grew up poor. He was also brilliant. A scholarship took him to the University of Vermont, and then to Harvard. During the First World War, he served as an officer in the American army, assigned to a segregated unit, and he saw American racism up close, an experience that shaped the rest of his life. Back at Harvard, he went on to law school, and he's usually counted as the first Puerto Rican to graduate from Harvard Law.

In 1930 he became president of the Nationalist Party, and he turned it into a movement that demanded nothing less than independence. He was a mesmerizing speaker, and he argued that the American takeover of 1898 had been illegal from the start. In 1936, after Nationalists killed the island's American police chief, Albizu Campos and other party leaders were convicted of conspiring to overthrow the government, and he was sent to a federal prison in Atlanta.

The next year, on Palm Sunday, police in Ponce opened fire on a Nationalist march held to protest his imprisonment, and about twenty people were killed. It's remembered as the Ponce Massacre.

He spent most of the rest of his life in prison. Released in the 1940s, he returned to Puerto Rico and was arrested again after the Nationalist uprisings of 1950. He was pardoned, then arrested once more in 1954, after four Nationalists opened fire in the United States Capitol, wounding five congressmen. In prison, he said that he was being bombarded with radiation. His jailers dismissed it as delusion, and some of his supporters believe it to this day. He was pardoned again, gravely ill, in 1964, and died the next year.

To his admirers, he's the greatest Puerto Rican patriot of the twentieth century. To his critics, he led young people into violence and death. Either way, you'll find his face on murals all over this neighborhood, and the little house in front of you carries his name.
=== DEEPER Territory, State, or Nation | politics
Puerto Rico has belonged to the United States since 1898, but it has never been fully part of it. In a series of Supreme Court decisions in the early 1900s, the justices described the island as belonging to the United States, but not a part of it, and that strange in-between status has lasted ever since.

Puerto Ricans are American citizens, as you heard at the start of the tour. Since 1952, the island has governed itself as a commonwealth, with its own constitution and an elected governor. But people living on the island can't vote for president, and in Congress they have a single delegate without a full vote. Move to Chicago, though, and a Puerto Rican can vote for president as soon as they register. That's one reason the island's politics are followed so closely on this street.

On the island, the political parties are organized around the status question. One favors statehood, one favors the commonwealth, and a smaller party favors independence. Puerto Ricans have voted on their status again and again, in referendums from 1967 to 2024. In the most recent ones, statehood came out ahead, but the turnout and the wording were fiercely disputed, the votes weren't binding, and Congress hasn't acted.

In Chicago, the independence cause once took a violent turn. In the 1970s and early 1980s, a clandestine group called the Armed Forces of National Liberation claimed responsibility for more than a hundred bombings in the name of independence, in New York, Chicago, and elsewhere, and several of its members came from Chicago's Puerto Rican community. To their supporters, they were political prisoners. To the families of the people their bombs killed, they were terrorists.

One of them, Oscar López Rivera, a Vietnam veteran who had been a community organizer here, spent about thirty-five years in prison, though he was never tied to a specific bombing. In 2017, President Obama commuted his sentence, and when he came home that May, motorcycles and crowds waving flags escorted him through this neighborhood, to a hero's welcome here, and to outrage elsewhere.
=== DEEPER Bomba and Plena | culture
Bomba and plena are often named together, but they're very different, and once you know what to listen for, you can tell them apart in a few seconds.

Bomba is the older of the two, born on the sugar plantations of colonial Puerto Rico. Its drums, called barriles, were traditionally made from old rum barrels, with a skin stretched over one end. One or two drummers keep a steady rhythm on the lower-pitched drums. The lead drum, called the primo or the subidor, is tuned higher, and it does something unusual. It doesn't lead. It follows the dancer.

That's the heart of bomba. A dancer steps out in front of the drums and challenges the lead drummer with sharp, sudden movements, a flick of the skirt, a stamp of the foot, a turn of the shoulder, and the drummer has to answer each one with a beat, instantly. It's a conversation and a duel at the same time, and when it's going well, you can't tell who's leading whom. Meanwhile a singer calls out a verse, and everyone answers in chorus.

Plena is younger. It took shape around the beginning of the twentieth century, in the working-class neighborhoods of the south coast, and spread to the barrios of San Juan. It's played on panderos, round hand drums like big tambourines without the jingles, and it has one basic rhythm that almost anyone can learn. Its songs tell stories: a hurricane, a scandal, a strike, a crime, the latest gossip. People call plena the newspaper of the people.

For a long time, both were looked down on as the music of poor and Black Puerto Ricans, and by the middle of the twentieth century they seemed to be fading. A revival late in the century, on the island and in New York and Chicago, brought them back. Today community groups here teach bomba and plena to children, and you'll hear them at festivals, at protests, and at parties along Paseo Boricua. If someone hands you a pandero, give it a try.
=== STOP The Humboldt Park Stables
=== DEEPER Humboldt the Explorer | people
In his own lifetime, Alexander von Humboldt was one of the most famous people in the world.

He was born in Berlin in 1769, into a wealthy Prussian family, and as soon as he had money of his own, he spent it on a voyage. From 1799 to 1804, with a French botanist named Aimé Bonpland, he explored Spain's colonies in the Americas: the rainforests along the Orinoco River in Venezuela, the Andes, Cuba, and Mexico. They traveled thousands of miles by canoe, mule, and foot, collected tens of thousands of plant specimens, and measured everything they could: temperature, altitude, air pressure, even the blueness of the sky. In 1802, Humboldt climbed most of the way up Chimborazo, in Ecuador, then believed to be the highest mountain on earth, higher than anyone on record had climbed before.

Out of those journeys came a new way of seeing nature, not as a collection of separate things, but as a web, in which climate, plants, animals, and people all depend on one another. He noticed how cutting down forests could change a region's climate, and he wrote against slavery, which he had seen in Cuba. His last great work, Kosmos, tried to describe the whole universe, and it was a bestseller. The cold ocean current off the coast of Peru is named for him, along with mountains, rivers, towns, and many plants and animals.

Germans everywhere treated him as a national hero, and when he died, in 1859, he was mourned around the world. Ten years later, the hundredth anniversary of his birth was celebrated in cities across the United States, especially by German Americans, and that was the year this park was named for him.

There's a fitting twist to the story. Humboldt's journeys made him a hero in Latin America too, where he's often honored as the scientific discoverer of the continent, and where his name is on everything from schools to a national park in Cuba. More than a century after Chicago's Germans named this park for him, it became the heart of Puerto Rican Chicago.
=== DEEPER Heroes in Bronze | look
The immigrants who settled around this park in the late 1800s filled it with their heroes, and each statue was a statement about who they were.

The Germans came first. The bronze of Alexander von Humboldt, near the boathouse, was unveiled in 1892, paid for by a German-born brewer named Francis Dewes. A year later came a statue of Fritz Reuter, a writer most Americans have never heard of. Reuter wrote in Low German, the everyday speech of northern Germany, and as a young man he had spent years in Prussian prisons for belonging to a student movement for German unity and freedom. For Chicago's Germans, some of whom had left home for political reasons of their own, he was a hero of the right kind: funny, plain-spoken, and a bit of a rebel.

The Norwegians answered in 1901 with Leif Erikson, the Viking who reached North America around the year 1000. Notice the day they chose to dedicate him: October twelfth, the anniversary of Columbus's landing. That was almost certainly no accident. Scandinavian Americans had argued for decades that Leif, not Columbus, was the first European to reach America, and here they made the point in bronze. The statue has moved since, though only about two hundred and fifty feet, in 1950.

The Poles followed in 1904 with the grandest monument of all, Thaddeus Kosciuszko on horseback. Kosciuszko was the engineer who fortified West Point for the Americans during the Revolution, and who later led an uprising for Polish freedom. You won't find him here today, though. In the 1970s, the statue was taken down when a ball field next to it was enlarged, and in 1978, Polish American organizations had it restored and moved to the lakefront, where it now stands on Solidarity Drive, near the museums.

So when you walk through the park, you're walking through a kind of argument in bronze: about who discovered America, about who deserves to be remembered, and about whose park this is.
=== DEEPER Bellow's Humboldt Park | culture
Saul Bellow was nine when his family came to Chicago, in 1924, and settled in a tenement near this park. His father tried one business after another. The neighborhood the boy grew up in was poor and crowded, and to him it was endlessly stimulating: full of immigrants arguing politics on street corners, orators on soapboxes, missions, and branch libraries where a bookish kid could read his way into the wider world.

At Tuley High School, he found friends who were just as hungry for books and ideas. Among them were Isaac Rosenfeld, who became a gifted essayist and critic, and Sydney J. Harris, who became one of Chicago's best-known newspaper columnists. They read everything they could find, argued about all of it, and dreamed of being writers. Bellow's mother died when he was seventeen, a loss that echoes through his fiction.

He went on to the University of Chicago and then to Northwestern, and by the 1940s he was publishing novels. The Adventures of Augie March, which you heard about at the stop, made his name in 1953 and won the first of his three National Book Awards. Then came Henderson the Rain King, Herzog, and, in 1975, Humboldt's Gift, which won the Pulitzer Prize. Its title character, the brilliant, self-destroying poet Von Humboldt Fleisher, was modeled on Bellow's friend Delmore Schwartz.

Bellow taught for some three decades at the University of Chicago, and he wrote about this city all his life: its politicians and gangsters, its intellectuals and hustlers, its neighborhoods and their ghosts. He wrote with a mix of love and exasperation, and with an ear for the way Chicagoans really talk, that few other writers have matched.

He died in 2005, at the age of eighty-nine. The streets around this park have changed completely since his boyhood, but in his books, the Humboldt Park of the 1920s is still alive, with its peddlers, its schemers, its scholars, and its strivers, all of them talking at once.

=== DEEPER Jensen's Later Years | people
Jens Jensen ran the West Side parks for about fifteen years after the reformers brought him back, and he used them as a laboratory.

His masterpiece was Columbus Park, on the far West Side, which he designed in the years around the First World War. There he created a whole prairie landscape in miniature: a broad meadow, a winding prairie river, a swimming hole edged with stone, and something you won't find in any other Chicago park he designed, a council ring. That's a low circle of stone benches around a fire pit, where people could sit facing one another as equals, to tell stories, sing, or talk things over. For Jensen, it was democracy in the shape of a landscape, and he built council rings wherever he could.

He was also a crusader. In 1913, he founded a group called the Friends of Our Native Landscape, to save wild places across the Midwest, and its members would include the great conservationist Aldo Leopold. Jensen campaigned to protect places like the Indiana Dunes, and he argued that every city child deserved to know the prairie, the woods, and the river.

Around 1920, after more battles with the politicians who ran the parks, he left the park system for good. He designed estates for wealthy clients, among them Henry Ford and Julius Rosenwald, but his heart was always in public landscapes.

After his wife died in the 1930s, he moved to the family's summer place at Ellison Bay, on the Door County peninsula in Wisconsin. There, in 1935, he founded The Clearing, a school in the woods where people could come to learn from the land itself, and from each other, in the spirit of the folk schools of his native Denmark. It's still running, on the bluffs above Green Bay. Jensen died there in 1951, at the age of ninety-one.

His ideas have outlived him. Today's movement to plant native gardens, and to bring prairie back into cities, follows the path he cut a century ago.
=== DEEPER The 1977 Uprising | history
Eleven years after the riot on Division Street, it happened again, here in the park.

On June fourth, 1977, Puerto Rican families filled Humboldt Park after that year's Puerto Rican parade. In the course of the day, there was gunfire, and two young Puerto Rican men, Julio Osorio, who was twenty-six, and Rafael Cruz, who was twenty-five, were shot and killed by Chicago police.

What happened, and why, has been disputed ever since. The police said they had moved in after a gang shooting in the park that had already wounded several people. Many in the crowd said the police had provoked the trouble and fired recklessly. Either way, the killings set off about a day and a half of rioting. Crowds, mostly of young Puerto Ricans, fought with the police, and many people were hurt and arrested.

There was little or no accountability for the two deaths, and for many in the community, the lesson was bitter. Eleven years and many promises after 1966, it seemed that not enough had changed.

But the neighborhood answered in its own way. By some accounts, the riot helped spur a new Puerto Rican parade along Division Street, first held in 1978, through the heart of the community instead of downtown. And the generation that came of age in those years went on to build the institutions you've seen on this tour, from cultural centers and schools to Paseo Boricua and its steel flags.

The families of the two men kept their names alive for almost half a century. In June 2026, honorary street signs went up for them at California and Evergreen Avenues, at the edge of the park, near where they died. The dedication called the signs a permanent marker of the community's truth, its grief, and its resilience.
=== DEEPER The Fern Room | nature
Jensen's other great West Side creation, the Garfield Park Conservatory, a couple of miles south of here, is worth the trip, above all for one room.

When it opened in 1908, most greenhouses displayed plants like specimens in a museum, in pots and neat rows. Jensen wanted something else: whole landscapes under glass. Working with the Prairie School architects Schmidt, Garden and Martin, and with a New York firm of greenhouse engineers, he designed a series of huge glass houses whose low, rounded profile, it's often said, was meant to recall a haystack on a Midwestern farm.

The Fern Room is the heart of it. Jensen built a still, dark lagoon, edged with layered stone like the ledges along the prairie river in front of you, and planted its banks with ferns, mosses, and lichens, so that you seem to step into a swamp forest from the age before flowers. That's the idea. About three hundred million years ago, the land that's now Illinois was covered with steamy swamps full of giant ferns and their relatives, and their remains became the coal beds under the state. Jensen's room is a glimpse of that lost world, in the middle of the West Side, and some of its oldest plants have been growing there for generations.

On June thirtieth, 2011, a violent hailstorm hit the conservatory, and roughly half of the glass in several of its houses was shattered, including much of the Fern Room's roof. Rare plants were suddenly open to the sky. It took years to repair all the damage. The new panes are laminated, so that in the next big storm they may crack, but they shouldn't shatter.

The Fern Room reopened with Jensen's lagoon at its center, just as he laid it out. On a winter day, when the park outside is frozen, it's one of the most peaceful places in Chicago: warm, green, quiet, and smelling of wet earth and stone.
