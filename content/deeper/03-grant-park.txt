=== DEEPER The Building They Almost Lost | history
You heard that the library eventually left and the building turned into a cultural center. What you didn't hear is how close it came to being torn down first.

By the late 1960s, the central library had outgrown its palace. The stacks were full, the reading rooms were crowded, and the wiring and plumbing were seventy years old. In a city that was demolishing old buildings by the dozen, the obvious answer seemed to be to knock this one down and put up something modern in its place, and there was serious talk of doing exactly that.

Preservation was not yet a popular cause in Chicago. In those same years, the city lost some of its finest nineteenth century buildings, and the people who protested were usually brushed aside. But this building had friends among architects, writers, and newspaper editors, and they made noise.

Then help came from an unexpected quarter. Eleanor Daley, the wife of Mayor Richard J. Daley, was known to everyone as Sis, and she rarely spoke out on city business. But she let it be known that she was for restoring and keeping all the beautiful buildings. In the Chicago of those years, a word from the mayor's household carried weight. Within about three weeks, the mayor's committee announced that the old library would be saved.

The building went onto the National Register of Historic Places in 1972, and a long renovation followed. In 1977, it reopened as a cultural center run by the library, with exhibitions, a theater, and concerts under the domes. Some library departments stayed on for years, until 1991, when the new central library opened on State Street, named for Mayor Harold Washington.

So when you climb the marble staircase, you're walking through a building that survived because the right person, at the right moment, said it should. Few Chicago buildings of its age were so lucky.
=== DEEPER The Old Soldiers' Lobby | politics
You heard that the Randolph Street end of this building honored the Grand Army of the Republic. It's worth knowing just how powerful those old soldiers were.

The Grand Army was founded in April 1866 in Decatur, in central Illinois, by a former army surgeon named Benjamin Stephenson. It began as a brotherhood of men who had fought for the Union, a way to remember the dead and look after widows and orphans. It grew into something much bigger. By 1890, it had more than four hundred thousand members, with posts in nearly every town in the North.

Those members voted, and they voted together. The Grand Army became one of the most powerful lobbies in American history, and its great cause was pensions. Congress kept widening the benefits for Union veterans and their families, until by the 1890s the pension bill was one of the largest items in the federal budget. Politicians courted the old soldiers relentlessly, and the Grand Army helped elect five presidents who had been Union officers, from Grant to McKinley. Grover Cleveland, who had paid a substitute to serve in his place during the war, vetoed hundreds of private pension bills, and many veterans never forgave him.

One of the Grand Army's leaders was an Illinois man, General John Logan. In 1868, as its commander in chief, he issued an order setting aside the thirtieth of May for decorating the graves of the Union dead. Decoration Day, as it was first called, became Memorial Day.

Here in Chicago, the state set aside the north end of this site for the veterans, and they gathered in the rooms beneath the second glass dome. But even the Grand Army couldn't escape time. As its members died, the rooms grew quiet, and after the Second World War the library took over the whole building. The last member of the Grand Army, a former drummer boy named Albert Woolson, died in Minnesota in 1956, and the order died with him.

Since a restoration finished in 2022, the memorial rooms are open again, and you can stand where the veterans met.

=== DEEPER Six Degrees of Lois Weisberg | culture
For more than twenty years, the person who ran Chicago's cultural life worked out of this building. Her name was Lois Weisberg, and if you've ever seen a painted fiberglass cow standing on a sidewalk, in Chicago or anywhere else, you've seen a little of her influence.

Weisberg became the city's first commissioner of cultural affairs in 1989, under Mayor Richard M. Daley, and she held the job until 2011, one of the longest runs of anyone in his cabinet. Before that, she had done a bit of everything. In 1975, she helped found Friends of the Parks, a citizens' group that still fights for the city's parks and lakefront. In 1983, she joined the staff of Mayor Harold Washington to run the city's special events.

Her ideas were cheerful and practical. She turned a grassy, empty downtown block, known as Block 37, into Gallery 37, a summer program that paid teenagers to make art, her answer to kids hanging around the parks with nothing to do. And in 1999, she helped persuade the mayor to fill the streets with Cows on Parade, hundreds of life-size fiberglass cows decorated by artists. Some Chicagoans rolled their eyes, but the crowds loved them, the cows were auctioned for charity, and cities around the world copied the idea.

That same year, the writer Malcolm Gladwell profiled her in The New Yorker, under the title Six Degrees of Lois Weisberg. His point was that she seemed to know everyone, in every walk of life, and that people like her, whom he called connectors, are how ideas and trends spread. The profile became part of his best-selling book The Tipping Point.

Weisberg left the job in 2011 and died in 2016, at the age of ninety. Some aldermen proposed renaming this building for her. Whatever it's called, the free concerts, exhibitions, and festivals that pour out of it are her monument.

=== DEEPER A Prairie on the Roof | nature
You heard about the tall hedge around the Lurie Garden. Step inside it, because this garden helped change the way public spaces are planted.

It sits entirely on a roof. Beneath the soil is a parking garage, so every plant here grows in a layer of earth laid over concrete. The garden covers about two and a half acres, and it was designed by a team that won a competition in 2000: the Seattle landscape firm Gustafson Guthrie Nichol; the theater designer Robert Israel, who shaped its concept and its lighting; and a Dutch nurseryman named Piet Oudolf, who chose the plants. In fall, the grasses turn the color of wheat, and the seed heads are left standing into winter, for their shapes in the snow and for the birds that feed on them.

A boardwalk runs along a shallow channel of water called the Seam. On a warm day, you can sit on the edge and dangle your feet in it. At night, a long wall beside it is lit from below, and its glow reflects in the water.

The garden is named for Ann Lurie, a Chicago philanthropist, whose family's name you may also see on the children's hospital in Streeterville. Before the city was built, much of the land around Chicago was tallgrass prairie, with grasses taller than a person. This garden is a memory of it, growing on top of a parking garage.

=== DEEPER Sisters and Brothers of America | history
You heard that the World's Parliament of Religions met in this building. Its opening day, September eleventh, 1893, produced one of the most famous speeches ever given in Chicago.

The parliament grew out of a plan by a Chicago lawyer, Charles Bonney, to hold congresses of ideas alongside the world's fair, so that the fair would show off thought as well as machines. A Presbyterian minister, John Henry Barrows, led the organizing committee. For seventeen days, representatives of the world's faiths spoke in turn in a great hall in the new building, the Hall of Columbus, with room for about four thousand people. Fullerton Hall, the museum's auditorium today, occupies roughly the spot where the speakers' platform stood.

On the first day, a thirty-year-old Hindu monk from Calcutta rose to speak. His name was Swami Vivekananda, and he had come to America with little money and few connections. He began with five words: Sisters and Brothers of America. By most accounts, the audience rose and applauded for minutes before he could go on. He spoke about tolerance, and against what he called sectarianism, bigotry, and its horrible descendant, fanaticism, which he hoped this gathering would help bring to an end.

He became the star of the parliament. A New York newspaper called him the greatest figure there, and he spent the next few years lecturing across the United States and in Britain, founding the Vedanta Society in New York. He died in India in 1902, at the age of thirty-nine. India now celebrates his birthday as its National Youth Day, and many Indian visitors to Chicago come here to see where he spoke.

He wasn't the only speaker who left a mark. A Buddhist reformer from Ceylon, Anagarika Dharmapala, made a strong impression. And a Japanese Zen abbot named Soyen Shaku caught the attention of Paul Carus, a publisher in La Salle, Illinois. Carus later brought Soyen Shaku's young student, D. T. Suzuki, to America, and Suzuki went on to do more than anyone to introduce Zen Buddhism to the West.
=== DEEPER The Farmer Was a Dentist | people
You heard that American Gothic won a prize here in 1930. It almost didn't.

The painter, Grant Wood, was a quiet Iowa artist who had studied in Paris and then come home to paint the Midwest. On a visit to the little town of Eldon, in southern Iowa, he noticed a small white farmhouse with a single pointed Gothic window in its upper gable, a fancy church-style flourish on a plain wooden house. He sketched it and imagined the kind of people who might live there.

To paint them, he used people he knew. The woman is his sister, Nan Wood Graham, who was about thirty. The man is his dentist from Cedar Rapids, Byron McKeeby, who was sixty-two. Neither of them farmed. Look at how Wood repeated the shape of the pitchfork, in the stitching of the man's overalls and in the window behind the couple.

Wood entered the painting in the Art Institute's annual exhibition of American art. One of the judges dismissed it as a comic valentine. But a museum patron argued for it, and the jury gave it a bronze medal and a prize of three hundred dollars. The museum bought it that year, and it has been here ever since.

Then came the reaction. Newspapers reproduced it across the country, and many Iowans were offended, convinced that Wood was mocking farm people as grim and narrow, and dozens of them wrote to complain. Nan was mortified that people took her for the wife of a man twice her age, and she said the couple were meant to be father and daughter. Wood insisted he admired the people he painted. In the hard years of the Depression that followed, the stern pair came to look less like a joke and more like a picture of endurance.

The painting has been copied and parodied more than almost any other American image. It rarely travels, and its trip to Paris and London in 2016 and 2017 was its first outside the United States. The house in Eldon still stands, and its visitor center lends out aprons and pitchforks, so you can pose in front of it.

In the spring of 1913, perhaps the most talked-about art exhibition in American history came to this building. It's known as the Armory Show, after the National Guard armory in New York where it had opened that February.

It was America's first big look at modern European art: Cézanne, Van Gogh, and Gauguin, and younger painters like Picasso, Marcel Duchamp, and Henri Matisse. The school's officials had heard about the plan and forbidden it, but some of the teachers were cheering the students on.

History has been kind to Matisse and unkind to his judges. Within a generation, the Art Institute was collecting modern art in earnest. === DEEPER Why Sixty-Six | names
You heard that Route 66 began at this corner. Here's how it got its number, a story of politics, pride, and a good ear.

In 1925 and 1926, federal and state officials set out to number the country's main highways in one national system for the first time. Roads running east and west would get even numbers, and the most important of them would end in zero. A highway official from Tulsa, Oklahoma, named Cyrus Avery was the great champion of a long diagonal road from Chicago to Los Angeles that would run through his state, and he wanted it to be Route 60.

Kentucky objected. Its governor was upset that no route ending in zero would cross his state, and he wanted the number 60 for a road through Kentucky. The argument dragged on for months. A compromise gave the Chicago road the number 62, but Avery didn't like it. Then he and the chief highway engineer of Missouri noticed that the number 66 hadn't been assigned to anything. In April 1926, they sent a telegram to Washington saying they preferred it. Avery thought it would be easy to remember and pleasant to say. The final plan was approved that November.

He was right about the sound. In 1939, John Steinbeck called it the mother road in The Grapes of Wrath, the road that carried families fleeing the Dust Bowl west to California. In 1946, the songwriter Bobby Troup turned it into a song, Get Your Kicks on Route 66, and Nat King Cole made it a hit, with its list of towns from Chicago to the coast. The interstates replaced the old road, and it was officially decommissioned in 1985, but it has never been forgotten. In 2026, it turned a hundred.

One more detail. The Begin sign is on Adams Street, which carries traffic west, and the matching End sign is a block south on Jackson Boulevard, which carries traffic east. And about a mile west on Jackson, across the river, Lou Mitchell's restaurant has been serving breakfast since 1923, and Route 66 travelers have long treated it as their first stop.

You heard that Kate Buckingham gave this fountain in memory of her brother. She was one of the most generous and least visible people in Chicago's history.

Her father, Ebenezer Buckingham, made the family fortune in grain elevators, the giant storehouses along the river that held the wheat and corn of the Midwest before it was shipped east. The family spread into banking, steel, and real estate. Kate never married. She was devoted to her brother Clarence and her sister Lucy Maud, and as each of them died, she inherited the family wealth and gave much of it away.

Clarence had fallen in love with Japanese art at the world's fair of 1893, and he spent the rest of his life collecting Japanese woodblock prints. His collection, some two and a half thousand prints, went to the Art Institute, where it's still counted among the finest outside Japan. Kate had her own passion. When the fountain was finished, she didn't trust the city to keep it up. === DEEPER The Machine Underneath | underfoot
Everything you see at Buckingham Fountain depends on something you can't see: a two-level pump house buried beneath the plaza.

The fountain holds about a million and a half gallons of water. During the big shows, the pumps push about fourteen thousand gallons a minute through the jets, enough to drive the central column to its full height while the smaller jets and the sea horses' spouts play around it. There are three main pumps, of different sizes, and some of the original equipment from 1927 kept running for decades.

For years, the computer that ran the shows wasn't even in Chicago. By one account, the control system sat in Atlanta, Georgia, until a renovation in 1994 moved it into the pump house. In 2013, the Park District replaced it with modern controls.

The 1994 renovation, which cost about two point eight million dollars, rebuilt the three smaller upper basins, and later projects have gone on repairing the marble, the plumbing, and the lights. Every fall, the fountain is shut down for the winter, and it comes back to life in the spring, an event many Chicagoans treat as a sign that the cold is finally over.

While you're here, look at the sea horses with fresh eyes. The French sculptor Marcel Loyau studied real sea horses in a scientific collection in Paris before he modeled them, and the figures won him a national prize at the Paris Salon of 1927. === DEEPER The Railroad That Wanted the Lake | politics
You heard about Montgomery Ward's lawsuits to keep the lakefront open. There was an older fight over this shoreline, and it went all the way to the Supreme Court. Its result still shapes how Americans treat their lakes, rivers, and coasts.

In the years after the Civil War, the Illinois Central Railroad was one of the most powerful companies in the state. Its tracks already ran along the shore on a trestle, and it wanted much more: a harbor, docks, and room to grow. In 1869, the Illinois legislature passed what became known as the Lake Front Act. It handed the railroad more than a thousand acres of lake bottom off downtown, essentially the whole bed of the Chicago harbor, to fill and build on. Charges of corruption hung over the vote from the start.

Public anger grew, and in 1873 the legislature repealed the act. The railroad refused to accept it, arguing that a grant is a grant, and that the state couldn't take back what it had given. It kept building along the shore while the lawsuits dragged on for years.

In December 1892, the Supreme Court decided the case, four votes to three. Justice Stephen Field wrote for the majority. The bed of the lake, he said, wasn't like an ordinary piece of land that the state could sell. The state held it in trust for the people, so that they could fish, sail, and travel on the water, and a legislature could no more give that trust away to a private company than it could give away its power to govern. The repeal stood.

For most of a century, the decision was a quiet precedent. Then, in 1970, a law professor named Joseph Sax argued that it should be the foundation of a public trust doctrine to protect natural resources, and courts around the country took up the idea. Lawyers in cases over beaches, rivers, and wetlands still cite the case of the Illinois Central and the Chicago lakefront.

So as you look out from the fountain toward the water, remember that part of what keeps it public is a ruling about this very shore.

You heard that the Stevens family built this hotel and lost it, and that one of the family's sons became a Supreme Court justice. His name was John Paul Stevens, and his long life ran through some of the great moments of the American century.

=== DEEPER The Column from Ostia | politics
You heard about Balbo Drive. About a mile south of here, in a grove of trees just east of Soldier Field, stands a stranger reminder of Italo Balbo: a real column from ancient Rome.

It was a gift from Benito Mussolini. After Balbo's squadron of seaplanes landed on the lake in 1933, Mussolini had a column taken from Ostia, the old port of ancient Rome, and shipped to Chicago. It's about two thousand years old, and it stands on a modern stone base, dedicated in 1934. The inscription on the base, in Italian and English, says that Fascist Italy, by command of Benito Mussolini, presents the column to Chicago in honor of the flight.

Balbo's own story ended badly. He fell out of favor in Rome, and he is said to have disliked Mussolini's alliance with Hitler and his anti-Jewish racial laws. He was sent away to govern Libya, and in 1940, early in the Second World War, his plane was shot down over Tobruk by mistake, by Italian anti-aircraft gunners. In English, his name lived on in an odd way: for years, any big formation of aircraft was called a balbo.

For decades, few people paid attention to the column. That changed in 2017, after a deadly white nationalist rally in Charlottesville, Virginia, set off a national argument about monuments. Protesters gathered here to demand that the column come down and that Balbo Drive be renamed. Many Italian American groups objected, saying the monument honored a great feat of flying and the pride of Italian immigrants in the 1930s, not fascism. In 2018, the city gave Ida B. Wells a different street. In 2020, it took down the statue of Christopher Columbus that had stood in Grant Park since the 1930s. And in 2022, a city advisory committee recommended removing the Balbo monument.

Before you leave this corner, look again at the two bronze horsemen at the entrance to Congress Plaza. Their maker, Ivan Meštrović, lived one of the most dramatic lives of any artist whose work stands in Chicago.

He was born in 1883 to a poor Croatian family and grew up in a village in the stony hills of Dalmatia, herding sheep and carving figures from wood and stone. With help from patrons, he went to study at the academy in Vienna, and by his late twenties he was famous across Europe, hailed by many as the great sculptor of the South Slavs.

Chicago's horsemen came from the height of his career, and they were installed in 1928. Walk around them. The riders are Native American warriors, bare-chested, one drawing a bow that isn't there, the other poised to hurl a spear that isn't there either. Notice the tension in the horses and in the riders' arms, as if the whole group is caught in the instant before the arrow flies. Because your eye supplies what's missing, the action feels even more alive.

Then history caught up with him. When the Axis powers broke up Yugoslavia in 1941, Croatia fell under a Fascist regime called the Ustaše. He taught at Syracuse University and then at the University of Notre Dame, in South Bend, Indiana, where he kept working until his death in 1962. Much of his late work was religious, shaped by the war and by his months in prison.

So the horsemen at the park's gate are the work of a man who became an exile, made in his golden years, long before the world he knew came apart.

=== DEEPER The Man Who Stopped the Music | people
You heard that the free summer concerts in Grant Park were started by James Petrillo. For decades, he was one of the most feared men in American entertainment.

Petrillo was born in Chicago in 1892, the son of Italian immigrants. He started out playing the trumpet, but his real gift was organizing. In 1922, he became president of the Chicago Federation of Musicians, the local musicians' union, and he ran it with an iron hand for decades. Radio stations, hotels, and theaters learned that if they crossed Petrillo, their musicians would walk out.

His Grant Park concerts began in 1935, in a bandshell that stood here on Hutchinson Field. Its successor, the Petrillo Music Shell, stands to the north, near Columbus Drive and Jackson Boulevard, and in 1984 it became the first home of the Chicago Blues Festival, a year after the death of Muddy Waters.

In 1940, Petrillo became president of the national union, the American Federation of Musicians, and he took on the record companies. Records and jukeboxes, he argued, were putting live musicians out of work, and the players who made the records got nothing when they were played again and again. On August first, 1942, in the middle of the Second World War, he ordered union members to stop making records altogether. There were no exceptions, not even for symphony orchestras or famous bandleaders. Decca gave in in 1943, but the two biggest labels, Columbia and Victor, held out until November 1944, when they agreed to pay royalties into a union fund.

The strike changed popular music. Singers didn't belong to the musicians' union, so the record companies kept recording them, backed by choirs of voices instead of instruments. During the ban and after it, the singers who had been featured with the big bands became stars on their own, and the big bands began to fade.

Congress struck back in 1946 with a law aimed squarely at his tactics, which everyone called the anti-Petrillo act. He led the national union until 1958, and he died in 1984, at the age of ninety-two.

=== DEEPER The Taste | food
Every summer for decades, this end of Grant Park smelled of barbecue smoke, fried dough, and cheesecake. The reason was the Taste of Chicago, once billed as the biggest food festival in the world, and it began as a one-day party.

=== DEEPER Who Owned Sue | history
You heard that Sue was found in South Dakota in 1990 and sold at auction after a legal fight. The fight was stranger than most people know.

The bones turned up on a summer day in August 1990. A crew from a private fossil company, the Black Hills Institute, had a flat tire, and while most of the team drove into town to get it fixed, Sue Hendrickson went walking along a bluff and spotted bones weathering out of the rock. They turned out to belong to the most complete Tyrannosaurus anyone had ever dug up.

The bluff was on a ranch owned by Maurice Williams, a member of the Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe, and the institute paid him five thousand dollars. The company's president, Peter Larson, believed they had bought the fossil. Williams later said the money was only for permission to dig. And there was a bigger complication. Because the land lay inside a reservation, it was held in trust by the federal government, and the government had a say over what was taken from it.

In 1992, agents of the FBI, backed by the National Guard, raided the institute and took the skeleton away. The courts eventually ruled that Sue belonged to Williams, with the federal government as trustee. In the long federal investigation that followed, Larson was convicted on customs charges and sent to prison, a sentence many of his supporters thought far too harsh.

Williams decided to sell, and Sotheby's auctioned the skeleton in New York on October fourth, 1997. There were nine bidders, and the bidding was over in about nine minutes. The Field Museum won, with backing from McDonald's, Walt Disney World Resorts, and the California State University system, for a total of about eight point four million dollars, by far the most ever paid for a fossil at that time.

=== DEEPER The Man Who Brought Animals Back to Life | people
When you walk into the great hall of the Field Museum, look for the two African elephants locked in battle. They're the work of one of the most remarkable people ever to work here, Carl Akeley.

Akeley grew up on a farm in western New York and learned taxidermy at a natural history supply house in Rochester. In those days, the craft mostly meant stuffing skins with straw and rags until they looked lumpy and sad, and he thought animals deserved better. He came to Chicago in 1896 to work for the young Field Museum and developed a new method. First he sculpted the animal's body in clay, muscle by muscle, from careful measurements and photographs. Then he made a light, hollow form from the clay model and stretched the skin over it. The results looked alive.

He and his wife, Delia, collected the elephants on an expedition to East Africa, and they went on display in 1909. When the museum moved to this building, they came by train and took up their places in the great hall, where they have stood for more than a century.

Akeley's life was a string of adventures. In Somaliland, by his own account, a leopard attacked him, and he killed it with his bare hands. On Mount Kenya, a wild elephant nearly crushed him to death. And he never stopped inventing. When the museum's first home, the old exposition building in Jackson Park, began to crumble, he devised a gun for spraying cement onto its walls, an ancestor of the sprayed concrete used today in tunnels and swimming pools. He also designed a motion picture camera light and quick enough to follow animals in the field, and the filmmaker Robert Flaherty used two of them to shoot Nanook of the North.

In 1909, Akeley moved on to the American Museum of Natural History in New York, and later he studied mountain gorillas in central Africa. He persuaded King Albert of Belgium to protect them, and in 1925 the king created Albert National Park, the first national park in Africa. Akeley died there the next year, of dysentery, on the slopes of Mount Mikeno, and he is buried in the park he helped create.
=== DEEPER Fourteen Seconds | culture
You heard about the famous fight here in 1927 between Gene Tunney and Jack Dempsey. Here's what the crowd saw.

It was a rematch. A year earlier, in Philadelphia, Tunney, a careful, clever boxer and a former Marine, had taken the heavyweight title from Dempsey, the most ferocious puncher of his time. Everyone wanted to see Dempsey try to win it back. The paid attendance here was reported as a hundred and four thousand, nine hundred and forty-three, and the gate came to more than two and a half million dollars, said to be the first time any event had taken in two million. Millions more listened on the radio.

For six rounds, Tunney outboxed Dempsey. Then, in the seventh, Dempsey caught him with a flurry of punches, and Tunney went down for the first time in his career. Under a rule that was still new, a fighter who scored a knockdown had to go to the farthest neutral corner before the referee would start the count. Dempsey, out of long habit, stood over his fallen opponent and then went to his own corner. The referee, Dave Barry, refused to begin counting until Dempsey moved, and precious seconds passed before he did.

Tunney got up at the count of nine. But by most estimates, he had been on the canvas for about fourteen seconds, well past the ten that would have ended the fight. He kept away from Dempsey for the rest of the round, knocked him down in the eighth, and won the decision.

Dempsey's fans swore he had been robbed. Tunney always insisted he could have gotten up sooner. Dempsey never fought for the title again. Tunney defended it once more and then retired as champion, married an heiress, and became a businessman who liked to lecture on Shakespeare. His son, John Tunney, became a United States senator from California.

You heard that the Shedd's first seawater came by railroad. The numbers were remarkable. In 1930, a fleet of twenty tank cars made eight round trips between Key West, Florida, and Chicago to bring in about a million gallons of ocean water, enough to fill the saltwater galleries.

=== DEEPER A Machine That Made the Stars | culture
You heard that the Adler was a first for this side of the Atlantic. Here's how it came to be, and why it was so new.

In the early 1920s, engineers at the Carl Zeiss company in Jena, Germany, invented a machine that could project the night sky onto the inside of a dome: thousands of stars, the planets in their wandering paths, the sun and the moon, all moving as they do in nature, at whatever speed you liked. The first public planetarium opened at the Deutsches Museum in Munich, the great museum of science and technology.

In 1928, Max Adler traveled to Germany with an architect, Ernest Grunsfeld Junior, and they toured the Deutsches Museum with its founder, Oskar von Miller. Adler came home determined to give Chicago a planetarium of its own. He bought a Zeiss projector, the first to operate outside Europe, and paid for a building to house it. The planetarium opened on May twelfth, 1930, his birthday.

Grunsfeld's design is a landmark of its era: a low, twelve-sided building of polished granite under its copper dome, with almost no windows, so that it looks more like a monument than a museum.

The first director, an astronomer named Philip Fox, wanted more than a sky show. He persuaded Adler to buy a celebrated collection of antique scientific instruments assembled by a dealer in Amsterdam: astrolabes, sundials, globes, and telescopes, some of them centuries old. It made the Adler a museum of the history of astronomy as well as a theater of the stars. Look also for the Atwood Sphere, a hollow metal globe about seventeen feet across, first shown at the Chicago Academy of Sciences in 1913, with hundreds of tiny holes punched through it in the patterns of the stars. On a guided tour, you can ride inside it while light pours through the holes and a sky of pinpoint stars turns around you. It's the oldest working sky simulator in the world, a homemade planetarium from before the Zeiss machine existed.

The original projector was retired long ago, but the Adler's first sky shows started a planetarium boom in America. Within a few years, cities from Philadelphia to New York to Los Angeles had their own.

=== DEEPER The Highway in the Sky | nature
Twice a year, the air above you becomes one of the great highways of North America. Millions of songbirds, warblers, thrushes, sparrows, and many more, migrate between their breeding grounds in the north and their winter homes in the south, and many of them travel at night along the western shore of Lake Michigan. The lake works like a guide rail. Birds that would rather not cross open water follow the shoreline, and it funnels them right past downtown Chicago.

For a small bird finding its way by the stars, a brightly lit city is a trap. Lit windows draw birds in and confuse them, and glass reflects trees and sky so convincingly that birds fly straight into it. Few places show this more starkly than McCormick Place, the convention center just south of here on the lakefront, whose Lakeside Center has long walls of glass facing the lake.

For about forty years, scientists and volunteers connected with the Field Museum, among them a longtime bird collections manager named David Willard, have walked around that building on migration mornings, collecting the birds that died overnight. Their specimens form one of the longest records of bird collisions anywhere. On a normal morning, they might find anywhere from none to fifteen. But after one night in October 2023, during an enormous wave of migration, volunteers picked up nearly a thousand dead birds. Willard described a carpet of them along the windows.

The records had already shown what helps. Researchers found that when fewer windows were lit, far fewer birds died, and Chicago's Lights Out program asks building owners downtown to dim their decorative lights during migration. After the disaster of 2023, McCormick Place spent more than a million dollars covering the Lakeside Center's glass with a film that birds can see. The next fall, volunteers counted fewer than twenty deaths there.

So if you're here at dawn in May or October, look and listen. Northerly Island's restored prairie and wetlands have become a resting place for exhausted travelers, and the shrubs may be full of birds that have flown all night, with a long way still to go.
