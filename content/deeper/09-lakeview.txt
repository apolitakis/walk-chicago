=== DEEPER First in the Nation | politics
In 1961, Illinois became the first state in the country to stop treating sex between consenting adults of the same sex as a crime. It happened quietly. Lawyers rewriting the state's entire criminal code left the old sodomy law out, the legislature passed the new code, and the governor signed it that summer. Hardly anyone remarked on it at the time.

The police didn't change their habits. Gay bars were raided for years afterward, on charges like public indecency or keeping a disorderly house. The most notorious raid came in the early hours of April twenty-fifth, 1964, at a bar called the Fun Lounge, just outside the city limits near O'Hare. Cook County sheriff's deputies arrested more than a hundred people, and the newspapers printed many of their names, their addresses, and even where they worked. People lost their jobs, and some lost their families.

The raid helped push Chicago's gay men and lesbians to organize. In 1965 they founded a group called Mattachine Midwest, borrowing the name of an older homophile society on the West Coast. It published a newsletter, spoke up when the police raided bars, and helped people who had been arrested. Its legal adviser was Pearl Hart, a lawyer who had spent decades defending clients other lawyers wouldn't touch, from immigrants facing deportation to gay men caught in raids, and who stood by the group until her death in 1975. Today, Chicago's library and archive of gay and lesbian history carries two names, Gerber Hart. One is Henry Gerber, whose house you can see in Old Town. The other is Pearl Hart.

Change at City Hall came slowly. An ordinance banning discrimination based on sexual orientation was voted down by the City Council in 1986. Mayor Harold Washington had backed it. After his death, under Mayor Eugene Sawyer, it finally passed, in December 1988, by twenty-eight votes to seventeen. In 2013, Illinois lawmakers voted to let same-sex couples marry, and by the middle of 2014, couples could marry anywhere in the state.
=== DEEPER The Plague Years | history
In 1981, doctors in New York and California began reporting rare cancers and pneumonias in young gay men. Within a year or two, the same illnesses were turning up in Chicago. The disease was named AIDS, and for most of the next fifteen years there was no effective treatment. In this neighborhood, as in gay neighborhoods across the country, a generation of young men sickened and died, often within months of being diagnosed.

Much of the early response came from the community itself. Back in 1974, a few medical students and volunteers had started a small clinic offering cheap testing for sexually transmitted infections to gay men. It was soon named for Howard Brown, a former top health official of New York City who had come out publicly as gay a little over a year before he died. When the epidemic arrived, Howard Brown was already there and already trusted. It became one of the city's front lines, caring for the sick and taking part in some of the earliest research on the disease, and it's now one of the largest health centers of its kind in the country.

Others chose protest. In April 1990, the activist group Act Up brought a national demonstration to Chicago. One of its targets was the county's public hospital, which, the protesters charged, had no beds for women in its AIDS ward. Many of the demonstrators were arrested.

To see how the city remembers those years, walk east to the lake, just south of Belmont Harbor. From the 1960s into the 1990s, the stepped limestone blocks along the shore there, known as the Belmont Rocks, were a favorite gathering place for gay men, a place to sunbathe, swim, and be seen. That spot is now the AIDS Garden Chicago. Its centerpiece, installed in 2019, is a thirty-foot sculpture called Self-Portrait, by the artist Keith Haring, who died of AIDS in 1990 at the age of thirty-one. It's the city's first public monument to the epidemic.
=== DEEPER Jane and Mary | people
Among the people honored on these pylons is Jane Addams, the founder of Hull House, whose public life is told on the West Loop tour. She's here because of a part of her life that her early biographers mostly left out: her forty-year partnership with a woman named Mary Rozet Smith.

Mary Rozet Smith came from a wealthy Chicago family, and she began volunteering at Hull House soon after it opened in 1889. She was about eight years younger than Addams, and much less public. She became one of the settlement's most generous supporters, helping to pay for its buildings and programs, and she became the center of Addams's private life. By most accounts, Addams's earlier closest friend, Ellen Gates Starr, who had founded Hull House with her, drifted to the edge of that life as Mary moved to the middle of it.

The two women traveled together, and one surviving letter shows them asking a hotel for a double bed rather than two singles. In 1904 they bought a summer house together on the coast of Maine, near Bar Harbor. When they were apart, Addams wrote to Smith constantly, and she referred to their partnership as a marriage. Many of their letters are gone, because Addams burned them.

What should we call them? Historians still argue over the words. Neither woman used the terms we would use today, and both guarded their privacy. What isn't in doubt is that for four decades, each was the other's closest companion, and the people around them understood them as a couple.

Mary Rozet Smith died of pneumonia in February 1934. Addams, whose own health had been failing for years, died fifteen months later, in May 1935.
=== STOP Wrigley Field
=== DEEPER The Lunchroom King | history
Charles Weeghman made his fortune feeding the Loop's office workers. Starting in 1901, he opened a chain of quick-service lunchrooms downtown, where a clerk could grab a sandwich and coffee and be back at a desk in minutes. By 1914 he was a millionaire, and he wanted a ball club.

The established leagues weren't selling, so Weeghman joined an upstart. The Federal League declared itself a third major league in 1914 and began luring players away from the other two. Weeghman leased this block, where a Lutheran seminary had stood, and hired Zachary Taylor Davis, the architect who had designed Comiskey Park for the White Sox four years earlier. Work began on March fourth, 1914, and the first game was played on April twenty-third, about seven weeks later, in a single-deck grandstand of steel and concrete that held about fourteen thousand people.

The team was known that first season as the Chi-Feds, and then as the Whales, and in 1915 the Whales won the Federal League pennant. But the league was losing money. It had sued the older leagues under the antitrust laws, in the courtroom of a federal judge named Kenesaw Mountain Landis, who let the case sit while the two sides negotiated. After the 1915 season they made peace. The Federal League disappeared, and as part of the deal, Weeghman was allowed to buy the Cubs, from Charles Phelps Taft, the half-brother of former President William Howard Taft.

Weeghman's luck soon ran out. His lunchrooms faltered, and within a few years control of the club had passed to one of his partners, William Wrigley Junior. The ballpark kept growing, and an upper deck went on in the late 1920s. In 1941 the club added the round clock on top of the scoreboard, with dots in place of numbers. In all the years since, no batted ball has ever hit that scoreboard. The golfer Sam Snead is said to have hit it in 1951, with a golf ball he drove from home plate, but golf balls don't count.
=== DEEPER Baseball in Daylight | politics
The first night game in the major leagues was played in Cincinnati in 1935, and other ballparks soon followed. Philip Wrigley, who ran the Cubs after his father's death, held out. By the usual account, he finally bought the equipment to light this field in 1941, and the day after Pearl Harbor he gave it all to the war effort. He never bought lights again. Day baseball became part of what it meant to be a Cubs fan: games after school, sunburns in the bleachers, and office workers with mysterious afternoon appointments.

The darkness produced one of the ballpark's greatest moments. On September twenty-eighth, 1938, the Cubs and the Pittsburgh Pirates were fighting for the pennant, tied in the ninth inning, with dusk falling so fast that the game was about to be called. With two strikes on him, the Cubs' catcher and manager, Gabby Hartnett, hit a home run that many in the crowd could barely see. The papers called it the Homer in the Gloamin'. It put the Cubs in first place, and they clinched the pennant a few days later.

In 1981, the Wrigley family sold the team to the Tribune Company, which owned the television station that carried the games, and the new owners wanted lights. Many neighbors were furious. They feared crowds, traffic, and noise on summer nights, and one group picked a name whose initials spelled the team's: Citizens United for Baseball in Sunshine. Lawmakers in Springfield and at City Hall passed rules that effectively barred night games here. Then baseball's commissioner warned that if the Cubs ever reached the postseason, their home games might have to be played somewhere else.

In 1988, the city gave way. The first night game was set for August eighth, 1988, a date full of eights. It was rained out after three and a half innings, and the first official night game at Wrigley Field was played the following evening. Even now, the city limits how many night games can be played here.
=== DEEPER Curses and Near Misses | legends
Cubs fans blamed a billy goat, as you heard. But over the decades, they collected omens the way other fans collect autographs.

In 1969, the Cubs had one of their best teams in memory, with four future Hall of Famers: Ernie Banks, Ron Santo, Billy Williams, and the pitcher Fergie Jenkins. In mid-August they led their division by nine games. Then, on September ninth, during a game at Shea Stadium in New York, a black cat appeared from under the stands, circled Ron Santo as he waited on deck, and stared toward the Cubs' dugout. The Cubs lost that game. The next day the New York Mets passed them in the standings, and the Mets went on to win the World Series.

In 1984, the Cubs needed to win just one of three games in San Diego to reach the World Series. They lost all three. In the last one, a ground ball rolled through the legs of the first baseman, Leon Durham, and a story grew up that a teammate had spilled a sports drink on his glove in the dugout.

In 2003, the Cubs were five outs from the World Series, leading the Florida Marlins three to nothing here at Wrigley in the sixth game of the playoffs. A foul ball drifted toward the seats down the left field line. As the left fielder, Moisés Alou, leaped for it, several fans reached up too, and one of them, a young man named Steve Bartman, deflected it. The Marlins scored eight runs that inning and won again the next night. Bartman had to be escorted from the ballpark for his own safety, and he has avoided the public eye ever since. A restaurant group bought the ball at auction for more than a hundred thousand dollars, and in 2004 it was blown up in a televised ceremony.

When the Cubs finally won in 2016, the team gave Steve Bartman a World Series ring. Fans still argue over whether there was ever a curse at all, or just a great deal of bad baseball.
=== DEEPER Voices of the Cubs | culture
Long before most fans saw this ballpark, they heard it. In the 1930s, a young announcer at W H O, a radio station in Des Moines, Iowa, broadcast Cubs games without leaving his studio. A telegraph operator at the ballpark sent the bare facts, a strike, a fly ball to center, and the announcer turned them into lively play-by-play. His name was Ronald Reagan.

Reagan loved to tell about a day in 1934 when the wire went dead in the ninth inning of a tie game, with Dizzy Dean pitching for the Cardinals. Rather than admit it, he had the batter foul off pitch after pitch for several minutes, until the line came back. Usually, in his telling, the batter was the Cubs' shortstop, Billy Jurges, though other versions name someone else. It's the kind of story that got better every time he told it.

In 1937, Reagan persuaded the station to send him to the Cubs' spring training on Catalina Island, off California, which the Wrigley family owned. While he was out west, he took a screen test at Warner Brothers, and the studio signed him. Half a century later, near the end of the 1988 season, he came back to Wrigley Field as president, threw out the first pitch, and called part of the game from the broadcast booth.

His host in the booth that day was Harry Caray, the voice many fans remember best. Caray had called games for the Cardinals and the White Sox, and at Comiskey Park he liked to sing along with Take Me Out to the Ball Game during the seventh-inning stretch. As Caray told it, the Sox owner, Bill Veeck, the same man who had planted the ivy here, secretly switched on the public address microphone one day in 1976, and a tradition was born. Caray brought it here when he joined the Cubs in 1982, and with games on WGN, carried by cable across the country, he helped make the Cubs a national team. Since his death in 1998, guest singers, from movie stars to astronauts, have led the crowd.
=== STOP Alta Vista Terrace
=== DEEPER The Workingman's Reward | companies
Alta Vista Terrace was a showpiece. Samuel Eberly Gross made his real fortune with something much plainer: small houses sold on credit to people who had never owned property.

Gross worked on a grand scale. He would buy a whole tract of farmland at the edge of the city, lay out streets, plant trees, put up dozens or hundreds of similar cottages, and give the development an appealing name. Then he advertised as no one in Chicago had before. His ads ran in the city's foreign-language newspapers as well as the English ones, and his big color posters showed neat cottages with gardens and families out front. One of the best known was titled The Workingman's Reward.

The pitch was simple. A family could move in after a small down payment and pay by the month, in amounts not much higher than rent. Instead of enriching a landlord, the payments bought a home of their own. For immigrants from countries where only the gentry owned land, that was a powerful promise. Gross wasn't the only developer selling houses this way, but he was the biggest. By one count he laid out more than twenty subdivisions and built some ten thousand houses, from neighborhoods near the stockyards to a whole suburb west of the city, which he named after himself: Grossdale. In 1905 its residents renamed it Brookfield.

Buying on installments had a hard edge. Under many contracts of the time, a family that fell behind could lose the house and everything it had paid in. And Gross's empire ended abruptly. In 1904, with several new projects barely begun, he sold off his holdings and left Chicago. The houses stayed, and many of his cottages are still lived in today, more than a century later.
=== DEEPER Swallowing Lake View | politics
When the first families moved onto Alta Vista Terrace, Lake View had been part of Chicago for only about a dozen years. Its annexation, in 1889, was part of the largest expansion in the city's history, and the deciding issue was water.

Lake View had grown fast in the 1880s. It became a city of its own in 1887, and it ran its own waterworks, drawing from the lake. But it was also pouring its sewage into the lake, and by local accounts its water was fouled again and again, with outbreaks of typhoid and cholera. A small city couldn't afford the pumping stations, sewers, and fire and police protection that Chicago could provide, and more and more residents decided they'd be better off inside the big city.

On June twenty-ninth, 1889, voters in Lake View, in Hyde Park to the south, and in several other towns and townships went to the polls to decide whether to join Chicago. Supporters promised better services and the prestige of a great city. Opponents warned about Chicago's taxes, its politicians, and its saloons. Some of the most determined opposition came from temperance voters in dry districts, who feared that once they were part of Chicago, the saloons would follow.

Annexation carried. In one day, Chicago grew by about a hundred and twenty-five square miles, and the census the next year showed that it had passed Philadelphia to become the second-largest city in the country, with more than a million people.

The vote didn't clean up the water by itself. That same year, the state created the Sanitary District, which went on to build the canal that reversed the river, the story told on the river tour. As for Lake View, the city vanished from the map, but its name never did. The neighborhood still uses it, run together into a single word.
=== DEEPER What the Street Signs Say | names
The street signs around the ballpark read like a puzzle, with a Civil War battle, a Connecticut railroad builder, a lake storm, and one real mystery.

Start with Belmont Avenue, a few blocks south. It's named for the Battle of Belmont, fought on November seventh, 1861, when Union troops attacked a Confederate camp at Belmont, Missouri, on the Mississippi River. It was a small, confused fight that ended with the Union soldiers hurrying back to their riverboats, and it would be long forgotten if not for their commander, a little-known brigadier general named Ulysses Grant. It was his first battle of the war.

Sheffield Avenue, which runs behind the right field bleachers, honors Joseph Sheffield, a Connecticut businessman who helped build the railroad from Chicago west to Rock Island, on the Mississippi, and who bought land on Chicago's North Side. Back east, Yale named its school of science for him after he gave it a fortune.

Waveland Avenue, behind the left field bleachers, takes its name, according to the usual story, from the lake waves that once washed over this low, sandy ground in storms.

Then there's Addison Street, which runs past the main entrance. It's usually said to honor Thomas Addison, a London doctor who first described the disorder of the adrenal glands now called Addison's disease. But nobody has found a convincing reason why a Chicago street would be named for him, and he seems never to have had anything to do with the city.

Finally, Irving Park Road, which leads to the cemetery, comes from a suburb laid out after the Civil War and named, by most accounts, for the writer Washington Irving, the author of The Legend of Sleepy Hollow. So as you walk toward Graceland, you're heading, in a manner of speaking, for the Headless Horseman.
=== STOP The Music Box Theatre
=== DEEPER Stars Indoors | architecture
The kind of theater in front of you was the invention of one man, the architect John Eberson. Born in what was then Austria-Hungary, he came to America around the turn of the twentieth century and designed theaters across the South and the Midwest. His Majestic Theatre in Houston, opened in 1923, was the first of what he called atmospheric theaters. Instead of a dome dripping with plaster and chandeliers, the auditorium was made to feel like the open air. The side walls became the facades of an imaginary Mediterranean town, and overhead was a smooth plaster sky.

Part of the appeal was cost. A traditional movie palace needed acres of ornate plasterwork. An atmospheric got most of its magic from paint, lighting, and the audience's imagination, so it could be built faster and cheaper. In the late 1920s, atmospherics went up all over the country. Eberson himself designed several in Chicago, including the Avalon on the South Side, which survives as the New Regal, and the vast Paradise on the West Side, torn down in the 1950s.

The Music Box was a small cousin, designed by the Chicago architects Edward Steinborn and Louis Simon, and it was a careful business proposition. The theater is tucked inside a larger building, with stores along the street and apartments upstairs, so their rents could help carry it. The auditorium itself cost about a hundred and ten thousand dollars. It opened on August twenty-second, 1929. About two months later, the stock market crashed, and the great boom in theater building soon came to an end.

When you go in, look at the side walls before you look up. They're designed to suggest the walls of old buildings around an Italian courtyard, so the dark ceiling above reads as the sky. Nearly a century later, the trick still works.
=== DEEPER The Talkies | history
The Music Box opened at a turning point in movie history. In 1927, Warner Brothers released The Jazz Singer, with a few minutes of synchronized songs and speech, and audiences were thrilled. Within two years, theaters across the country were scrambling to install sound equipment, and the studios had all but stopped making silent films.

The Music Box was built for the new era. Its first feature, according to theater historians, was a talking picture called Mother's Boy, starring the popular tenor Morton Downey. Yet the building also had chambers for organ pipes, a sign of how fast things were changing. Just a few years earlier, every movie house needed live music to accompany silent pictures, from a lone organist in a small theater to a full orchestra in a palace. Sound put thousands of theater musicians out of work. The Music Box's organ chambers stood empty for more than half a century, until 1984, when a custom electronic organ was installed, with its speakers hidden where the pipes would have gone.

Today the theater is a guardian of film itself. Its projection booth is one of the few left that can still run the big seventy-millimeter prints made for epics like Lawrence of Arabia and 2001: A Space Odyssey, and the theater holds a festival of them every year.

The people behind the Music Box have also shaped what the rest of the country sees. In 2007, the company that runs the theater started a distribution business, Music Box Films, which brings foreign and independent movies to American screens. One of its early hits was the original Swedish film of The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo, in 2010. So a neighborhood movie house on Southport Avenue helped decide what was playing in art houses from coast to coast.
=== DEEPER Selig's Jungle | companies
About a mile and a quarter west of here, around Irving Park Road and Western Avenue, stood one of the first real movie studios in America. Its founder, William Selig, had been a stage magician and the manager of a traveling minstrel show before he got hold of a motion picture camera in the 1890s and began making short films in Chicago.

In 1907, Selig built a proper studio, filling the block bounded by Irving Park, Western, Byron, and Claremont, with a glass-roofed stage to catch the daylight and a backlot for outdoor scenes. The Selig Polyscope Company turned out westerns, comedies, and adventure pictures by the hundreds, and it gave a young cowboy named Tom Mix his start in the movies. Mix went on to become the biggest western star of the silent era.

Selig's most notorious production came in 1909. Former President Theodore Roosevelt had just left for an African safari, and the whole country wanted to see it. Selig couldn't get a camera to Africa, so he staged the hunt himself. He hired a vaudeville actor who looked something like Roosevelt, bought an aging lion for a few hundred dollars, dressed a set as the jungle, and filmed the hunt. Hunting Big Game in Africa was a hit. Newspapers of the day reported that it had been shot right here in Chicago, and plenty of moviegoers, it's said, thought they were watching the real thing.

Selig also saw that Chicago's gray winters were a problem for an industry that depended on sunlight. In 1909 he sent a crew to Los Angeles, and his company is often credited with building the first permanent movie studio there. Over the next decade the whole industry followed the sun west. Selig's Chicago studio didn't survive the 1910s, and Essanay, the city's other big studio, which you can visit on the Uptown tour, didn't last much longer. One building from Selig's lot still stands, on Claremont Avenue at Byron Street.
=== STOP Graceland Cemetery: the Gate & Eternal Silence
=== DEEPER A Garden for the Dead | history
Graceland belongs to a movement that changed how Americans buried their dead. In the early 1800s, most city dead went into crowded churchyards or bleak public burying grounds. Then, in 1831, Boston's leading families opened Mount Auburn Cemetery, outside the city in Cambridge, as a landscaped garden with winding paths, ponds, and trees, where the living could stroll and remember. It was such a success that cities across the country copied it, and for a generation these garden cemeteries served as America's first great public parks.

Chicago's own city cemetery, on the lakefront where Lincoln Park is now, was low, wet, and much too close to the city's drinking water. In 1860, a lawyer and businessman named Thomas Bryan bought land out here, then well beyond the city limits, and founded Graceland. Bryan was a devoted gardener, and a few years later he was the man who bought Lincoln's own draft of the Emancipation Proclamation at the city's great Sanitary Fair. The first plan of the grounds was drawn by Swain Nelson, a Swedish-born gardener. Then William Le Baron Jenney, the engineer who later designed the Home Insurance Building downtown, was hired to drain the marshy ground and dig the little lake to the north, Lake Willowmere.

The person who shaped Graceland most was Bryan's nephew, Bryan Lathrop, who led the cemetery for decades. Lathrop wanted the grounds to read as one continuous landscape, not a city of little fenced family yards. In the 1870s, on the advice of the landscape architect Horace Cleveland, the cemetery even turned the gravel paths between the graves into lawn, and owners were steered away from walling off their lots with fences, hedges, and stone curbs.

That's why Graceland feels so different from older burial grounds. Here the monuments stand in a landscape, instead of the landscape squeezing in between the monuments.
=== DEEPER The Quiet Revolutionary | nature
Much of the landscape around you is the work of Ossian Cole Simonds, one of the most influential American landscape designers most people have never heard of.

Simonds grew up on a farm in Michigan and studied engineering and architecture at the University of Michigan, where one of his teachers was William Le Baron Jenney. In 1878, Jenney brought him here to help with the drainage. Simonds stayed for more than half a century, as the cemetery's superintendent until 1897 and as its consulting landscape gardener until his death in 1931.

From Bryan Lathrop he learned the English style of naturalistic landscape design, with its sweeping lawns and irregular groves. But Simonds gave it a Midwestern accent. Instead of exotic imports, he favored the trees and shrubs that grew wild in the region, like oaks, hawthorns, and dogwoods, and he planted them in thick masses along the edges, the way they grow at the margin of a woodland. The centers he left open, so you look down long, sunlit lawns framed by trees. As you walk, notice how the drives curve so that each bend opens a new view, and how the plantings screen out the busy streets beyond the walls.

Simonds became one of the founders of the American Society of Landscape Architects, in 1899, though he always preferred to call himself a landscape gardener. He designed parks, campuses, and estates across the Midwest, and he worked for years on the northern extension of Lincoln Park. In 1915 the writer Wilhelm Miller named him, along with Jens Jensen and Walter Burley Griffin, as a creator of what Miller called the prairie style of landscape gardening, the outdoor cousin of Frank Lloyd Wright's Prairie School houses. In 1920 Simonds put his ideas into a book, Landscape Gardening. Its advice comes down to this: study nature, and learn from it.
=== DEEPER Baseball, Incorporated | companies
William Hulbert's stone baseball marks the grave of the man who turned the game into a business.

Hulbert was a Chicago coal dealer and a backer of the White Stockings, a professional team founded in 1870. The Great Fire of 1871 burned their ballpark, and the club sat out the next two seasons. By 1875 Hulbert was running the team, and he secretly signed four stars of the champion Boston club, including the pitcher Albert Spalding, plus a young infielder from Philadelphia named Adrian Anson. That broke the rules of the existing league, so Hulbert organized a new one. In February 1876, he and other club owners met at a hotel in New York and founded the National League. Unlike the old league, it was run by the owners, not the players, and a few years later it adopted the reserve clause, which bound a player to his team for as long as the team wanted him. Chicago won the new league's first championship.

Spalding pitched and managed, and in 1876 he and his brother opened a sporting goods store in Chicago. It grew into one of the biggest sporting goods companies in the world, and for a century it supplied the league's official baseball. When Hulbert died in 1882, Spalding took over the club.

The team's great star was Anson, known as Cap, a big first baseman who led Chicago to five pennants in the 1880s and became, by most counts, the first player to reach three thousand hits. He also made a public show of refusing to play against Black players, and historians count him among the men most responsible for the color line that kept Black players out of the major leagues until 1947.

The team went through several nicknames. Early in the twentieth century, sportswriters started calling the young squad the Cubs, and the name stuck.
=== DEEPER The Fight of the Century | people
Jack Johnson won the heavyweight championship in Australia, the day after Christmas in 1908, and much of white America immediately went looking for someone to take it back from him. Newspapers called the search a hunt for a Great White Hope. The man they settled on was Jim Jeffries, the retired former champion, who had never lost a fight. Jeffries came out of retirement, he said, to prove that a white man was better than a Black man.

They met in Reno, Nevada, on July fourth, 1910, in an outdoor ring built for the occasion, before a crowd of nearly twenty thousand. Johnson toyed with Jeffries, smiling and talking as he fought, and in the fifteenth round, after Jeffries had been knocked down for the first time in his career, his corner stopped the fight.

That night, as Black Americans celebrated, white mobs attacked them in cities across the country, and a number of people were killed. Even the film of the fight became a battleground. Many cities banned it, and two years later Congress banned the shipping of prizefight films across state lines altogether, a ban that lasted until 1940.

After his conviction, Johnson fled to Europe. In 1915 he lost his title to Jess Willard, in an open-air fight in Havana that went twenty-six rounds. In 1920 he surrendered and served his sentence at the federal penitentiary in Leavenworth, Kansas, where, the story goes, he worked out an improved wrench that he later patented.

He spent his last years boxing in exhibitions, giving talks, and telling his own story to anyone who would listen. In June 1946 he was driving fast through North Carolina when his car crashed, and he died soon afterward. By the usual account, he had just stormed out of a roadside diner that refused to serve him because he was Black. He was sixty-eight years old.
=== STOP Graceland: the Architects & the Tycoons
=== DEEPER The Eye That Never Sleeps | people
Allan Pinkerton came to America on the run. Born in Glasgow in 1819, he trained as a cooper, a barrel maker, and as a young man he joined the Chartists, a movement demanding the vote for working men. In 1842, with the authorities after him, he married and sailed for America.

He settled in Dundee, Illinois, on the Fox River northwest of Chicago, and opened a cooperage. One day in 1847, looking for wood for barrel staves on an island in the river, he stumbled on a camp of counterfeiters. He helped the sheriff round them up, and before long he was a deputy sheriff himself. In 1849 he became Chicago's first full-time police detective, and around 1850 he went into business for himself. His agency's emblem was a wide-open eye over the words We Never Sleep, and it's often said that's where the phrase private eye comes from.

Pinkerton was a fierce abolitionist. In March 1859, when John Brown passed through Chicago with a group of people he had freed from slavery in Missouri, Pinkerton sheltered them, gave Brown five hundred dollars, and got them onto a train to Detroit, on the road to Canada. That fall, Brown led his raid on Harpers Ferry.

During the Civil War, Pinkerton ran intelligence for General George McClellan, under the name Major E. J. Allen, and his reports badly overestimated the size of the Confederate army, which helped make the cautious general even more cautious. After the war, his agency chased train robbers and outlaws across the country.

His sons built it into one of the biggest private police forces in the country, hired by companies to guard property and break strikes. In 1892, a gun battle between Pinkerton guards and striking steelworkers at Homestead, Pennsylvania, left men dead on both sides. Pinkerton didn't live to see it. He died in 1884, and the story goes that he slipped on a sidewalk, bit his tongue, and died of the infection that followed.
=== DEEPER Sullivan's Three Tombs | architecture
Louis Sullivan designed only three tombs in his career, and two of them are here. Seeing them side by side is one of the best ways to understand what he was after.

The first is the Ryerson tomb, built in the late 1880s for Martin Ryerson, a lumber merchant who had died in 1887. His son, also named Martin, was one of Sullivan's patrons, and later one of the great benefactors of the Art Institute and the University of Chicago. Sullivan made the tomb of polished dark granite, combining two ancient Egyptian forms, a low, flat-topped tomb and a pyramid. It's heavy, dark, and severe.

The second is the Getty tomb, from 1890, which you heard about at this stop. Henry Getty built it for his wife, Carrie. Frank Lloyd Wright, who was working in Sullivan's office then, later wrote that outside of music he could think of no finer requiem.

The third is in Saint Louis. The Wainwright tomb, from 1892, was built for the wife of Ellis Wainwright, a brewer, whose office tower in Saint Louis Sullivan had just designed. It's a stone cube topped by a dome, plainer than the Getty tomb and lighter than the Ryerson.

All three show Sullivan working on the problem that occupied him all his life: how to make an architecture of his own time that didn't simply copy the past. Even when he borrowed ancient forms, he stripped them down and then gave them ornament of his own, drawn from plants and geometry.

A few steps from Sullivan's own grave, look for the small marker of Richard Nickel, the photographer who spent his life documenting and trying to save Sullivan's buildings, and who died in 1972 inside one of them, the Stock Exchange, as it was being torn down.
=== DEEPER Reading the Stones | look
Graceland rewards wandering, and its old monuments speak a language of their own. Once you know a few words of it, you can read the stones as you walk.

An obelisk, the tall, tapering shaft borrowed from ancient Egypt, stood for eternity, and it was hugely fashionable among wealthy Victorians. A column broken off partway up marks a life cut short. An urn draped with cloth is a sign of mourning, the veil between the living and the dead. A torch turned upside down means a life put out. A lamb usually marks the grave of a child. And if you see a gravestone carved to look like a tree stump, it may belong to a member of the Woodmen of the World, a fraternal insurance society that provided tree-stump markers to its members.

Then look for a few of Graceland's stranger monuments. Peter Schoenhofen ran one of Chicago's big breweries, makers of Edelweiss beer, and his family's tomb, designed by the architect Richard Schmidt, is a small stone pyramid. Guarding its door are a sphinx on one side and a Christian angel on the other, as if the family wanted to cover every possibility.

On the grave of Victor Lawson, who published the Chicago Daily News for nearly half a century, stands a towering knight gripping a sword. It's called the Crusader, and it's by Lorado Taft, the sculptor of Eternal Silence. The words carved below come from an old book of scripture: Above all things, truth beareth away the victory. It's a fitting motto for a newspaperman.

And look for the grave of Fazlur Khan, the structural engineer behind the Hancock Center and the Sears Tower. The architects who shaped the skyline are here, and so is one of the engineers who made it stand up.
