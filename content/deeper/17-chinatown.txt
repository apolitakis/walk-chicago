=== DEEPER The Man With Three Names | people
The man whose motto crowns the gate went by several names, and each one tells part of his story. He was born in 1866 as Sun Wen, in a farming village in southern China, not far from Macau. At thirteen, he sailed to Hawaii, where an older brother had prospered, and he studied at a missionary school in Honolulu. Later he trained as a doctor in Hong Kong, where he was baptized a Christian.

In the West, he's known as Sun Yat-sen, the Cantonese way of saying a name he took as a young man. In China, he's best known as Sun Zhongshan. During years of exile in Japan, he lived under a Japanese alias, Nakayama, meaning middle mountain, and in Chinese those same characters are read Zhongshan. Today, many Chinese cities have a Zhongshan Road, his home county was renamed Zhongshan in his honor, and the high-collared jacket he made popular is called the Zhongshan suit, although in the West it's better known as the Mao suit.

He spent years as a fugitive, raising money among Chinese communities around the world for the cause of overthrowing the Qing dynasty. In 1896, Chinese officials seized him in London and held him prisoner inside their legation, and he was freed only after his old teacher from medical school went to the newspapers. In 1904, to travel freely in the United States, where the exclusion laws would have kept him out, he obtained papers claiming that he'd been born in Hawaii.

In October 1911, he was in Denver, on a fundraising tour, when he read in a newspaper that a revolt had broken out in the city of Wuchang. Province after province broke away from the empire, and on the first day of 1912, he was sworn in as the provisional president of the new republic. In return for the young emperor's abdication, he soon agreed to hand the presidency to a powerful general, Yuan Shikai. He died in Beijing in 1925, his work unfinished. Taiwan honors him today as the father of the nation, and mainland China as a forerunner of its revolution.
=== DEEPER The Year Beast | legends
Long ago, the story goes, a monster called the Nian lived in the mountains, or in some tellings beneath the sea. On the last night of every year, it came down to the villages to devour the crops, the animals, and sometimes the people, and every year, the villagers fled into the hills. Then one year, an old beggar stayed behind. When the beast arrived, he was waiting, with red paper pasted on the doors, candles burning, and stalks of bamboo bursting with loud cracks in a fire. The Nian, terrified of the color red, the light, and the noise, ran away. The old man, some say, was an immortal in disguise.

That's why, as the story is told, the new year is red and loud. The Chinese phrase for celebrating the new year, guo nian, can be read as getting past the Nian. Families paste red banners on either side of the door, written with lucky verses, and set off firecrackers, whose ancestors were those bursting stalks of bamboo. The old Chinese word for firecracker means exploding bamboo.

Some say the lion dance began the same way, as a way to scare off evil with a creature fiercer than the evil itself. Before a new lion dances for the first time, a dot of red is painted on its eyes, to wake it and bring it to life. And when the lion reaches for the lettuce a shopkeeper has hung over the door, it's playing on words: in Cantonese, the word for lettuce sounds almost exactly like the words for growing wealth. The lion eats the greens and then spits them out, scattering good fortune over the shop and the crowd.

Look in shop windows for the character fu, meaning good fortune, hung upside down. That's another pun. The word for upside down sounds like the word for arrived, so an upside-down fu announces that good fortune has come. And about a week before the new year, many families send off the Kitchen God, who's said to report on the household to the Jade Emperor in heaven. They offer him sweets, so that he'll have only sweet things to say.
=== DEEPER Built to Be Seen | architecture
Why do so many American Chinatowns look alike? Part of the answer lies in San Francisco. In April 1906, an earthquake and the fires that followed left its Chinatown in ashes, and some city leaders saw their chance to move the Chinese out of the heart of the city for good. The neighborhood's merchants fought back, and one of them, Look Tin Eli, had an idea. Chinatown would be rebuilt in a style so exotic, so unmistakably Chinese, that it would become one of the sights of San Francisco, and too valuable to move.

Two of the first new buildings went up at the corner of Grant Avenue and California Street in 1907 and 1908. Their designers were Americans who had never been to China. Working largely from old drawings of Chinese temples, they put pagoda towers, upturned eaves, and colored tiles on top of ordinary Western commercial buildings. The look was a hit with visitors, and it spread to Chinatowns across the country. It's a style you'd hardly find in China itself, a kind of architecture invented in America.

The On Leong Building, finished two decades later, is one of the grandest examples, and like the buildings in San Francisco, it was designed by architects who weren't Chinese. Its towers, glazed tiles, and terra cotta show an American idea of China, commissioned by Chinese Americans to tell the city who they were.

The colors carry meaning. Red stands for luck and joy, and it's the color of weddings and the new year. In imperial China, yellow was reserved for the emperor, and gold suggests wealth. Green tiles, like the ones on the gate, were used on temples and on buildings a step below the emperor's, like the palaces of princes.

The gate itself follows an old Chinese form called a paifang, a ceremonial archway. In Chinese cities and villages, archways like this marked the entrances to neighborhoods, or honored local worthies: a famous scholar, a devoted son, or a widow known for her virtue. Here, it marks the front door of Chinatown.

=== DEEPER Gold Mountain | history
Most of the Chinese who came to America in the first great wave came from one small corner of China: a handful of rural counties in the delta of the Pearl River, southwest of Guangzhou. The most important of them was Taishan, also spelled Toisan, a county of rocky hills and thin soil, where many families couldn't live on what their land produced. When word reached the delta that gold had been found in California, young men began leaving for what they called Gold Mountain.

Over the following decades, Taishan sent so many of its sons overseas that it became known as the home of the overseas Chinese. For generations, the Chinese most often heard in American Chinatowns, Chicago's included, was the Taishan dialect, which can be hard to follow even for speakers of standard Cantonese. Many of Chicago's early merchants, among them members of the Moy family, came from Taishan.

The money the emigrants sent home transformed the place. Families with a man on Gold Mountain built new houses, schools, and ancestral halls. One emigrant who had made his fortune in Seattle, Chin Gee Hee, came home and built a railroad through the county, early in the twentieth century, with money raised from Chinese overseas.

In the neighboring county of Kaiping, returning emigrants built well over a thousand fortified towers, called diaolou, to protect their families from bandits, who knew which households received money from abroad. Many of the towers mix Chinese roofs with columns, domes, and balconies copied from buildings the emigrants had seen in America and Europe, and in 2007, they were named a World Heritage Site.

For many of the men who worked in Chicago's laundries and restaurants, home meant one of those villages, and the family they'd left there. Some came back to Chicago after visits home with new wives or sons, and some went home for good. Today, many Chinese American families can still name the village their great-grandparents left, and some travel back to see the halls and houses that Gold Mountain money built.
=== DEEPER The Interrogation Rooms | politics
The paper son system began with a disaster. On April eighteenth, 1906, an earthquake struck San Francisco, and in the fires that followed, the city's birth records burned. Chinese men who'd been in America for years could now say they had been born in San Francisco, and no one could prove otherwise. As citizens, they could visit China and report the birth of a son there, and every son on paper was a right of entry to the United States. Those rights, called slots, could be sold to young men who weren't related at all, and each paper son took the name of the family whose slot he'd bought.

The government knew what was going on, and it fought back with questions. From 1910 to 1940, most Chinese arriving in San Francisco were held at the immigration station on Angel Island, in San Francisco Bay, while inspectors questioned them, sometimes for days. How many windows did your house in the village have? Who lived in the third house in your row? Then the inspectors asked the paper father the same questions, and compared the answers. A few mistakes could mean being sent back to China.

So paper sons studied. They bought coaching books, full of details about the families and villages they were claiming, and memorized them, and many are said to have thrown the books overboard as their ship came into port. Some waited on the island for weeks, and some for months.

Some of them poured their fear and anger into poems, which they carved into the wooden walls of the barracks. After the station closed, the poems were forgotten, until 1970, when a park ranger noticed the writing on the walls of a building that was due to be torn down. Well over a hundred poems have since been recorded, and the barracks are now a museum.

In the 1950s, the government offered paper sons a chance to confess, and many were allowed to keep their status. Tens of thousands came forward, although some were deported. Even so, many Chinese American families still carry paper names, and some only learned their true family names decades later.
=== DEEPER The Laundrymen | companies
Chicago's first Chinese laundry opened in 1872, on Madison Street, and by 1883, there were nearly two hundred of them across the city.

Why laundries? In China, washing clothes was mostly women's work, and few of the men who came to America had ever done it. But in the American West, where women were scarce, there was plenty of demand, and few white men wanted the job. A laundry took little money to start: a rented storefront, some tubs, and a stove to heat the irons. It didn't require much English, and it didn't compete head-on with white workers, which mattered at a time when Chinese workers were being driven out of mines, factories, and whole towns.

The work was grueling. Laundrymen often worked from early morning until late at night, scrubbing by hand and pressing shirts with heavy irons, and many of them slept in the back of the shop. Customers usually got a ticket marked with Chinese characters, and its matching half was tied to the bundle.

The laundries also produced one of the great cases in American law. In the 1880s, San Francisco required a permit to run a laundry in a wooden building, and the city granted permits to all but one of the white applicants while turning down every Chinese applicant, about two hundred in all. A laundryman named Yick Wo took his case to the Supreme Court, and in 1886, the justices ruled unanimously that a law enforced so unequally violated the Constitution's promise of equal protection, even for people who weren't citizens. Lawyers still cite the case today.

In the 1930s and 1940s, a University of Chicago student named Paul Siu, himself a Chinese immigrant, spent years getting to know Chicago's laundrymen for his doctoral research. He described men he called sojourners, who lived in America, sometimes for decades, while their hearts and plans stayed in China. His study, The Chinese Laundryman, is still read as a classic portrait of Chinese Chicago in the years of exclusion.
=== STOP The Nine Dragon Wall, the Library & Chinatown Square
=== DEEPER Three Walls in China | architecture
Only a handful of old nine dragon walls survive in China, and the three most famous each have their own story.

The oldest and biggest stands in the city of Datong, in the north. It was built in 1392, in front of the palace of a Ming dynasty prince, a son of the dynasty's founder. It's about a hundred and fifty feet long and more than twenty-five feet high, and although the palace is long gone, the wall survived.

The wall that Chicago's is modeled on, in Beihai Park in Beijing, was built in 1756, in the reign of the Qianlong emperor. Unlike most screen walls, it's decorated on both sides, with nine great dragons on each face, and by one count, more than six hundred dragons in all, large and small.

The third, inside the Forbidden City, was built in the 1770s for the same emperor, in front of the palace where he planned to spend his retirement. It comes with a legend. As the story goes, when the wall was nearly finished, a worker broke one of the glazed tiles on the belly of a white dragon. There was no time to fire a new one before the inspection, and the punishment for the mistake would have been death. So a craftsman carved a replacement out of wood overnight and painted it to match, and no one noticed. Look closely at that dragon today, and you can see the piece of wood among the tiles, where its paint has worn away.

The wall here is a smaller, partial copy of the one in Beihai Park, made of glazed tiles from China. The Chinatown Chamber of Commerce dedicated it on September ninth, 2003, a date full of nines, and a plaque calls it one of only three such walls outside China. Look at the colors of the nine great dragons, and then try counting the small ones. There are said to be more than five hundred.
=== DEEPER The Great Race | legends
Why these twelve animals, and why in this order? The best-known answer is a story. Long ago, the Jade Emperor, the ruler of heaven, announced a race. The first twelve animals to cross a great river and reach him would each have a year named after them.

The rat was small and couldn't swim well, so it asked the ox for a ride, and the good-natured ox agreed. Just as the ox reached the far bank, the rat leaped off its head and scurried ahead to finish first. That's why the rat leads the zodiac and the ox comes second. The tiger fought the current to come in third, and the rabbit hopped across on stones and floating logs. The dragon, who could have flown across and won easily, stopped to bring rain to a village that needed it, and came in fifth. And so on, down to the pig, who stopped for a meal and a nap, and came in last.

And the cat? In one version, the cat and the rat were friends, and they planned to ride across together on the ox. But the rat didn't wake the cat on the morning of the race, or in other tellings pushed it into the river, and the cat was left out of the zodiac altogether. Cats have chased rats ever since. In Vietnam, though, the cat got its revenge: the Vietnamese zodiac has a year of the cat in place of the rabbit.

The animals are only half of the system. Each year is also linked to one of five elements, wood, fire, earth, metal, and water, so it takes sixty years for every animal to meet every element. Some combinations carry superstitions of their own. In Japan, an old belief held that girls born in a year of the Fire Horse would bring misfortune to their husbands, and in 1966, a Fire Horse year, the number of births in Japan fell by about a quarter. The next Fire Horse year arrived in 2026.
=== DEEPER Yum Cha | food
In Cantonese, going out for dim sum is called yum cha, which simply means drinking tea, and the tea comes first. The custom grew up in the teahouses of Guangzhou, where travelers and merchants stopped to rest, and over time, the snacks served with the tea became the main attraction. Dim sum is usually translated as touching the heart: little things to eat that hit the spot. An old phrase for a proper morning out is one cup, two pieces, a pot of tea and a couple of dumplings.

In the old style of service, servers push carts through the dining room, you point at what you want, and they mark a card on your table. Many places now hand you a paper menu to check off instead. Either way, a few dishes are classics. Har gow are shrimp dumplings in a wrapper so thin it's nearly clear, and a skilled chef, it's said, should pleat each one at least seven times. Siu mai are open-topped dumplings of pork and shrimp. Char siu bao are fluffy steamed buns filled with sweet barbecued pork. There are rice noodle rolls, slippery and soft, and phoenix claws, which is the elegant name for chicken feet, braised until they're tender. For dessert, try an egg tart, a cousin of the British custard tart and the Portuguese pastel de nata, which came to Cantonese bakeries by way of Hong Kong and Macau.

There's etiquette too. When someone pours tea for you, tap two fingers on the table to say thank you. The story goes that the Qianlong emperor, traveling in disguise, once poured tea for one of his servants. The servant couldn't kneel and bow to the emperor without giving away his secret, so he bent two fingers and tapped them on the table, like a tiny pair of kneeling legs. And when your teapot is empty, leave the lid open or tilted to one side, and someone will come by to refill it.
=== STOP Ping Tom Memorial Park
=== DEEPER The Noodle Maker's Son | people
Ping Tom was born in Chicago in 1935, into one of Chinatown's best-known families. His father, Tom Y. Chan, had been born in 1881 in a village in southern China, in the same county as Sun Yat-sen. He learned to make noodles in New York, came to Chicago, and founded the Chinese Noodle Company, which made egg noodles and the skins for wontons and egg rolls. He became a civic leader too, raising money for Chinese war orphans and for American war bonds during the Second World War. When he died, in 1944, he couldn't be buried beside his wife in the cemetery where she lay, for reasons that some believe were racist.

Ping went to the Haines School in Chinatown, and then won a scholarship to the Francis W. Parker School on the North Side. In time, he ran the family businesses, which came to include the noodle company and a company that made chop suey pails, the folded paper cartons Americans still carry home full of Chinese food. That carton is a Chicago invention too. A Chicago inventor named Frederick Weeks Wilcox patented a version of it in 1894, as a pail for oysters.

But Ping Tom is remembered for the land. He believed Chinatown, boxed in by railroads and expressways, couldn't survive as a neighborhood of families unless it found room to grow. In 1984, he founded a company, the Chinese American Development Corporation, that bought the old Santa Fe railroad land north of Cermak Road and began building on it. He also pressed the city to turn more of the old railroad land along the river into a park for the people of Chinatown.

He didn't live to see it. Ping Tom died of cancer in July 1995, at the age of sixty. On October second, 1999, the Chicago Park District dedicated this park in his name, and it has since grown north along the river, with a field house and the boathouse. Many of the homes you passed on the way here stand on the old railroad land he fought to open up.
=== DEEPER The Poet in the River | legends
The dragon boat races remember a poet. More than two thousand years ago, in the age of the Warring States, Qu Yuan served as a minister to the king of Chu, a great kingdom along the Yangtze. He was a loyal adviser, the story goes, but rivals at court slandered him, and he was sent into exile. There he wrote some of the most celebrated poems in the Chinese language, among them a long lament called Encountering Sorrow.

When he heard that the capital of Chu had fallen to a rival kingdom, he walked into the Miluo River, holding a heavy stone, and drowned. The people who loved him rowed out in their boats to search for him, beating drums and splashing with their paddles to frighten away the fish, so that they wouldn't eat his body. They threw rice into the water for the fish to eat instead. Later, as the story goes, Qu Yuan's ghost appeared and told them that a river dragon was stealing the rice, so they began wrapping it in leaves tied with colored thread.

That's the origin, as it's told, of the Dragon Boat Festival, held on the fifth day of the fifth month of the Chinese calendar, which usually falls in late May or June. Families eat zongzi, sticky rice wrapped in bamboo or reed leaves, filled with pork, egg yolk, beans, or dates, depending on the region. They hang bundles of mugwort and calamus on their doors to keep away illness, because the fifth month was traditionally a season of sickness.

Historians suspect that the festival is older than Qu Yuan, and that it began with rituals honoring dragons, the bringers of rain, and with boat races meant to drive off disease in the hot months. In 2009, the United Nations cultural agency added the Dragon Boat Festival to its list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity. The boats keep the dragon alive: watch for the carved head at the bow, the tail at the stern, and the scales painted along the sides.
=== DEEPER The Bridge That Rises | look
Look south along the river at the railroad bridge with two tall towers. It's the Canal Street railroad bridge, finished around 1914 for the Pennsylvania Railroad, and today it's the only lift bridge on the Chicago River.

It was designed by the engineering firm of Waddell and Harrington, and its senior partner, John Alexander Low Waddell, was the great champion of this kind of bridge. In the early 1890s, he designed one of the first modern lift bridges, which carried Halsted Street over this same South Branch. A lift bridge has one big advantage over the bascule bridges downtown, whose leaves tilt up into the air: its span stays level, so it can be very long and very heavy, which suited a railroad.

The trick is in the towers. The span hangs from steel cables that run up and over giant wheels at the top of each tower, called sheaves, and down to counterweights that weigh about as much as the span itself. Because the span and the counterweights balance each other, the motors don't have to lift the full weight, only to get it moving. As the span rises, the counterweights sink, and if you're watching when the bridge moves, you may see them sliding down inside the towers.

The span is about two hundred seventy feet long, and it weighs about fifteen hundred tons, by most accounts. It replaced an old swing bridge on the same spot, and the new bridge, it's said, was built right above the old one, so that the trains could keep running while the work went on.

The bridge now belongs to Amtrak, and since 2007, it's been an official Chicago Landmark. It's one of the last reminders of the railroad empire that once covered this side of the river, including the yards where you're standing. When it rises, you're watching a machine more than a century old do exactly what it was built to do.
