=== STOP The Auditorium Building
=== DEEPER An Opera House for Everyone | history
Ferdinand Peck was a rich man with an unusual idea about music. The son of one of Chicago's pioneer merchants, he was a lawyer and a tireless civic booster, and he believed that a great city needed great music, and that it shouldn't belong only to people like him.

In the 1880s, grand opera in America meant boxes. At the Metropolitan Opera House, which opened in New York in 1883, the best places in the hall were rings of private boxes, rented by the richest families in the city, who came as much to be seen as to listen. Peck wanted the opposite: a hall where a clerk with a single ticket could see and hear as well as a millionaire.

He tried the idea out first. In 1885, he organized an opera festival in the huge exhibition hall that then stood on the lakefront, where the Art Institute is today, and Adler and Sullivan built a temporary theater inside it for crowds of several thousand. The crowds came, and the sound was superb. Peck had found his architects.

In December 1886, he incorporated the Chicago Auditorium Association and raised the money from the city's business leaders. He wanted no boxes at all. His investors insisted on some, so in the end there were a few dozen, pushed off to the sides, holding only a small share of the audience. The rest of the house was a great sweep of individual seats, more of them than the Metropolitan had.

Opening night was December ninth, 1889. Crowds lined the streets to watch the guests arrive. The president and the vice president were in the audience, and the star of the evening was Adelina Patti, the most celebrated soprano of her day, who sang Home, Sweet Home.

For the next forty years, this was the musical heart of the city. The orchestra that became the Chicago Symphony, founded in 1891 by the conductor Theodore Thomas, played here until it moved into its own hall up the avenue in 1904, and Chicago's opera companies made this stage their home until 1929, when they left for a new opera house on the river.
=== DEEPER Floating on Clay | underfoot
Under the sidewalk here, there's no solid rock for a long way down. What there is, is clay: firm near the top, soft and wet below. In the 1880s, Chicago's builders didn't try to reach bedrock. They spread their buildings out on top of the clay, the way snowshoes spread your weight on snow, and trusted it to hold.

The Auditorium was the biggest test of that idea anyone had tried. Dankmar Adler and the engineer Paul Mueller designed what's called a raft, a floating foundation of crisscrossed timbers and railroad beams, encased in concrete. On top of it they stacked stone footings in stepped pyramids, to carry the thick outer walls.

Adler tested the ground before he trusted it. He loaded trial footings with heavy bars of pig iron and measured how far they sank. His biggest worry was the tower. It would be much heavier than the rest of the building, and it would go up last, so its footings would sink later and deeper than the walls around them, and the building could crack where the two met. So as the lower floors rose, he loaded the basement under the future tower with pig iron and bricks, about as heavy as the finished tower would be. As the tower went up, workers took the extra weight away, a little at a time, so that the ground beneath had already done most of its settling.

It mostly worked. The heavy outer walls have sunk more than the light interior, so floors inside tip and roll, but the building has stood for well over a century.

The theater was full of machinery too. Under the stage were twenty-six hydraulic lifts that could raise and lower sections of the floor for scenery. In the ceiling, great hinged panels could be lowered to close off the top two galleries, shrinking the hall for a smaller crowd. And in hot weather, fans drew in outside air and pushed it over ice before it flowed into the hall, which made the Auditorium one of the first theaters anywhere that was both heated and cooled.
=== DEEPER The Rabbi's Son | people
Louis Sullivan gets most of the credit for this building, but the man who made it stand up and sound right was his partner, Dankmar Adler.

Adler was born in 1844, in a small town in central Germany. His mother died at his birth, and according to a family story, his father named him Dankmar, joining the German word for thanks to a Hebrew word meaning bitter. His father, Liebman Adler, was a rabbi. He brought the family to America when Dankmar was a boy, first to Detroit and then, in 1861, to Chicago, to lead the city's oldest Jewish congregation.

The son went to war. He served in the Union army with an Illinois artillery battery, and he did engineering and mapping work in the campaigns around Chattanooga and Atlanta. Afterward, he learned the building trade in Chicago drafting rooms, and after the fire of 1871, there was more work than anyone could handle.

What made his name was sound. In 1879, he designed the Central Music Hall, at State and Randolph, and its acoustics were so admired that theater builders across the country began to seek his advice. Even Carnegie Hall in New York, which opened in 1891, consulted him. Around the time of the music hall, he hired a gifted, prickly young draftsman named Louis Sullivan, and within a few years he made him his partner. Adler handled the engineering, the clients, and the money, and Sullivan the design.

If you can get inside the theater, look up. Its great golden arches step outward from the stage, each one wider than the last, like the bell of a horn. They carry the sound to the farthest seats, and they carry the lights as well: art, acoustics, and engineering in a single gesture.

Adler also cared about his profession. When Illinois passed the first law in the country requiring architects to be licensed, in 1897, he served as president of the state board that examined them. He died in 1900, at fifty-five, the half of the partnership that the public forgot and that architects never did.
=== DEEPER All Passes, Art Alone Endures | culture
Before you go, take a closer look at the doorway of the Fine Arts Building, just north of the Auditorium. Carved over it are the words All Passes, Art Alone Endures.

The building went up in 1885 for the Studebaker brothers of South Bend, Indiana, who made wagons and carriages and needed a place in Chicago to assemble and sell them. Their architect was Solon Spencer Beman, who was designing George Pullman's company town at about the same time. In 1898, Beman remodeled it for a new life, taking off the top floor and adding three more, and filling it with studios for artists and musicians, and a theater.

For a few years before the First World War, these halls were the center of literary Chicago. The Dial, a respected literary magazine, had its offices here, and in 1907 its editor, Francis Fisher Browne, opened a bookstore on the seventh floor, designed by Frank Lloyd Wright. In 1912, Maurice Browne and Ellen Van Volkenburg started the Chicago Little Theatre in a tiny hall on the fourth floor, with just ninety-nine seats, staging serious modern plays for small audiences. It's often credited with starting the little theater movement that spread across the country.

In 1914, a young woman named Margaret Anderson started a magazine in room nine seventeen and named it The Little Review, after the theater downstairs. She published the boldest new writers she could find. After the magazine moved to New York, it began printing James Joyce's novel Ulysses, chapter by chapter, and its editors were taken to court for obscenity.

The sculptor Lorado Taft had a studio here, and so did W. W. Denslow, the illustrator who drew the first pictures of Dorothy, the Scarecrow, and the Tin Woodman, for The Wonderful Wizard of Oz in 1900.

Step inside if the building is open, and climb the stairs. The halls are lined with wooden doors and frosted glass, and behind them you may hear violins, pianos, and singers practicing, much as you would have a century ago.

=== DEEPER Harold | people
Harold Washington was born on the South Side in 1922, and politics ran in the family. His father, Roy, was a lawyer and a Methodist minister, and one of the first Black precinct captains for the Democratic Party, at a time when most Black Chicagoans still voted Republican, for the party of Lincoln.

In the Second World War, Harold served in a segregated engineering unit of the Army Air Forces, building airfields in the Pacific, and he came home a first sergeant. Then he enrolled at a brand-new school, Roosevelt College, which soon moved into the Auditorium Building, a couple of blocks east of here.

Roosevelt had a remarkable beginning. In 1945, the president of a downtown college was told by his board to count his students by race and religion. He refused, telling them, the story goes, that we don't count that way, and he was forced out. Many of the faculty and students followed him to start a new college, open to everyone, named for Franklin and Eleanor Roosevelt. Harold Washington was elected to lead its student council, and after graduating in 1949, he went on to law school at Northwestern.

When his father died, in 1953, Harold took over his precinct, in the Third Ward organization of Ralph Metcalfe, the Olympic sprinter who had won a gold medal running on the same relay team as Jesse Owens, in Berlin. For years, Washington was a loyal soldier of the machine. He went to the state legislature in Springfield in 1965, and he stayed for sixteen years.

There were stumbles. In the early 1970s, he was convicted of failing to file his income tax returns for several years, and he spent about a month in jail. But he was also growing more independent of the machine, and in 1977, after the death of the first Mayor Daley, he ran in the special election for mayor and finished far behind. In 1980, he was elected to Congress.

Two years later, when Black Chicagoans began organizing to elect a mayor of their own, he was the man they wanted.
=== DEEPER We Shall See in Eighty-Three | politics
Harold Washington didn't simply decide to run for mayor. He was drafted.

By 1982, many Black Chicagoans felt betrayed by Mayor Jane Byrne. They had helped elect her in 1979, and they believed she had then sided with the old machine against them. That summer, Jesse Jackson's Operation Push led a boycott of ChicagoFest, the mayor's big music festival on Navy Pier. A journalist and radio commentator named Lu Palmer had already been calling for a Black candidate, with a slogan that spread all over the South Side: We shall see in eighty-three.

The candidate they wanted was the congressman from the South Side, and he set conditions. He would run only if the movement put tens of thousands of new voters on the rolls and raised real money first. So churches, community groups, and Black radio stations launched a registration drive aimed at the deadline for the fall election, with a rallying cry: Come Alive October Five. Volunteers signed people up wherever they gathered, in churches, on street corners, and at bus stops, and the rolls swelled.

On November tenth, 1982, he announced his campaign. His campaign, he told supporters, was about the plan, not the man.

After his upset in the primary, the general election, against the Republican Bernard Epton, turned ugly. Epton's slogan was Epton, Before It's Too Late, and everyone understood what it meant. On Palm Sunday, Washington and the former vice president Walter Mondale visited Saint Pascal's Church, on the Northwest Side. They were met by a jeering crowd and had to be escorted out by the police, and the scene went out on television across the country.

On election day, April twelfth, 1983, nearly one point three million people voted. Washington won by about forty-eight thousand.
=== DEEPER Council Wars | politics
Winning the election turned out to be the easy part.

At the new mayor's first city council meeting, in May 1983, twenty-nine of the fifty aldermen, nearly all of them white, took control of the council and its committees. Their leader was Edward Vrdolyak, the chairman of the Cook County Democratic Party, a sharp-tongued lawyer from the Southeast Side. Washington had twenty-one votes.

For three years, the two sides fought over almost everything. The twenty-nine blocked the mayor's appointments to city boards and rewrote his budgets. Washington vetoed their measures, and they never had the votes to override him. Reporters started calling the city Beirut on the Lake, after the war-torn capital of Lebanon, and the fight got the name it still has, a play on the Star Wars movies: Council Wars.

The way out came through the courts. Black and Latino voters had sued over the city's ward map, arguing that it had been drawn to weaken their votes, and a federal court agreed and ordered new boundaries. In 1986, special elections were held in seven redrawn wards, all of them represented by members of the opposing bloc. Washington's candidates won four. The last two were decided in runoffs that spring, and one of the winners was Luis Gutiérrez, a young Puerto Rican activist who later served many years in Congress. Now the council was split twenty-five to twenty-five, and the mayor cast the deciding vote.

In 1987, Washington won a second term, beating Jane Byrne in the primary and Vrdolyak, running on a third-party ticket, in the general election. Then, just months after his second inauguration, he was gone.

The council chose his successor in an all-night meeting, while crowds outside chanted for Timothy Evans, the alderman most of Washington's followers wanted. At about four in the morning on December second, 1987, it elected Eugene Sawyer instead, a quiet Black alderman, with most of his votes coming from the old opposition bloc. Washington's coalition split apart, and in 1989, Richard M. Daley was elected mayor, the office his father had held for twenty-one years.
=== DEEPER The Library Chicago Waited For | look
For fourteen years before this building opened, Chicago had no proper main library. When the old one on Michigan Avenue was turned into the Cultural Center, in 1977, the central library moved into rented floors of an office building at four twenty-five North Michigan Avenue, and most of its books went into storage. A plan to put the library into an empty department store on State Street was drawn up and dropped.

In July 1987, a few months before he died, Mayor Washington and the city council approved a design competition and the money to build. Five teams of architects and builders made the final round, a citizens' jury chose the winner in June 1988, and the library opened in October 1991, named for the mayor who had set it in motion.

Now take a closer look at the walls. The ornament on this building was meant to be read. Look for the round medallions with the face of Ceres, the Roman goddess of grain and harvests, framed by ears of corn, for the farm country that made Chicago rich. Look, too, for the faces of the cold north wind, figures the designers called Windy City men. Much of the sculpture, including the owls on the roof, was the work of Raymond Kaskey, who also made Portlandia, the huge hammered copper goddess who kneels over the entrance of a city office building in Portland, Oregon.

Inside, in a niche off the main lobby, is a mosaic by the painter Jacob Lawrence called Events in the Life of Harold Washington, in tiles of blue, yellow, and green, ringed with words from the mayor's first inaugural address.

And on the ninth floor, the library's special collections keep Harold Washington's own papers: his speeches and the records of his years in office. It's a fitting place for them, in the building he set in motion, a few blocks from the college where his political life began.
=== STOP Printers Row & Dearborn Station
=== DEEPER Changing Trains | history
Here's the strange thing about the railroad capital of America: almost no train went through it. Railroads from the East ended in Chicago, and railroads to the West began here, each at its own station. A traveler going from New York to San Francisco had to get off one train, cross downtown, and board another.

There were six big passenger stations around the Loop: Union Station, the North Western Station, LaSalle Street, Grand Central, Central Station down by the lake, and Dearborn, right here. If you came in from New York on the Twentieth Century Limited, you arrived at LaSalle Street. If you were going on to Los Angeles on the Santa Fe, you left from here.

For more than a century, the business of getting people and their trunks from one station to the next belonged largely to one company, Parmelee. Franklin Parmelee started it in the 1850s with horse-drawn omnibuses, and for generations, many railroads sold their through passengers a Parmelee transfer coupon along with the ticket. Its coaches, and later its buses, were as much a part of the Chicago scene as the trains themselves.

Travelers grumbled, and just after the Second World War, a railroad executive named Robert Young turned their grumbling into full-page newspaper ads. A hog, they said, can cross the country without changing trains, but you can't. It was true. Freight cars were handed from one railroad to the next all the time, while people had to haul their luggage across town. The ad embarrassed the railroads into running a few sleeping cars that were switched from one company's train to another's, so that a passenger could stay in bed from coast to coast.

But most passengers kept changing stations to the end. In 1971, Amtrak took over nearly all the country's passenger trains and gathered Chicago's long-distance routes into Union Station. Dearborn saw its last long-distance trains that spring, and Central Station closed the next year. Within a few years, Central Station and Grand Central, which had closed even earlier, had both been torn down. Dearborn was luckier. Its head house survived.
=== DEEPER The Train of the Stars | culture
Of all the trains that left from this station, the most glamorous was the Santa Fe's Super Chief.

It started running in 1936, a train made up entirely of sleeping cars, pulled by the railroad's new diesel engines, and it cut the trip from Chicago to Los Angeles to under forty hours. It cost extra to ride, and the people who could afford it were the people of Hollywood. Movie stars traveled east and west on it so often that it became known as the Train of the Stars, and photographers waited at the stations to catch them stepping down.

Stars coming from New York usually arrived on the Twentieth Century Limited at LaSalle Street Station, a few blocks north, and crossed the Loop to board the Super Chief here, the eastern train behind them and the western one ahead.

The train's look was part of its fame. Its diesel engines soon wore the Santa Fe's red and silver warbonnet colors, and the cars inside were decorated with designs drawn from the art of the Southwest. The food came from the Fred Harvey company, which ran the dining cars on the Santa Fe and the restaurants in its stations, famous for their waitresses, the Harvey Girls. In 1951, the train added the Turquoise Room, a small private dining room in the lounge car that passengers could reserve for a party of up to twelve. The railroad advertised it as the only private dining room in the world on rails.

Airplanes ended its reign. By the 1960s, the stars flew. Amtrak kept the train running after 1971, from Union Station, and its descendant, the Southwest Chief, still leaves Chicago every afternoon for Los Angeles, across Kansas, Colorado, New Mexico, and Arizona, much as the Super Chief did.
=== DEEPER Ink on Dearborn Street | companies
Two of the great names in American printing grew up in Chicago, and both started small.

Richard Robert Donnelley came to Chicago from Hamilton, Ontario, in 1864, a young printer with large ambitions. The Great Fire of 1871 wiped out his business, and he started over. Under his son, Thomas, the company grew into one of the biggest printers in the country, and its name became a byword for quality.

Thomas Donnelley believed printing could be an art as well as an industry. In the late 1920s, the company set out to show what American printing could do, with a project it called the Four American Books: Thoreau's Walden, Dana's Two Years Before the Mast, the tales of Edgar Allan Poe, and Herman Melville's Moby Dick. The Moby Dick became the most famous. The Lakeside Press published it in 1930, in three volumes and only a thousand copies, with hundreds of haunting black and white drawings by the artist Rockwell Kent. It's often called one of the great American illustrated books, and collectors still prize it.

Rand McNally began in Chicago too. In 1856, a printer named William Rand opened a shop in the city, and a couple of years later a young Irish immigrant, Andrew McNally, came to work for him. For a time, the two of them ran the printing plant of the Chicago Tribune, but their future lay with the railroads that met in Chicago. They did printing jobs for the lines and published a guide to their timetables, and in December 1872, the guide carried their first map. Railroad maps led to atlases, and when Americans took to the road in automobiles, Rand McNally sold them road maps by the millions. For much of the twentieth century, if you unfolded a map on a family road trip, there was a good chance it came from a Chicago company.

Why here? Because the city sat at the center of everything a printer needed: paper arriving by rail, the biggest customers in the country in the railroads and the mail-order houses, and trains that could carry the finished work anywhere in America within days. For the better part of a century, the clatter of presses was the sound of these streets, and the smell of ink hung over them day and night.
=== STOP Clarke House & Glessner House
=== DEEPER The Last Richardson in Chicago | architecture
Henry Hobson Richardson was the most admired architect in America in the 1880s, and this house is the only building of his still standing in Chicago.

He was born in Louisiana in 1838, studied at Harvard, and then went to Paris, to the École des Beaux-Arts, only the second American to study architecture there. His breakthrough was Trinity Church in Boston, finished in 1877, a great mass of stone with round arches and a heavy central tower. The style he developed, rough stone, deep round arches, and a feeling of enormous weight, came to be called Richardsonian Romanesque, and for a generation it spread across the country, to courthouses, libraries, train stations, and city halls.

His greatest Chicago building was the Marshall Field Wholesale Store, a block-long warehouse in the Loop, as plain and powerful as a Roman aqueduct, long since torn down. Louis Sullivan admired it enormously, and his design for the Auditorium, where this tour began, grew plainer and heavier after the wholesale store went up. In the Auditorium's great stone walls, you can still see Richardson's influence.

Richardson worked at a furious pace in his last years, even as his health failed. He was a huge man, and he suffered from a kidney disease that he knew was killing him. He kept working anyway, and the Glessner House was among the last designs he completed. His successors carried out his drawings after his death.

Now look closely at the walls. The granite blocks are cut rough on their faces but laid with great precision, in bands of different heights. The deep arched entrance seems carved out of solid rock. The windows are few and deliberate. There's almost no ornament at all, and yet the house has more presence than any of the fancier mansions that once surrounded it. That was Richardson's gift: making a building seem as if it had always been there, and always would be.
=== DEEPER The Girl Who Wasn't Sent to College | people
Frances Glessner was nine years old when her family moved into this house, and she grew up inside its walls, taught by private tutors. Her brother, George, went to Harvard. Frances wanted to go to college too, and to study medicine, but her parents believed that college was no place for a girl.

In 1898, she married a lawyer named Blewett Lee, and they had three children, but the marriage failed, and they divorced in 1914.

The turn in her life came through her brother's Harvard classmate, George Burgess Magrath, who became the medical examiner in Boston. Over many conversations, he told her how often deaths were misjudged, by coroners who were elected and had no medical training, and by investigators who didn't know what to look for. She was fascinated, and when she came into the family fortune, she put it to work. In the early 1930s, she endowed a department of legal medicine at Harvard, the first of its kind in the country, and gave it a library.

She did much more than write checks. Starting in 1945, she ran week-long seminars in homicide investigation for experienced police detectives, and she planned every detail herself, down to the grand dinner at the Ritz-Carlton hotel in Boston that ended each week. In the 1940s, the New Hampshire State Police, near the family's summer estate, made her an honorary captain, and she's often described as the first woman police captain in the country.

She died in 1962. Her tiny death scenes are kept today at the medical examiner's office in Baltimore, and in 2017 and 2018 the Smithsonian displayed them in Washington, where crowds lined up to study them, trying to solve the cases. The answers have never been published. They're still used to train investigators, and the people who know the solutions keep them to themselves.
=== DEEPER What's Left of Millionaires' Row | look
Look across Prairie Avenue from the Glessner House, and you'll see what the street's other residents thought a mansion should look like.

Directly across the avenue, on the southeast corner, is the Kimball House, finished in 1892 for William Wallace Kimball, whose company was one of the biggest makers of pianos and organs in America. It's a French chateau in gray stone, with turrets and a steep slate roof, designed by Solon Spencer Beman, the architect of Pullman's company town. It's everything the Glessner House isn't: open to the street, proud, and a little showy. Next door to it stands another survivor, the Coleman House, from the 1880s. In 1991, these two mansions became the headquarters of the United States Soccer Federation, and in 2023 they were sold, to be turned back into homes.

Walk south toward 19th Street. On the west side is the Keith House, from about 1870, one of the oldest houses on the avenue. A little farther, at nineteen nineteen, is the house of Marshall Field Junior, the merchant's son, where he was fatally shot in 1905, in circumstances you'll hear more about at the end of this tour. It has been restored and divided into homes.

These few houses are almost all that's left. In the 1880s, mansions lined the avenue for blocks, built by the men who ran the city's railroads, banks, and stores. Within a generation, most of their families had moved to the North Side, and over the following decades, nearly all of the houses were torn down for warehouses, printing plants, and parking lots.

Stand here for a moment and try to fill in the gaps: carriages at the curb, lawns behind iron fences, and some of the richest people in America living side by side, a few blocks from the railroad yards that made their fortunes and would eventually drive them away.
=== DEEPER The House That Kept Moving | history
The Clarke House has outlived almost everything around it, partly by moving out of the way.

Henry Clarke and his wife, Caroline, came to Chicago from the East in 1835, when the town had only a few thousand people. They built far out on the prairie, in the Greek Revival style that was fashionable back East, with a columned portico like the front of a Greek temple. Its frame is made of heavy timbers, joined in the old way, and it was solid enough to survive two long journeys across the city.

In 1872, the year after the Great Fire, a tailor named John Chrimes bought the house and had it moved about four miles south, to Wabash Avenue at 45th Street. According to the city's history of the house, he feared another fire, and one of his children was sick and needed country air. The columned portico came off for the move. The family lived there for decades, and later the house became the rectory of Saint Paul Church of God in Christ.

By the 1970s, it was recognized as the oldest house in Chicago, and the city decided to bring it home. The move, in December 1977, was harder than anyone had planned. To get the house over the elevated train tracks, the movers had to jack it high in the air, and then a hard freeze set in. The hydraulic equipment froze, and for about two weeks the oldest house in Chicago sat stranded beside the tracks, waiting for a thaw.

It finally came to rest here, near its original site, and was restored as a museum, furnished as a prosperous family's home might have been in the 1850s and 1860s, when Chicago was turning from a frontier town into a metropolis.

It's worth going inside if it's open. The town of Chicago didn't become a city until 1837, so when you stand in these rooms, you're standing in a house that's older than the City of Chicago itself.
=== STOP Pullman's Mansion & the Battle of Fort Dearborn
=== DEEPER The Fifteenth of August | history
In the summer of 1812, Fort Dearborn was a lonely post at the far edge of American territory. When war broke out with Britain that June, the British and their Native allies quickly captured the American fort on Mackinac Island, at the top of Lake Michigan, and General William Hull ordered Fort Dearborn abandoned.

The commander, Captain Nathan Heald, met with the Potawatomi leaders gathered around the fort and promised to hand over the fort's goods if they let the garrison leave in peace. Then, afraid of what the goods might be used for, he had the extra guns and ammunition and the barrels of whiskey destroyed, dumped in the river and down a well. To the Potawatomi, it looked like a promise broken.

On the morning of August fifteenth, the column marched out of the gate and turned south along the beach: about fifty-five soldiers, a dozen militiamen, nine women, and eighteen children, with a small escort of Miami warriors led by the scout William Wells.

About a mile and a half down the shore, where a ridge of sand dunes ran beside the beach, between four and five hundred Potawatomi warriors attacked. The fight lasted perhaps a quarter of an hour. The Miami escort rode away. Twenty-six soldiers were killed, along with all twelve militiamen, two women, and twelve children, who had been riding in a wagon. Heald, wounded, surrendered the survivors.

The captives were taken to villages across the region. Some died, some were ransomed by traders over the following months, and some were held for a year or more. The day after the battle, the warriors burned the fort.

For the Potawatomi, it was a victory in a war they hoped would halt the American advance into their country. That hope died two years later, when Britain made peace with the United States and left its Native allies to face the Americans alone.
=== DEEPER The Chief and the Peace Medal | people
The figure in Pullman's sculpture is Black Partridge, a Potawatomi chief whose village stood on the Illinois River, near what's now Peoria.

He had been a friend of the Americans. He wore a silver peace medal they had given him, as a token of that friendship. In the days before the evacuation, according to the accounts of survivors, he came to the fort and handed the medal back to Captain Heald. His young men were determined to fight, he said, he could not restrain them, and he would not wear a sign of peace while he was forced to act as an enemy.

During the battle, the story goes on, he saved Margaret Helm, a young officer's wife and the stepdaughter of the trader John Kinzie, by pulling her into the lake and holding her there, out of reach, until the fighting stopped. That's the scene the sculpture shows. It comes from the Kinzie family's account, and like much of that account, it can't be checked against any other source.

What happened next is rarely told. In October 1812, Illinois militia marched north to punish the Potawatomi villages around Peoria Lake. Black Partridge came home to find his village burned and, by one account, his daughter and grandchild dead. The chief who had saved American lives then took up arms against the Americans himself, and he joined an attack on the new American fort at Peoria, which failed. In 1815, with the war over, he signed a treaty of peace.

His name survives in Black Partridge Woods, a forest preserve southwest of the city, on the bluffs above the Des Plaines River, where spring-fed streams run through wooded ravines, on land his people once called home. And the Potawatomi remember the battle not as a massacre but as a fight for their homeland, one they were about to lose.
=== DEEPER The Treaty and the Last Dance | politics
Twenty-one years after the battle, the Potawatomi left Chicago for good, and they left by treaty.

In September 1833, thousands of Potawatomi, Ottawa, and Ojibwe people gathered around the little town for a treaty council with American commissioners. For weeks, their camps spread across the prairie, and traders, land speculators, and whiskey sellers crowded in to profit. The treaty they signed on September twenty-sixth gave up the last of their lands in northern Illinois and southern Wisconsin, about five million acres, in exchange for land west of the Mississippi and payments of money and goods. Part of the money went to pay debts that traders claimed the tribes owed them.

Two years later, in August 1835, before they departed, some eight hundred warriors gathered for a last war dance. Painted and carrying their weapons, they danced through the streets of the town, past the houses and stores of the people who had taken their land, while the settlers watched, uneasy. A young lawyer named John Dean Caton, who later became chief justice of the Illinois Supreme Court, saw it and wrote about it years afterward. Then they were gone, west to new lands in Missouri and Iowa, and later to Kansas.

Not everyone went. Leopold Pokagon, a Potawatomi leader in southwestern Michigan who had become a Catholic, won the right for his band to stay. Sixty years later, at the World's Fair of 1893, his son Simon Pokagon spoke to an enormous crowd on Chicago Day, about the city that had been built on his people's land.

The Potawatomi nations live on today, in Kansas, Oklahoma, Wisconsin, Michigan, and Indiana. And Chicago is home to one of the largest urban Native American communities in the country, much of it brought here in the 1950s and 1960s by a federal program that encouraged families to move from reservations to cities.

The murals in this church were painted by one of its own members, Frederic Clay Bartlett, who grew up nearby, the son of a wealthy hardware merchant. His father's firm, Hibbard, Spencer, Bartlett, was one of the biggest hardware wholesalers in the country, and the brand name it created, True Value, still hangs over hardware stores across America.

Frederic studied painting in Germany and France and came home to work in the Arts and Crafts spirit, as much a decorator as a painter. He was still in his twenties when fire gutted the church in 1900, and its rebuilding gave him the chance to paint on a grand scale. If you're inside, notice how the painting, the woodwork, and the glass all seem to belong together. That was the point. In the Arts and Crafts way of thinking, a room was a single work of art.

He had a second career as a collector. With his second wife, Helen Birch, he bought modern French paintings at a time when few Americans would: Cézanne, Van Gogh, Gauguin, Toulouse-Lautrec, and the young Picasso. In 1924, they bought Georges Seurat's A Sunday on La Grande Jatte. Helen died the next year, and in her memory, Frederic gave their collection to the Art Institute. That's how one of the most famous paintings in the world came to Chicago, from a family that worshipped in this church.

Later in life, he married again and spent his winters in Fort Lauderdale, Florida, at a house called Bonnet House, which he painted and decorated with the same playful hand. It's a museum now, too.

So if you ever stand in front of La Grande Jatte, with its crowds of tiny dots and its Sunday strollers by the river, remember the man who brought it to Chicago, and where he first learned to paint on a grand scale: on these walls, among the angels.
=== DEEPER The Machine and the Craftsman | culture
The Arts and Crafts movement came to Chicago in a rush around 1900, and it took a very Chicago turn.

In 1897, a group of artists, architects, and reformers founded the Chicago Arts and Crafts Society at Hull House, Jane Addams's settlement house on the Near West Side. They believed that good design and honest handwork could improve the lives of the people who made things, as well as the people who bought them. But unlike the English reformers who inspired them, they were willing to make peace with the machine.

Workshops sprang up all over the city. The Kalo Shop, founded in 1900 by a woman named Clara Barck Welles, made hand-hammered silver, trained a generation of silversmiths, and kept working until 1970. Northwest of the city, a terra cotta company began making Teco pottery, in soft, matte greens, with designs by some of Chicago's leading architects. Bookbinders, weavers, and furniture makers set up shop in studios downtown.

Then, in March 1901, a young architect from Oak Park stood up at Hull House and made the case for the machine more boldly than anyone. In a lecture called The Art and Craft of the Machine, Frank Lloyd Wright argued that the machine wasn't the enemy of beauty at all. In the hands of an artist, it could make simple, beautiful things for everyone, not only for the few who could afford handmade ones.

That argument, between the handmade and the machine made, runs through everything you've seen on this tour. This church's interior is the handmade ideal, every surface designed and crafted by hand. A few blocks north, the presses of Printers Row turned out books and catalogs by the million. And on Prairie Avenue, the richest families in Chicago filled their houses with handcrafted furniture paid for by reapers, sleeping cars, and canned meat, made by machines.

Chicago, being Chicago, wanted both.
=== DEEPER Motor Row | companies
The automobile came to Chicago early. On Thanksgiving Day, 1895, the Chicago Times-Herald newspaper sponsored one of the first automobile races in America, from Jackson Park to Evanston and back, about fifty-four miles, through snow. Only two cars finished. The winner, Frank Duryea, took more than ten hours.

By the 1910s, this stretch of Michigan Avenue had become Motor Row, a long line of showrooms, garages, and parts dealers, selling dozens of makes, most of them long forgotten.

Why here? Partly because of the Coliseum, a huge exhibition hall a few blocks northwest, at Wabash Avenue and 15th Street, where the Chicago Auto Show began in 1901. Buyers who had seen the new models at the show could walk over and order one. The Coliseum had other uses too. Five Republican national conventions met there, including the one in 1912, when Theodore Roosevelt's supporters walked out. That August, they came back to the same hall to launch their own party, the Progressives, nicknamed the Bull Moose.

The showroom buildings were designed to sell. Their tall ground floors put the newest models on display to everyone passing by, and inside, freight elevators big enough to carry automobiles lifted them to the floors above, where they were stored and repaired.

Motor Row faded as the dealers followed their customers out to the neighborhoods and the suburbs, and many of its buildings became warehouses. The city made it a landmark district in 2000, and today its old showrooms hold restaurants, offices, and event halls. The auto show goes on, though. It's held every winter at McCormick Place, a short walk southeast of here, and it's one of the biggest in the country.

=== DEEPER The Poet of the Blues | people
Willie Dixon was born in Vicksburg, Mississippi, in 1915. As a boy he wrote poems, and he heard the blues everywhere around him, in juke joints, in work camps, and, after he was picked up for hoboing as a teenager, on a county prison farm.

He came to Chicago in 1936, a big, powerful young man, and his first career was boxing. In 1937, he won the Illinois Golden Gloves title as a heavyweight novice. Then music took over. He learned the bass and sang with vocal groups around the city. In 1941, when the draft came for him, he refused to serve, saying he would not fight for a country that treated Black people the way his did, and he spent months in jail for it. After the war, he formed a group called the Big Three Trio.

Before long, he went to work for the Chess brothers as a bass player and became much more: producer, arranger, talent scout, and above all songwriter. He wrote Hoochie Coochie Man and I Just Want to Make Love to You for Muddy Waters, My Babe for Little Walter, and Spoonful, Little Red Rooster, and Back Door Man for Howlin' Wolf. Wang Dang Doodle became a hit for Koko Taylor. People called him the poet laureate of the blues.

His songs traveled around the world, recorded by the Rolling Stones, Cream, the Doors, and Led Zeppelin. But for years Dixon saw little of the money, because like many blues writers, he had signed away the publishing rights to his songs. He spent much of his later life fighting to get them back. He sued Led Zeppelin, arguing that their song Whole Lotta Love took its words from his song You Need Love, which Muddy Waters had recorded in 1962, and the case was settled out of court in 1987.

He started the Blues Heaven Foundation in 1982, to help other musicians collect what they were owed. He died in 1992, and afterward his widow, Marie, moved the foundation into this building, the place where so many of his songs had been recorded.
=== DEEPER Was Muddy Painting the Ceiling? | legends
In June 1964, five young Englishmen on their first American tour walked through this door to record. For the Rolling Stones, it was a pilgrimage. Mick Jagger and Keith Richards had renewed their boyhood friendship on a train platform in England, when Keith noticed that Mick was carrying a couple of Chess records. Much of the music they loved had been recorded in this building and in the label's earlier studios.

In two days of sessions, they cut more than a dozen tracks, including a song called It's All Over Now, which became their first number one hit in Britain.

Then there's the story. Keith Richards has told it many times. When the band walked in, he said, there was Muddy Waters, the king of Chicago blues, up a ladder, painting the ceiling, because his records weren't selling and the Chess brothers had put him to work around the building. It's a story about the way America treated its own great musicians, while young white Englishmen got rich playing their songs.

But almost nobody else who was there remembers it that way. Marshall Chess, Leonard Chess's son, who was working at the label that summer, has said flatly that there's no truth in it, and he teases Keith about it as one of his fantasies. The band's bass player, Bill Wyman, remembered something smaller: Muddy Waters helping to carry their amplifiers in from the car.

So it's a legend. Maybe it grew out of that small kindness, or a workman on a ladder, or a memory blurred by time and by everything that came after. What's documented is the music, and what happened next. Young white fans who loved the Rolling Stones went looking for the records of the men the Stones had learned from, and in the late 1960s, Muddy Waters, Howlin' Wolf, and other Chess artists found new audiences on college campuses and at rock festivals, far from the South Side bars where their music had been born.
=== DEEPER The Brothers from Motal | companies
Leonard and Phil Chess were born in Motal, a small Jewish town in what was then Poland and is now Belarus. Their father emigrated to Chicago first, and the rest of the family followed in 1928, when Leonard was eleven.

The brothers grew up on the South Side and went into the bar business. In the 1940s, Leonard ran a nightclub called the Macomba Lounge, at 39th Street and Cottage Grove Avenue, where he heard the music the Great Migration had brought north. In 1947, he invested in a small record company called Aristocrat, started that spring by Charles and Evelyn Aron, because the musicians in his clubs had nowhere to record. In 1948, it released Muddy Waters' I Can't Be Satisfied, which became Muddy's first hit. By 1950, the brothers had bought out their partners, and they gave the label their own name.

They ran it like a family store. Leonard handled the artists and the recording, and spent weeks on the road, driving through the South with records in the trunk, visiting disc jockeys and record shops. Phil ran the office. Over the years they added other labels, including Checker and Argo, and recorded jazz, gospel, and soul as well as the blues.

Their reputation is mixed. Some of their artists remembered them as loyal friends who lent money and paid hospital bills. Others felt cheated, and argued that the brothers had paid them in cars and cash advances instead of fair royalties. Both things may be true.

In 1963, the brothers bought a radio station and renamed it WVON, the Voice of the Negro. With its disc jockeys, the Good Guys, it became the most important Black radio station in the city, and it carried the news of the civil rights movement as well as the music. Its call letters are still on the air in Chicago today.

In 1969, the brothers sold the company. A few months later, in October, Leonard died of a heart attack, at fifty-two. Phil lived until 2016. The label's catalog has been sold and resold since then, but the records made here have never gone out of print.
=== DEEPER Capone's Corner | history
Look south along Michigan Avenue to 22nd Street, now called Cermak Road. On the corner, for a century, stood a big brick hotel called the Lexington, built in 1892 for visitors to the World's Fair.

In 1928, Al Capone moved his headquarters into the Lexington, taking over a suite of rooms, and he ran his empire from here until his conviction for tax evasion, in 1931.

How Capone got here starts a block west, at twenty-one twenty-six South Wabash, where Big Jim Colosimo ran the most famous restaurant in the Levee. Colosimo had started out, by most accounts, sweeping streets, and he rose to become the boss of the district's brothels, with the protection of the First Ward's aldermen. To help run his businesses, he brought in Johnny Torrio, a hard young man from Brooklyn who was said to be a relative of his wife's.

On May eleventh, 1920, Colosimo was shot dead in the entrance of his café. No one was ever convicted. Torrio, who took over, was widely suspected of arranging it, because Prohibition had begun that January, and Colosimo, it's said, had little interest in the bootlegging business that was about to make his successors rich. Torrio's right-hand man was a young tough he had known back in Brooklyn, Al Capone. In 1925, after Torrio was badly wounded in an ambush, he retired and handed the business to Capone.

The Lexington's strangest moment came on April twenty-first, 1986, when the television reporter Geraldo Rivera hosted a live two-hour special from its basement, promising to open Capone's secret vault. Tens of millions of people watched. The crew broke through the wall and found little more than dirt and an empty bottle or two. The hotel was torn down in the 1990s.

So if you've spent the afternoon walking from the opera house to the blues, end it with this thought: the same few blocks also gave the country its most famous gangster, and one of its most famous empty rooms.
